# Ch458 Multiple Sclerosis and Other Demyelinating Diseases

- **Definition and terminology:**
    - <u>Demyelinating disorders</u> - immune-mediated condition w/ preferential destruction of CNS myelin and sparing of the PNS

## Multiple Sclerosis

- **Definition** - autoimmune disease of the CNS characterised by demyelinating lesions <u>disseminated in space and time</u>, where the course can be relapsing-remitting (RRMS) or progressive (PPMS/ SPMS)
- **Epidemiology of MS:**
    - <u>Prevalence</u> - 2,500,000 individuals affected worldwide
    - <u>Demographic</u>:
        - Age - typical age of onset at 20-40y (although 10% cases present \< 18y)
        - Sex - female preponderance (F:M = 3:1)
- **Physiology of normal nerve conduction** - 'saltatory' conduction:
    - <u>Myelination by oligodendrocytes in CNS</u> - myelin sheath burries voltage-dependent K channels along much of the axon to prevent hyperpolarisation of the axonal membrane (N.B. conduction block occurs during demyelination as impulses cannot traverse the hyperpolarised demyelination segment)
    - <u>Nodes of Ranvier</u> - each node being membrane not covered by node's of Ranvier, enabling depolarisation due to presence of Na channels
    - <u>Saltatory nerve conduction</u> - nerve impulse jumps from one node of Ranvier to the next:
        - Avoids depolarisation of axonal membrane underlying the myelin sheath between nodes
        - Achieves a much faster conduction velocity (~70m/s) than continuous propagation of unmyelinated nerves (~1m/s)

- **Pathophysiology of multiple sclerosis** - sequence of events including 1) chronic inflammation, 2) demyelination, 3) gliosis, and 4) axonal degeneration:
    - **Disruption of BBB** - unlike 'vasculitic' phenomenon, endothelial cells are preserved, but allows infiltration of mononuclear inflammatory cells, resulting in <u>perivenular cuffing</u> of axonal fibres by infiltrates
    - **Demylelination** - hallmark of the pathology, evident at earliest point of injury:
        - <u>Preservation of oligodendrocyte precursors and mature oligodendrocytes</u> - may partially remyelinate the surviving naked axons, producing 'shadow-plaques'
        - <u>Temporary conduction block due to demyelination</u> - exposure of normally burried voltage-dependent K channels results in **hyperpolarisation of the resting axonal membrane**, such that impulses are unable to traverse the demyelinated segment
        - <u>Redistribution of Na channels along the naked axon</u> - redistribution of Na channels from Nodes of Ranvier enables continuous propagation of nerve impulses, albeit an **conduction slowing and incomplete block** occurs, affecting high- but not low-frequency volleys of impulses
        - <u>Heat-dependent conduction block</u> - accounts for clinical variation throughout the day or in association w/ fever or w/ exercise (<u>Uhthoff's phenomenon</u>)
    - **Astrocytic proliferation and gliosis** - lesion evolves and may be evident on MRI
    - **Aconal destruction** - although relatively axon-sparing, partial or total axonal destruction occurs w/ highly inflammatory lesions, and hence <u>MS may be neurodegenerative</u>
- **Risk factors for MS:**
    - **Non-modifiable risk factors:**
        - <u>Genetics and immunology</u> - Caucasians and those w/ +ve FHx, and may related to genes involving T cell, B cell functions: 
        ![](img/risk_of_developing_ms.png)
    - **Modifiable risk factors:**
        - <u>Vitamin D deficiency</u> - may be explained by immunoregulatory effects of vitamin D, where vitamin D deficiency is a/w increased risk of MS, and may also increase disease activity after MS begins
        - <u>EBV exposure after early childhood</u> - based on numerous epidemiological and laboratory studies, although no pathogenic role well-defined
        - <u>Cigarrette smoking</u> - the lung being the critical site of activation of pathogenic T cells responsible for demyelination
        - <u>High dietary sodium</u> - has been linked to activation of autoreactive T cells, and could explain the observed increase in prevalence of MS in recent years
- **Common sites of MS** - theoretically any-part of the CNS:
    - Periventricular
    - Juxtacortical
    - Infratentorial
    - Spinal cord
- **Clinical features of MS** - onset may be abrupt or insiduous, with initial Sx being trivial (0.1% autopsy w/ asymptomatic MS) or severe impacting clinical presentation:
