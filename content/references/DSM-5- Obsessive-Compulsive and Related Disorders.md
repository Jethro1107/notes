# Obsessive-Compulsive and Related Disorders

- **Overview:**
    - Used to be included within Anxiety disorders within DSM-IV?, but refactored into its own section in DSM-V reflecting increasing body of evidence of these disorders' relatedness in terms of diagnostic validators as well as clinical utility in grouping these disorders together
    - Although grouped together, treatment approaches may differ across the disorders
    - There is close relationship between anxiety disorders and some of the obsessive-compulsive and related disorders (primarily OCD) but not others
- **Obsessions** - recurrent and persistent thoughts, urges, or images that are <u>experienced as intrusive and unwanted</u>:
- **Compulsions** - repetitive behaviour or mental acts that an individual feels driven to perform in response to an obsession or according to rules that must be applied rigidly
- **Contents of obsession and compulsion** - varies among individuals but certain symptom dimensions are common in OCD:
    - <u>Cleaning</u> - contamination obsession and cleaning compulsions
    - <u>Symmetry</u> - symmetry obsessions and repeating, ordering, and counting compulsions
    - <u>Forbidden or taboo thoughts</u> - aggressive, sexual, and religious obsessions and related compulsions
    - <u>Harm</u> - fear of harm to oneself or others and related checking compulsions
- **Cognitive component of obsessive-compulsive and related disorders** - insight as specifier:
    - Good or fair insight
    - Poor insight
    - Absent insight/ delusional beliefs (do not attribute as psychotic disorder)

## Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder

- **DSM-V diagnostic criteria of OCD:** 
![](img/dsm-v_obsessive_compulsive_disorder_diagnostic_criteria.png)
- **Dysfunctional beliefs** - altered cognition in individuals w/ OCD precipitating its pathogenesis:
    - Inflated sense of responsibility and tendancy to overestimate threats
    - Perfectionism and intolerance of uncertainty
    - Over-importance of thought and the need to control thoughts
- **Diagnostic features** - presence of obsessions <u>AND</u> compulsions (Criterion A):
    - **Obsessions** - defined as recurrent and persistent thoughts, urges, or images and experienced to be intrusive and unwanted, with attempts to ignore or suppress such thoughts, urges or images, or to neutralize them with some other thought or action:
        - <u>Repeated and persistant</u> - which can be thoughts (e.g. of contamination), images (e.g. of violent or horrific scenes), or urges (e.g. to stab somone)
        - <u>Involuntary</u> - these thoughts are experienced as intrusive and unwanted
        - <u>Distressing</u> - these thoughts cause marked distress or anxiety in most individuals
        - <u>Attempts to ignore or suppress</u> - ignore through avoiding known triggers or use of thought suppression, or to neutralise them with another thought or behaviour (i.e. the compulsion)
    - **Compulsions** - defined as repetitive behaviour or mental acts that the individual feels driven to perform in response to an obsession or according to rules that must be applied rigidly, which is aimed at reducing anxiety or distress, or preventing some dreaded event, but these behaviours are not connected in a realistic way, or deamed to be excessive:
        - <u>Trigger</u> - often triggered by an intrusive, obsessive thought
        - <u>Aimed at relief</u> - to reduce the distress triggered by obsession or to prevent a feared event
        - <u>Lack of connection or clearly excessive</u> - the compulsions lack a connection to the feared event, or are clearly excessive (e.g. washig hours for 10 minutes)
    - **Nature of obsession and compulsions** - beyond the occasional intrusive thought or repetitive behaviour:
        - <u>Time-consuming</u> (Criterion A) - e.g. obsession prevents acting upon more pressing matters, or compulsion takes up significant time (e.g. \> 1h per day)
        - <u>Distressing and functional incapacity</u> (Criterion A) - results in clinically significant distress or results in impairments in social functions
- **Associated features:**
    - **Affective responses:**
        - <u>In response to situations that trigger obsessions</u> - range of affective response (may be deemed abnormal) when confronted with situations that trigger obsessions and compulsions that would not be observed in other people, often **marked anxiety**, ocassionally **disgust** (e.g. aggressive/ sexual urges)
        - <u>In response to compulsions</u> - sense of uneasiness or incompleteness until the ritual is sufficiently complete
    - **Avoidance** - functional limitation as a result of avoidance of people, places or things that might trigger obsession and compulsion
- **Epidemiology:**
    - <u>Prevalence</u> - 1.1% - 1.8%
    - <u>Demographic</u>:
        - <u>Age</u> - peak onset in adolescence (see details below)
        - Sex - slight female preponderance (although M affected earlier)
- **Development and course:**
    - **Onset** - usually onset in adolesence and early adulthood (rarely \> 35y):
        - <u>Nature of onset</u> - onset of symptoms is typically gradual, but
        - <u>Earlier onset in M</u> - 25% onset \< 10y
    - **Clinical course** - typically follows such course and may be lifetime, and often <u>complicated by psychiatric comorbidities</u>:
        - Usually chronic, <u>waxing and waning course</u>
        - Some have an <u>episodic course</u>
        - Minority have a <u>deteriorating course</u>
    - **Remission rates** - generally low (20% if evaluated after 40y), but likely dependent on the age of onset:
        - <u>Onset at adulthood</u> - likely will lead to a lifetime of OCD
        - <u>Onset in children or early adolescence</u> - 40% will experience remission by early adulthood
- **Risk factors:**
    - **Temperamental factors:**
        - Behavioural inhibition
        - Negative emotionality/ affectivity
        - Greater internalizing symptoms
    - **Environmental factors:**
        - <u>Upbringing</u> - physical and sexual abuse, other traumatic events
        - <u>Infectious agents</u> - paediatric autoimmune neuropsychiatric disorders a/w Streptococcal infection (PANDAS)
    - **Genetic and physiological factors:**
        - <u>Genetic factors</u> - 2-3x risk if present in first-degree relatives (10x risk if onset of OCD in relative in childhood or adolesence)
        - <u>Physiological factors</u> - dysfunction of orbitofrontal cortex, anterior cingulate cortex and striatum heavily indicated
- **Functional consequences of OCD** - caused by 1) time spent obsessing and doing compulsions, 2) avoidance, 3) developmental difficulties and difficulty socialising, and 4) effects on relationships:
    - <u>Nature of functional limitations</u> - social and occulational impairment depending on the nature of obsession:
        - Excessive obsessions in general may be time consuming and results in general social and occupational impairment
        - Obsessions of contamination may avoid visits to doctor and hospitals, and may develop dermatological problems (e.g. skin lesions due to excessive washing)
        - Obsessions of harm may interfere with relationships with family and friends, resulting in avoidance of relationships
        - Obsessions of symmetry may result in imposition of rules and prohibitions with family members and can lead to family dysfunction
    - <u>Correlation w/ severity</u> - degree of functional limitation often a/w symptom severity
- **Differential diagnosis of OCD:**
    - <u>Anxiety disorders</u> - content of thought differ between the two disorders, where GAD (or other anxiety disorders) is often a/w worries about real-world concerns (e.g. personal health, finance), while OCD is often a/w worries that are odd, irational or of seemingly maginal nature
    - <u>Major depressive disorder</u> - ruminations during depressive episodes but these depressive ruminations are usually mood-congruent and not necessary experienced as intrusive or distressing, and not linked to compulsions
    - <u>Eating disorders</u> - consider OCD if obsessions and compulsions are not limited to weight and food
    - <u>Tics and sterotyped movement</u> - aim of tics are not to neutralise an obsessions (difficult to differentiate especially 30% of OCD patients have documented tic disorders)
    - <u>Psychotic disorders</u> - both disorders characterised by poor insight and delusional beliefs, and necessary to screen for other features of psychotic disorders
    - <u>Other compulsive-like behaviours</u> - e.g. paraphilia, gambling disorder, substance use, but the distinction is made where compulsions are driven to neutralise an obsession and obtain relief, while compulsive-like behaviours are aimed at deriving pleasure from the activity
    - <u>Other obsessive-compulsive related disorders</u> - dependent on the theme and nature of compulsion
    - <u>Obsessive-compulsive personality disorder</u> - more pervasive where compulsion-like activity is more driven by a pervasive maladaptive pattern of excessive perfectionalis and rigid control rather than an impulsive thought
- **Psychiatric comorbidities of OCD:**
    - <u>Anxiety disorder</u> (76%) - e.g. panic disorder, social anxiety disorder, GAD, specific phobia
    - <u>Mood disorder</u> (63%) - e.g. depressive or bipolar disorders
