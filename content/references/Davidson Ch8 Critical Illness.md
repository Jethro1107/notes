## Physiology of Critical Illness

### Oxygen transport

- **Principles of circulation and cardiopulmonary function** - provision of O2 (and other nutrients), and removal of CO2 (and other metabolic wastes), with matching of specific metabolic requirements of individual tissue:
    - <u>Adequate adequate O2 uptake in the lungs</u> - adequate oxygenation of blood dependent on SpO2 (dependent on ventilation, gas exchange etc.) and haemoglobin concentration
    - <u>Global matching of delivery and consumption</u> - matching of consumption and removal at the whole-body level
    - <u>Regional control of circulation</u> - loco-regional control of blood flow based on the metabolic demand of individual tissue
- **Principle of oxygen transport:**
    - <u>O2 cascade in transport of oxygen in inspired gas to cell</u> - O2 moves down a partial pressure gradient from atmospheric air to mitochondria: 
    ![](img/o2_cascade_from_atmosphere_to_mitochondria.png)
    - <u>Oxygen delivery</u> (DO2) - concept describing the factors involved in delivery of O2 from LV to systemic tissue capillaries:
        - DO2 = CO (flow) x CaO2 (arterial O2 content)
        - CaO2 = SpO2 (oxygen saturation) x Hb (haemoglobin concentration)
    - <u>Uptake of O2 from tissue capillaries into cells</u> - down a partial pressure gradient, regulated by microcirculatory, tissue diffusion and cellular factors thus also influence the O2 status of the cells

### Cardiovascular component of oxygen delivery - flow

DO2 is affected by blood flow generated by cardiac output.

- **Factors influencing cardiac output** - CO = SV (preload, afterload, inotropy) x HR
- **Preload** - i.e. atrial filling pressures, determines end-diastolic ventricular volume (LVEDV):
    - **Principle factors influencing preload** - venous return:
        - Intravascular volume - increased intravascular volume, increased venous return, increased preload
        - Venous tone - increased venous tone, increased venous return, increased preload
        - Intrathoracic pressure - more negative intrathoracic pressure, greater pressure gradient, increased venous return, increased preload
    - **Measurement of preload** - central venous pressure (CVP) monitoring as a surogate: 
    ![](img/cvp_measurements.png)
    - **Principles of Mx of reduced preload** - volume loading with IV fluid is most appropriate:
        - <u>Physiological response to reduced intravascular volume</u> - increased venous tone to offset subsequent fall of preload and SV
        - <u>Gradual fluid administration</u> - slowly normalise CVP and reflex increase in CVP ablates (200-250 mL of NS bolus over few minutes, titrated against BP/P, peripheral circulation, and CVP measurement) 
        ![](img/haemodynamic_response_by_cvp_based_on_volume_status.png)
        - <u>Rapid fluid administration</u> - insufficient time for fall in venous and arteriole tone and results in fluid overload and **acute pulmonary oedema** (APO)
    - **Principles of Mx of excess preload** - e.g. in the context of reduced myocardial contractility or fluid overload:
        - <u>Removal of intravascular volume</u> - e.g. diuretics or haemofiltration
        - <u>Venodilators</u> - morphine, glyceryl trinitrate (TNG)
- **Afterload** - resistance to ventricular outflow:
    - **Principle factors influencing afterload** - high BP (high resistsance produce lower CO at higher perssures for given amount of ventricular work)
- **Myocardial contractility** - SV generated against a given afterload for a particular preload:
    - **Principle factors of reduced inotropy in critically ill patients:**
        - <u>Pre-existing cardiovascular disease</u> - principally ischaemic heart disease
        - <u>Drugs</u> - e.g. beta-blockers, verapamil
        - <u>Disease process</u> - e.g. sepsis (due to low diastolic BP that compromise coronary arterial perfusion)

### Oxygenation component of oxygen delivery - content

DO2 is affected by oxygen content of arterial blood.

- **Oxygen content of arterial blood** (CaO2) - determined by 2 factors:
    - <u>Oxygen carrying capacity</u> - Hb concentration
    - <u>Oxygen saturation of haemoglobin</u> - SpO2
- **Oxyhaemoglobin dissociation curve** - describes relationship between SaO2 and PO2 in blood:
    - **Shape of curve** - sigmoid shape, has 2 implications:
        - <u>Small drop in PO2 below 60 mmHg</u> results in <u>marked drop in SpO2</u>
        - <u>Increase in PO2 above 60 mmHg</u> results in <u>minimal change in CaO2</u>
    - **Shift of curve** - influence uptake and release of O2 molecule in tissues:
        - <u>Rightward shift</u> - SpO2 reduced for any given PO2, such that less O2 will be taken up by lungs, but more will be released into tissue (Bohr Effect, see diagram)
        - <u>Leftward shift</u> - SpO2 increased for any given PO2, more O2 taken up by lungs, but are not released into tissue

    
    ![](img/o2_dissociation_curve.png)
    - **Implications of Mx** - increasing FiO2 until SpO2 \> 90%, because further increasing PO2 does not produce any meaningful increase in SpO2 and thus CaO2 given the same Hb concentration
- **Optimal haemoglobin concentration in critically ill patients:**
    - **Targets** - historically target 10 g/dL, but now depends on population due to new evidence:
        - <u>Otherwise well</u> - 7-9 g/dL (restrictive transfusion strategy)
        - <u>Comorbid</u> (CAD, cardiogenic shock, significant AS, TBI, elderly) - 10 g/dL remains appropriate
    - **Rationale** - demonstrated improved outcomes within the range, likely a balance between maximising CaO2 while avoiding regional microcirculatory problems due to increased viscosity

### Oxygen consumption

- **Global oxygen consumption** (VO2) - sum of O2 consumed by the various organs (250 mL/min for 70kg adults performing daily activities)
- **Principles of O2 consumption:**
    - <u>Varying metabolic demand of tissues</u> - e.g. hepatic venous saturation does not exceed 60%, while renal venous saturation may reach 90%
    - <u>Indirect measurement of O2 consumption</u> - measurement of O2 **not** consumped by tissues by measuring O2 saturation at pulmonary artery (mixed venous O2 saturation, SvO2):
        - Theoretically a flow-weighted average measured in the mixed effluent blood from all tisue, influenced by DO2 and VO2
        - Enables monitoring whether DO2 is adequate to meet overall demands in low flow situation

### Relationship between oxygen consumption and delivery

- **Principle of tissue oxygen extraction ratio** (OER) - proportion of O2 extracted from blood:
    - <u>Oxygen extraction ratio as the capacity to extract oxygen from circulation</u> - 20-25% at rest, but can increase with increased consumption or deminished supply, up to maximum of 60%
    - <u>Limited capacity to extract O2</u> - if consumption still exceeds 60% of given DO2:
        - Results in cellular hypoxia, anaerobic metabolism, lactic acid production
        - Multiorgan failure and increased risk of death
    - <u>Implications to clinical practice</u> - metabolic acidosis, hyperlactinaemia +/- oliguria implicates DO2 is insufficient (rise in O2 consumption indicates repaying of 'oxygen debt')

### Pathophysiology of the inflammatory response

- **Terminology in the inflammatory state:** 
![](img/terminology_of_inflammatory_state.png)
- **Inflammation** - physiological response against a primary insult that results in delivery, sequestration and activation of WBC to the injured site
    - **Physiology of local inflammation** - physiological local inflammation is under delicate balance achieved between pro- and anti-inflammatory mediators
    - **Systemic leakage of cytokines** - occurs when inflammatory response is excessive:
        - **Initiation of haemostasis** - platelet activation and fibrin deposition
        - **Viscious cycle of derranged haemostasis** - leukocyte and endothelial cell activation results in release in tissue factor initiating coagulation pathways (depletion of natural anticoagulants systemically results in DIC, increased thrombin activation, and further endothelial cell dysfunction)
        - **Microvascular occlusions** - results in organ dysfunction (acute lung injury, ATN) and further release of inflamatory mediator
        - **Distributive shock** - endothelial dysfunction resulting in release of NO resulting in vasodilation
        - **Abnormal cellular energetics** - causes mitochondrial dysfunction resulting in impaired oxidative phosphorylation and aerobic energy generation, resulting in impaired O2 utilisation, i.e. cytopathic hypoxia (low OER despite normal or supranormal DO2)

## Presenting Problems and Management of Major Organ Failure

### Circulatory Failure or Shock

- **Definition of shock** - generalised hypoperfusion of tissue, defined by a level of oxygen delivery that fails to meet the metabolic requirements of the tissue (not synonymous with hypotension)
- **Defining features of shock** - enables early identification of shock:
    - Increasing base deficit - ?HAGMA
    - Increased blood lactate
    - Reduced urine output or oliguria
- **Etiology of shock** - classified into low-output shock and high-output shock:
    - **Low stroke volume:**
        - <u>Hypovolaemic shock</u> - any condition provoking major shrinkage of intravascular volume resulting reduction in preload (e.g. haemorrhage, severe burns, salt and water depletion)
        - <u>Cardiogenic shock</u> - severe cardiac impairment (e.g. myocardial infarction, acute MR, SAH resulting in catecholamine-mediated myocardial stunning)
        - <u>Obstructive shock</u> - obstruction of blood flow around circulation (e.g. massive PE, tension pneumothorax, cardiac tamponade)
    - **High stroke volume** - usually caused by vasodilatation:
        - <u>Anaphylactic shock</u> - inappropriate vasodilatation triggered by an allergen results in tissue oedema, depleted intravascular volume, and hypoperfusion
        - <u>Sepsis/ SIRS</u> - systemic inflammatory response resulting in widespread endothelial damage, with vasodilatation, AV shuntng, microvascular occlusion etc.
        - <u>Neurogenic shock</u> - disrupted brainstem and neurogenic vasomotor control resulting in inappropriate bradycardia due to combination of loss of noradrenaline-mediated vasoconstriction and adrenaline-mediated chronotropy
- **Clinical features of shock** - dependent of primary pathophysiology:
    - **Features of low-output shock** - reflects reduced CO and physiological compensation:
        - <u>Features of low-stroke volume</u> - cold peripheries, weak or absent peripheral pulses, weak central pulses
        - <u>Features of physiological compensation</u>:
            - Reflex tachycardia
            - Narrow pulse pressure - i.e. increased diastolic pressure and reduced systolic pressure (e.g. 105/95 mmHg)
    - **Features of high-output shock:**
        - <u>Features of increased stroke volume</u> (due to reduced afterload) - warm peripheries, bounding pulses
        - <u>Features of physiological compensation</u>:
            - Widden pulse pressure
        - <u>Delayed features of distributive shock</u> - results in hypovolaemia due to third spacing, and myocardial depression:
            - Cool peripheries
            - Weak pulses
- **Mx:**
    - **Principle of Mx:**
        - Restore global DO2 - correct hypoxaemia, optimise fluid status, optimise Hb, ensure adequate CO
        - Maintain MAP to ensure adequate perfusion of vital organs - target pressure is patient specific depending on pre-morbid factors (60-90 mmHg)
        - Establish monitoring - invasive measures (e.g. CVP measurements), and document trends in haemodynamics, ABG, H+, base deficits, and lactate
        - Treatment of underlying cause - e.g. cardiac tamponade, pulmonary embolism, sepsis

    
    ![](img/principles_of_management_of_circulatory_collapse_davidson.png)
    - **Optimisation of cardiac functions:**
        - **Optimisation of preload** - fluid resuscitation and fluid challenges:
            - **Fluid challenges** - guided by CVP:
                - <u>CVP \< 6mmHg</u> - 250 mL NS or colloid
                - <u>CVP \> 6mmHg or poor ventricular function suspected</u> - 100 mL bolus to avoid precipitating APO
        - **Optimisation of afterload:**
            - **Pharmacological Mx** - arteriole dilators:
                - <u>Selection</u> - nitrates
                - <u>Limitations</u> - can reduce afterload but at a cost of fall of MAP
            - **Mechanical circulatory support** - e.g. intra-aortic balloon pump:
                - <u>Indications</u> - cardiogenic shock with particular value in treating myocardial ischaemia
                - <u>Rationale</u> - counterpulsation enables 1) reduction of LV afterload, while 2) increase in CO, diastolic pressure, and hence coronary perfusion
        - **Optimisation of myocardial contractility** - vasopressors and inotropes can be applied: 
        ![](img/common_vasoactive_agents.png)
    - **Control of rate and rhythm** - target HR between 90-110 bpm, treat reversible causes of arrhythmias, and restore sinus rhythms:
        - **Treatment of reversible causes of arrhythmias** - e.g. hyper-/ hypo-kalaemia, hyper-/ hypo-magnesemia
        - **Treatment of AF in the critically ill** - IV amiodarone (300mg over 30-60 min, 900 mg over 24h) to control ventricular rate and rhythm control
- **Prognosis** - dependent on presence of significant organ failure:
    - <u>Early shock</u> (precipitating cause reversed before organ failure) - good prognosis
    - <u>Late shock</u> (MOF ensues) - poor prognosis, increases w/ number of organs failed (\> 80% if \> 4 organ systems failed), duration of organ failure, and patients age

### Respiratory Failure and Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome

- **Classification of respiratory failure:**
    - <u>Type 1 respiratory failure</u> - hypoxaemia (PaO2 \< 8kPa \[60 mmHg\] on room air) without hypercapnia
    - <u>Type 2 respiratory failure</u> - hypoxaemia with hypercapnia (PaCO2 \> 6.5 kPa \[49 mmHg\]) due to **alveolar hypoventilation**
- **Etiology of respiratory failure** - may be a primary problem or constitute a secondary complication:
    - **Type 1 respiratory failure** - results from an increase in ventilation-perfusion mismatch and can arise from almost any pulmonary pathology:
        - <u>Pulmonary parenchymal disease</u> - pneumonia, pulmonary oedema, pulmonary fibrosis, lung collapse, aspiration, pulmonary contusion
        - <u>Pulmonary vascular disease</u> - pulmonary embolism
        - <u>Pleural disease</u> - pneumothorax
        - <u>Others</u> - acute pulmonary oedema, ARDS
    - **Type 2 respriratory failure** - results from alveolar hypoventilation:
        - <u>Central depression of respiratory drive</u> - drugs
        - <u>Peripheral neuromuscular disease</u> - e.g. Guillain-Barre syndrome, myasthenia gravis
        - <u>Severe airway obstruction</u> - upper airway obstruction (infection, oedema, foreign body), late severe acute asthma, AECOPD
        - <u>Reduced chest wall movement</u> - flail chest injury, exhaustion (includes all type 1 causes)

  
  ![](img/causes_of_respiratory_failure.png)
- **Assessment of respiratory failure** - clinical examination and Ix to determine most appropriate respiratory support:
    - **Respiratory rate and pattern:**
        - <u>Tachypnoea</u> - first indicator of respiratory illness
        - <u>Inspiratory stridor</u> - typically caused by partial upper airway obstruction
        - <u>Prolonged expiratory efforts</u> - indicates severe bronchospasm
        - <u>Rapid shallow breathing</u> - indicates more severe respiratory failure
    - **Conscious level** - reducing GC or agitation indicates more severe respiratory failure as a result of hypoxaemia, more importantly raises a <u>suspicion of hypercapnia</u>
    - **Pulse oximetry** - measurement of SpO2:
        - <u>Threshold for arterial hypoxaemia</u> - SpO2 \< 94% (recall O2-dissociation curve)
        - <u>Context of measurement</u> - failure to respond to to supplemental O2 esp. high flow (FiO2 \> 60%) is an indicator of severe VP mismatch
    - **Arterial blood gas** - detection of hypoxaemia, hypercapnia and VF ratio:
        - <u>Threshold of hypoxaemia</u> - 8 kPa (60 mmHg)
        - <u>Threshold of hypercapnia</u> - 6.5 kPa (49 mmHg)
        - <u>PF ratio</u> - ratio between PaO2 to fractional inspired oxygen delivered (PaO2/FiO2) as an indicator fo disease
- **Mx:**
    - **Principles of Mx** - aims of respiratory support include:
        - Maintain patency of airway
        - Correct hypoxaemia and hypercapnia
        - Reduce work of breathing
    - **Oxygen therapy** - ensure adequate arterial oxygenation (SpO2 \> 90%):
        - <u>Mode of delivery</u>:
            - Facemask
            - Nasal canulae
        - <u>Indications for mechanical respiratory support</u> - if uncorrected hypoxaemia, or causing progressive hypercapnia (e.g. COPD)
        - <u>Caution in COPD patients</u> - risk of progressive hypercapnia overstated, as in patient who is acutely hypoxaemic, maintenance of cerebral oxygenation to avoid irreversible hypoxic brain injury takes precedance over theoretical risks
    - **Non-invasive respiratory support** - techniques that do not require sedation or airway management:
        - **Classification of non-invasive respiratory support:**
            - Continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) therapy
            - Non-invasive ventilation (NIV) - i.e. CPAP plus additional support
        - **Role:**
            - <u>Correct hypoxaemia and hypercapnia</u> - used to support selected patients with type 1 and type 2 respiratory failure
            - <u>Reduce work of breathing</u> - preserve patient's respiratory muscle activity and reduces complications such as nosocomial infection
        - **Prerequisites:**
            - <u>Adequate consciousness</u> - not indicated for comatosed patients who cannot protect airway from aspiration
            - <u>Copoperative patient</u> - CPAP and NIV is uncomfortable and poorly tolerated
            - <u>Strength to breath and cough effectively</u> - neccessity for CPAP, less important for BiPAP
        - **CPAP therapy:**
            - <u>Principles of CPAP</u> - provision of continuous positive airway pressure throughout breathing cycle (5-10 mmHg):
                - **Recruitment of collapsed alveoli** - enhances VQ matching by recruiting collapsed alveoli in scenario of pulmonary atelectasis
                - **Clearance of alveolar fluid** - effective for treatment of pulmonary oedema and patients w/ consolidation (esp. if immunocomprimised)
            - <u>Mode of delivery</u>:
                - Tight-fitting facial mask
                - High-flow nasal nanulae
                - Castar hood
            - <u>Effectiveness in Tx of respiratory failure</u>:
                - **Type 1 respiratory failure** - extremely effective in correcting hypoxaemia
                - **Type 2 respiratory failure** - can be effective by increasing lung compliance (through increase lung volume and reduced alveolar fluid) and thus reduce work of breathing, but often these patients require NIV or mechanical ventilation
            - <u>Duration of trial</u> - failure to improve within 24-48h or further deterioration of GC and ABG indicates step up for invasive ventilation
        - **Non-invasive ventilation:**
            - <u>Principles of NIV</u>:
                - **Bi-level turbine ventilator** (BiPaP) - air delivered at different pressures based on respiratory cycle:
                    - <u>Phase of higher pressure</u> (15-25 cmH2O) - conincides with inspiration
                    - <u>Phase of lower pressure</u> (4-10 cmH2O) - conincids with expiration
                - **Ventilation can be spontaneous or timed** - systems that synchronise with the patient's effort are better tolerated and more effective
            - <u>Mode of delivery</u>:
                - Nasal cannulae
                - Full facemask
            - <u>Indications for NIV</u> - considered for patients deteriorating into **type 2 respiratory failure** by <u>reducing work of breathing</u>:
                - **AECOPD complicating with type 2 failure** - first line therapy to reduce work of breathing and offload of diaphragm and should be initiated early when severe respiratory acidosis or deterioriating GC
                - **Other causes of type 2 failure** - e.g. exhaustion secondary to pulmonary oedema, pneumonia (no good evidence on effectiveness)
                - **During weaning from invasive ventilation**
    - **Emergency endotracheal intubation and mechanical ventilation:**
        - <u>Indications</u> - based on clinical grounds and shared decision making rather than ABGs: ![](img/indications_for_et_intubation_and_mechanical_ventilation.png) 
        ![](img/common_conditions_requiring_mechanical_ventilation.png)
        - <u>Techniques for endotracheal intubation</u> - see more detailed notes:
            - **Medications** - induction of anaesthesia + muscle relaxant in conscious patients or sedation in obtunded patients
            - **Pre-oxygenation** - pre-oxygenation enables lungs to be 100% filled with oxygen
            - **Apply cricoid pressure** - avoid aspiration
            - **Insertion of ET tube** - best in a critical care environment, with expert assistance, resuscitation facilities and appropriate medications
        - <u>Complications of intubation in the critically ill</u> - hypotension and cardiovascular collapse, particularly if associated CHF:
            - **Cardiac depression following sedation or anaesthesia** - direct CVS effects of anaesthetic agent and loss of sympathetic drive
            - **Increased intrathoracic pressure in PPV** - impaired venous return and hence reduced cardiac output
    - **Mx of ventilated patients:**
        - **Classification of ventilatory support:** 
        ![](img/classification_of_ventilatory_support.png)
        - **Principles of Mx:**
            - <u>Mandatory mode of ventilation</u> - choose between volume controlled modes or pressure controlled modes
            - <u>Initial settings</u> - ventilator parametor settings for patients, with re-assessment to see whether modification is needed
            - <u>Continuous monitoring</u> - reassessment to see whether changing parameters is necessary
            - <u>Weaning</u> - assess whether support can be gradually reduced as underlying condition is resolved: 
            ![](img/evidence_for_mechanical_ventilation_in_ards.png)
        - **Ventilator parameters and initial settings:** 
        ![](img/ventilator_parameter_settings.png)
        - **Mandatory modes of ventilation:**
            - **Volume-controlled modes** - set to deliver preset tidal volume at set frequence to guarantee specified minute ventilation:
                - <u>Examples</u> - Synchronised intermittent mandatory ventilation (SIMV) as the safest option
                - <u>Complications</u>:
                    - Pneumothorax from lung barotrauma (pressure damage) or volutrauma (overstretch) - set volume delivered may occur at high pressures if lung compliance is low and lung volume is low
            - **Pressure-controlled modes** - set to deliver set pressure for a specific duration:
                - <u>Examples</u> - pressure-controlled ventilation (PCV), bi-level positive airway pressure ventilation (BiPAP)
                - <u>Complications</u> - minute ventilation depends on lung compliance:
                    - Insufficient ventilations - in stiff lungs (low pulmonary compliance), only small TV can be generated at given pressure
                    - Excess tidal volume - occurs in excessively high lung compliance
                - <u>Routine monitoring</u> - blood gases need to be routinely assessed to identify changes in pulmonary compliance
            - **Weaning or spontaneous breathing modes** - assisted breathing with additional pressure by flow trigger to augment each breathing effort:
                - <u>Selection</u> - pressure support ventilation (PSV) or assisted spontaneous breathing (ASB)
            - **Mixed modes** (mixing complete support with partial support) - e.g. specify frequency of SIMV or PCV and provide PSV for additional breathing efforts
        - **Additional ventilation strategies:**
            - Prone ventilation
            - High-frequency oscillatory ventilation (HFOV)
            - Nitric oxide
            - Extracorporeal membrane oxygenation therapy (ECMO)
            - Corticosteroids (conflicting evidence regarding use in acute lung injury)
    - **Weaning from respiratory support:**
        - **Problems with weaning from respiratory support** - patients unable to sustain modest degree of respiratory work:
            - Low lung compliance - e.g. from severe lung injury such as ARDS
            - Respiratory muscle weakness - from exhaustion or disuse of respiratory muscles
            - High work of breathing
        - **Criteria for weaning from respiratory support:**
            - <u>Indications for mechanical intervention</u> - original indication for mechanical ventilation has resolved
            - <u>Satisfactory gas exchange function</u> - PaO2 \> 10kPa (75 mmHg) on FiO2 \< 0.5; PaCO2 \< 6kPa (\< 45 mmHg)
            - <u>Stable circulation</u> - no S/S of pulmonary oedema, heart failure or excessive fluid overload
            - <u>Regaining of consciousness</u> - patient regains consciousness and able to cough and protect airway
            - <u>Adequate analgesics</u> - pain relief sufficient
            - <u>Metabolically stable</u> - no pending metabolic problems
        - **Approaches to weaning:**
            - <u>Spontaneous breathing trials</u> (SBTs) - removal of all respiratory support (along with sedation breaks) on daily basis and observing how long the patient breathes unassited
            - <u>Progressive reduction in pressure support ventilation</u> - progressive reduction in PSV applied for each breath over period of hours or days according to patients response
            - <u>Weaning protocols</u> - initiate and progress weaning within agreed guidelines to reduce ventilation times

1.  Acute Lung Injury and the Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome

    - **Definitions and terminology:**
      - <u>Acute lung injury</u> (ALI) - diffuse acute inflammatory process of the lungs resulting from direct, or indirect causes
      - <u>Acute respiratory distress syndrome</u> (ARDS) - severe acute lung injury resulting in type 1 respiratory failure (hyoxaemia without hypercapnia)
    - **Etiology of ALI and ARDS:** 
    ![](img/conditions_predisposing_to_ards.png)
    - **Pathophysiology of ALI and ARDS:**
      - **Diffuse inflammatory process of the lung** - inflammation occurs throughout the lungs affecting both endothelial and epithelial cells, with damage to type I and II alveolar cells:
        - <u>Exudation</u> - sequestration of activated neutrophils into alveoli and accumulation of protein-rich cellular fluid within alveoli results in formation of the pathologically characteristic <u>'hyaline membranes'</u>
        - <u>Loss of surfactant and impaired surfactant production</u> - as a result of widespread damage to type II alveolar cells
      - **Physiological effects of widespread inflammation:**
        - <u>Consolidation</u> - due to accumulation of exudates
        - <u>Reduced lung compliance</u> - from loss of surfactant resulting in impaired ventilation and increased work of breathing
        - <u>Alveolar collapse</u> - from loss of surfactant resulting n increased VQ mismatch, increased pulmonary shunt, and hypoxaemia
    - **Ix of ALI and ARDS:**
      - **CXR** - non-specific and difficult to distinguish from APO or cardiac failure:
        - Bilateral coalescent opacities
        - Absence of effusions, lung collapse or nodules explaining opacities

        
        ![](img/cxr_ards.png)
    - **Dx of ARDS** - Berlin definition of ARDS:
      - <u>Time course</u> - within 1 week of known clinical insult, or new or worsening respiratory symptoms
      - <u>Radiographic features</u> - bilateral opacities, not fully explained by effusions, collapse or nodules
      - <u>Not attributable to heart failure</u> - patient not in fluid overload and objective assessment by echocardiography to exclude hydrostatic oedema
      - <u>Impaired oxygenation</u> - attributable to VQ mismatch as defined by VF ratio

      
      ![](img/berlin_definition_of_ards.png)

### Sepsis

- **Definition** - localised infection that progresses to an uncontrolled systemic inflammatory response, rapidly leading to acute physiological deterioration with risk of MOF and death
- **Epidemiology** - increasing incidence:
    - <u>Incidence</u> - increasing incidence in recent years (attributable to population aging, increased use of invasive surgery, higher bacterial resistance, increasing number of immunocomprimised patients)
- **Risk factors for sepsis:**
    - <u>Immunosuppression</u> - DM, immunodeficiency, haematological disorders
    - <u>Substance</u> - alcohol and substance abuse
    - <u>Chronic comorbidities</u> - chronic disease (heart, lungs, kidneys, liver), cancer
    - <u>Trauma</u> - trauma and burns
    - <u>Iatrogenic</u> - recent surgery/ invasive procedure, invasive lines (IV, arterial, urinary catheterse, NG tubes), prolonged ICU stay, invasive ventilation, stress ulcer prophylaxis
- **Common sites of infection in critically ill patients:**
    - <u>Chest infections</u> (esp. nosocomial pneumonia) - high risk of nosocomial pneumonia in ICU patients that are intubated especially if ABx given
    - <u>Intra-abdominal infections</u> - intra-abdominal abscess or necrotic gut should be considered in post-operative patients, while pancreatitis, acalculous cholecystitis and PPU can complicate prolonged critical care
    - <u>Urinary tract infection</u> - uro sepsis a relatively unusual source in unexplained sepsis
    - <u>Intravenous lines</u> (esp. central lines) - replace any lines that have not been changed for \> 4d
    - <u>Other sites of infections</u>:
        - Infective endocarditis
        - Meningitis and other CNS infections
        - Septic arthritis and osteomyelitis
        - Rhinosinusitis, otitis media and deep neck space infections
        - Genitourinary infections
        - Gastrointestinal infection

  
  ![](img/sites_of_infections_in_critically_ill_patients.png)
- **Clinical presentation of sepsis** - speed of onset may mimic major PE or acute MI:
    - **Features relating to focus of infection:**
        - <u>Chest infections</u> - cough and purulent sputum, dyspnoea, consolidations
        - <u>Invasive line infections</u> - erythema, tenderness over line
        - <u>Intra-abdominal infections</u> - abdominal pain, N/V etc.
    - **Features of systemic inflammatory response syndrome** - defined by the presence of two or more of the following:
        - <u>Respiratory rate</u> - \> 20/min
        - <u>Heart rate</u> - \> 90 bpm
        - <u>WBC</u> - \> 12 x 10<sup>9</sup>/L (leukocytosis) or \< 4 x 10<sup>9</sup>/L (septic neutropenia)
        - <u>Temperature</u> - \> 38°C (**fever**) or \< 36°C (septic hypothermia)
        - <u>PaCO2</u> - \< 4.3 kPa (\< 32 mmHg) or ventilated
    - **Presentation as septic shock or hypotension** - characteristically initially a/w wide pulse pressure (e.g. 115/42 mmHg) indicating early septicaemia, which progresses into narrow pulse pressure (e.g. 78/68 mmHg) due to distributive shock
        - <u>Wide pulse pressure</u> - due to systemic endothelial dysfunction and vasodilation manifesting as **warm peripheries** ('warm' shock), **bounding pulse**
        - <u>Narrow pulse pressure</u> - due to 'third spacing' and shrunken intravascular volume, manifesting as **cold peripheries** ('cold' shock), **absent pulse**
- **Ix in sepsis** - complete septup workup:
    - **Routine bloods** - CBC, LRFT, inflammatory markers, ABG, Clotting profile, RBG, latate, procalcitonin:
        - **CBC:**
            - <u>Hb</u> - anaemia due to red blood cel
            - <u>WBC</u> - leukocytosis \> 12 x 10<sup>9</sup>/L or leukopenia \< 4 x 10<sup>9</sup>/L
            - <u>Platelet</u> - thrombocytopenia (\< 100 x 10<sup>9</sup>/L)
        - **LFT:**
            - <u>Transaminase</u> - rapid rise of transaminase with reversed de Retis ratio (\> 10000 mmol/L) with raid decline compatible with <u>shock liver</u>
            - <u>Bilirubin</u> - hyperbilirubinaemia (\> 70 mmol/L) reflective of acute liver failure
        - **RFT:**
            - <u>Electrolytes</u> - derranged in AKI, and rarely reflects adrenal insufficiency (hypoNa, hyperK)
            - <u>Cr</u> - sudden increase in sCr \> 44.2 mmol/L compatible with KDIGO guidelines of AKI
        - **Inflammatory markers** (CRP/ESR) - non-specifically elevated
        - **ABG:**
            - <u>PaO2</u> - hypoxaemia (PF ratio \< 300)
            - <u>PaCO2</u> - may reveal hypercapnia
            - <u>pH and HCO3</u> - acidosis, which may be HAGMA (septic shock), respiratory acidosis or mixed
        - **Clotting profiles** - prolonged PT and aPTT suggestive of DIC
        - **RBG** - hyperglycaemia in the absence of DM
        - **Lactate** - \> 2 mmol/L as the earliest sign of septic shock
        - **Procalcitonin** - elevated reflects bacterial infection and sepsis
    - **Urinalysis** - look for UTI
    - **CXR** - look for pneumonia
    - **Microbiological workup:**
        - <u>Blood cultures</u> - only 10% of ICU patients with clinical diagnosis of septic shock will have +ve blood cultures due to prior ABx Tx
        - <u>Sputum culture</u> - microbiological workup for pneumonia
        - <u>Culture of IV lines, urine and wound discharge</u> - if applicable
    - **Additional investigations** - dependent on suspected infection:
        - <u>Abdominal USG</u> - look for intra-abdominal infections and biliary disease
        - <u>CT abdomen</u> - look for intra-abdominal source of infections
        - <u>Echocardiography</u> - if suspected infective endocarditis
        - <u>Lumbar puncture</u> - for suspected meningitis only if **platelet and clotting normal**

    
    ![](img/sites_of_infections_in_critically_ill_patients.png)
- **Mx:**
    - **Principles of Mx:**
        - <u>Prompt resuscitation</u> - high concentration O2 and volume resuscitation
        - <u>Establish monitoring</u> - per ICU care
        - <u>Eradication of infection</u> - adminstration of appropriate IV ABx with spectrum wide enough to cover probable organisms based on likely site and source of infection
        - <u>Mx of multi-organ dysfunction</u> - e.g. vasoactive agents for shock
        - <u>Additional therapies</u> - e.g. sedative and muscle relaxants, corticosteroids, inotropes, packed cells transfusion
    - **Initial Mx** - secure airway, correct hypoxaemia and establish IV access 
    ![](img/davidson_mx_of_severe_sepsis.png)
    - **O2 therapy** - give high concetration O2 (+/- additional respiratory support):
        - <u>Rationale</u> - reasons for administration of high concentration O2 in sepsis:
            - Reduced cardiac output - sepsis is a common cause of cardiac suppression especially in later stages of sepsis
            - Increased oxygen consumption - sepsis is a hypermetabolic state (at least in hyperinflammatory sepsis)
            - Microcirculatory collapse - failed regional distribution of flow despite optimal DO2 results in cellular hypoxia
            - Ineffective oxygen utilisation - mitochondrial dysfunction accompanying SIRS results in low tissue oxygen extraction ratio (OER) despite normal or supra-physiological DO2
        - <u>Target</u> - not known, but typically target 90-96% on pulse oximetry
    - **Volume resuscitation** - fluid challenges even if clinically not dry:
        - <u>Rationale</u> - relative volume depletion due to vasodilation and hypotension (MAP titrated at 65 mmHg to ensure CO is not the limiting factor of global DO2)
        - <u>Regimen</u> - no rigid protocol due to unpredictable response:
            - Blood transfusion - indicated based on Hb (restrictive transfusion strategy)
            - Colloids - 100-200 mL boluses to assess BP response to volume (?Surviving Sepsis guidelines)
            - Crystalloids
    - **Vasoactive agents** - noradrenaline typically preferred to raised BP
    - **Sedatives and muscle relaxants:**
        - <u>Rationale</u> - reduced demand (i.e. metabolic rate) to ensure DO2 is sufficient to meet demand
    - **Corticosteroids** - corticosteroid replacement therapy is controversial:
        - <u>Rationale</u> - certain series demonstrated 30% of patients have demonstrable adrenal insufficiency by short Synacthen test
        - <u>Evidence</u> - a/w earlier resolution of shock but has no effects on survival

## General Principles of Critical Care Management

- **Management of patients on admission to ICU:**
    - <u>Handover</u> - transferring team to ICU staff with review of medical and social history
    - <u>Clinical assessment</u> - complete clinical assessment
    - <u>Stabilisation</u> - ongoing resuscitation
    - <u>Monitoring</u> - establishment of monitoring
    - <u>Workup</u> - investigation to establish or confirm definitive diagnosis
    - <u>Management</u> - formulation and implementation of management care
    - <u>Communication</u> - communication with and explanation to relatives
- **Daily clinical management in the ICU** - regular clinical assessment aat least daily and ward rounds:
    - **Role of ward rounds:**
        - <u>Detailed clinical assessment systematic assessment</u> - at least twice daily to detect changes in patient condition
        - <u>Revise Mx</u> - alter management based on patient's condition and application of evidence-based measures to reduce complications (FAST HUG)
    - **Evidence-based measures to reduce complications** - FAST HUG:
        - Feeding
        - Analgesia
        - Sedation
        - Thromboprohylaxis
        - Head of bed elevation
        - Ulcer prophylaxis
        - Glucose control
    - **Daily clinical assessment** - patient review should include:
        - <u>Progress notes</u> - review of progress reports from nursing and medical staff, and any specialist opnion
        - <u>Review hourly charts</u> - vital signs, GCS etc.
        - <u>Physical examination</u> - general (skin, line site, wounds etc.), system examinations
        - <u>System reviews</u>:
            - Cardiovascular - haemodynamics, volume status, fluids and inotropes
            - Respiratory - ventilation settings, ABG
            - Gastrointestinal - nutrition, NG aspirate, bowel function
            - Renal - urine output, volume status, RFTs, RRT
            - Neurological - sedation, GCS, pupil response
        - <u>Laboratory results</u> - haematology, coagulation, LRFTs
        - <u>Microbiology</u> - temprature, WBC, line sites, and other possible sources of infection, resultes of cultures, antibiotic therapy
        - <u>Drug therapy</u> - consider adverse effects, drug drug interactions, discontinue unecessary medications
        - <u>Imaging</u> - review X-rays and other imaging investigations
        - <u>Monitoring</u> - ensure that all measures are measured (remove CVP catheter, A-line and peripheral venous catheters soon if not needed)
        - <u>Management</u> - formulate an integrated plan
- **Sedation and analgesia:**
    - **Reasons for sedation in critically ill patient:**
        - <u>Comfort</u> - ensure comfort and relieve anxiety to reduce pain, and increase tolerance for invasive procedures
        - <u>Reduced metabolic demand</u> - e.g. in conditions of raise dICP that require deep sedation and reduce tissue oxygen requirements and prevent ischaemic damage
    - **Complications of excessive sedation** - common in ICU patients:
        - Longer ICU stays
        - Higher prevalence of delirium
        - Prolonged requirement for mechanical ventilation
        - More ICU-acquired infections
    - **Selection for sedation** - continuous infusion of seadative and/or analgesic drugs:
        - <u>Sedative</u> - propofol (short acting IV agent with predictable half life not affected by liver and renal impairment)
        - <u>Analgesia</u> - morphine (caution in AKI/CKD), fentanyl, alfentanil
    - **Monitoring of sedation** - patient should be comfortable and tolerate Tx, but is awake and lucid:
        - <u>Richmond Agitation Sedation Scale</u> (RASS): 
        ![](img/richmond_agitation_sedation_scale.png)
        - <u>Advantages of regular monitoring</u> - a/w shorter ICU stays
    - **Sedation break** - daily period when all sedation is stopped for re-assessment of patient (reduces over-sedation and shortens ICU stay)
    - **Muscle relaxants** - limited utility as high prevalence of precipitating critical illness neuropathy and myopathy:
        - <u>Indications for muscle relaxants</u>:
            - Rapid sequence induction for ET intubation (e.g. succinylcholine)
            - Raised ICP for prevention of cough (e.g. atracurium)

## Discharge from Intensive Care

## Outcomes of Intensive Care

- **Common measures of outcomes in ICU:**
    - ICU mortality - ~ 20% combined but variable
    - Hospital mortality - 30% combined
    - ICU length of stay
    - Hospital length of stay
- **Factors affecting ICU mortality:**
    - <u>Type of admission</u> - medical vs surgical
    - <u>Age</u> - single strongest independent risk factor of ICU mortality
    - <u>Comorbidities</u> - significant pre-existing comorbidities, esp. fever disease, severe cardiorespiratory disease, malignancy, immunosuppression, chronic renal failure
    - <u>General condition</u> - poor pre-existing nutritional state and/or general physical condition
    - <u>Acute disease severity</u> - e.g. APACHE score, SAPS score
    - <u>Disease course</u> - shock, respiratory failure, AKI etc.
- **Scoring systems:**
    - <u>Examples</u>:
        - APACHE score
        - SAPS II score
    - <u>Principle</u>:
        - Big data analysis - calculated from large databases generated from a range of ICUs in the States (principally used to evaluate performance relative to reference ICUs by calculating SMRs)
        - Predicted mortality - correlation with hospital mortality (but not 100% accurate)
