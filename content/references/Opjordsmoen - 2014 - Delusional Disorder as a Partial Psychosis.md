# Opjordsmoen - 2014 - Delusional Disorder as a Partial Psychosis

- **Historical Context: The Paradigm of "<u>Partial Psychosis</u>"**
    - Understanding modern <u>delusional disorder</u> requires revisiting its foundational conceptualization as a "<u>partial psychosis</u>" or paranoia, a term comprehensively described by the French psychiatrist Esquirol in 1838.
    - Core Psychopathology of <u>Partial Psychosis</u>:
        - The condition is defined by encapsulated, well-organized, and persistent delusions.
        - The delusions are defended intelligently, utilizing sharp argument and a great deal of emotion.
        - Crucially, the psychopathological part (the delusions) is encapsulated, meaning the rest of the patient's personality remains as normal as it used to be.
    - "<u>La Monomania Raisonnante</u>" (Goal-Directed Planning):
        - Patients behave rationally in areas outside their delusions, making their argumentation appear quite convincing to onlookers.
        - The individual applies their full intellectual capacity and initiative to achieve defined goals aligned with their delusional content.
    - Clinical Evasion and <u>Dissimulation</u>:
        - The condition is notoriously difficult to uncover because patients adapt to their surroundings and actively dissimulate to avoid appearing "mad" to others.
        - Patients often refuse psychiatric help, perceiving suggestions that their beliefs are psychological symptoms as humiliating accusations.
        - While laypeople might assume the delusions have vanished, the patient remains internally convinced and driven.
- **Conceptualizing Delusions: From Categorical to Dimensional** ([Note not found: Delusions and Other Disorders of the Content of Thought](id:5ba36f4a-c635-4a77-afa0-37930b3da55a))
    - Historically, definitions of delusions trace back to Karl Jaspers, who characterized them as abnormal beliefs held with extraordinary conviction, impervious to counter-arguments or experiential evidence, and often bizarre.
    - The Problem with the "<u>Bizarre Criterion</u>":
        - DSM-IV used bizarre content to distinguish schizophrenia from delusional disorder (which required nonbizarre delusions involving plausible real-life situations, like being followed or having a disease).
        - This distinction is problematic, as experts lack strong agreement on what constitutes "bizarre" versus "nonbizarre" delusions.
        - Furthermore, distinguishing delusions from strongly held (overvalued) ordinary beliefs is clinically challenging.
    - The <u>Multidimensional Continuum Model</u>: \[Image of multidimensional continuum of delusions\]
        - Delusions are now best understood not as a binary category, but along a continuum, deviating from normal beliefs across multiple dimensions.
        - Factor analyses of rating scales highlight five distinct dimensions of delusions:
            - 1\. Conviction.
            - 2\. Delusional construct (organization and bizarreness).
            - 3\. Preoccupation.
            - 4\. Subjective distress.
            - 5\. Behavior.
- **Etiological Frameworks**
    - The source text outlines two major theoretical frameworks regarding the origin and tenacity of delusional beliefs:
        - Dynamic and Psychoanalytic Mechanisms:
            - Delusional tenacity may serve a defensive or dynamic function for the patient.
            - Delusions act as an attempt to escape tension and anxiety utilizing the psychological processes of projection and denial.
        - Organic and Cognitive Hypotheses:
            - Organic brain disorders presenting with perceptual difficulties.
            - Alterations in "theory of mind".
            - Underlying emotional disturbances.
            - Biases in probabilistic reasoning, specifically "jumping to conclusions".
            - Attributional biases.
- **DSM-5 Diagnostic Criteria and Shifts**
    - The DSM-5 organizes psychotic disorders along a psychopathological gradient, placing delusional disorder at the "benign" end, as it is limited strictly to the domain of delusions.
    - Diagnostic Requirements:
        - Presence of delusions for at least 1 month without other psychotic symptoms.
        - Hallucinations may be present, but they must not be prominent and must be strictly related to the delusional theme.
        - Key Shift: DSM-5 no longer requires delusions to be nonbizarre.
    - Functional Impact:
        - While delusional content can influence behavior, impairments in psychosocial functioning may be more circumscribed than in schizophrenia.
        - Behavior is not obviously odd or bizarre.
    - Symptom Severity Tracking (DSM-5 Section III):
        - Clinicians rate psychosis symptom severity over the past 7 days on a scale from 0 (not present) to 4 (severe).
        - A severe score (4) for delusions specifically denotes a "severe pressure to act upon beliefs, or is very bothered by beliefs".
- **Clinical Course, Prognosis, and Treatment Resistance**
    - \[Image of diagnostic stability in delusional disorder vs schizophrenia\] The heterogeneity of delusional disorder leads to varied clinical outcomes, distinct from the typical trajectory of schizophrenia.
    - Diagnostic Stability and Transition:
        - Delusional disorder has moderate diagnostic stability (around 60% in long-term follow-up), whereas schizophrenia is highly stable (75%-99%).
        - In long-term studies, roughly 20% of cases diagnosed as delusional disorder are eventually changed to a diagnosis of schizophrenia.
    - Prognostic Outcomes:
        - Remission occurs in approximately one-third of patients.
        - Remission is most likely when the duration of psychosis before treatment is short and when precipitating factors are clearly present during development.
        - For most stable patients, delusions weaken over the long run, but a small core group maintains the same delusional intensity for years.
    - Therapeutic Challenges:
        - While pharmacological and cognitive-behavioral therapies result in improvement for some, a cohort of nonresponsive patients exists.
        - In nonresponsive patients, the conviction becomes a "way of living or assessing life".
        - For these individuals, logical counterarguments are entirely useless, regardless of the relevance of the information presented.
- **Forensic and Clinical Challenges: The <u>Breivik Case</u>**
    - The tragic case of Anders Behring Breivik (Norway, 2011) illustrates the profound medicolegal difficulty in assessing "<u>partial psychosis</u>," highlighting how highly organized, goal-directed behavior can mask or complicate severe psychopathology.
    - Evaluation 1: Paranoid Schizophrenia (Legally Unaccountable).
        - Psychiatrists concluded Breivik had grandiose delusions with bizarre qualities regarding his mission (e.g., leading the Knights Templars, deciding who lives/dies, planning ethnic cleansing and genetic pool improvements).
        - Clinical observations included neologisms (using common words in unusual contexts) and affective flattening with incongruent affect.
    - Evaluation 2: Narcissistic Personality Disorder & Pseudologia Fantastica (Legally Accountable).
        - Following months of observation, Breivik toned down his delusions, admitting exaggeration and labeling himself a mere "foot-soldier".
        - The second psychiatric team interpreted his pathological self-aggrandizement as never reaching a psychotic severity.
        - His pre-attack suspiciousness and social withdrawal were framed as natural consequences of systematically planning his terror acts.
    - Clinical Implication:
        - This discrepancy mirrors the 19th-century debates surrounding Esquirol's "<u>partial psychosis</u>," where individuals commit crimes driven by unusual thoughts but otherwise exhibit systematic, rational planning.
        - The case underscores the diagnostic gap between full-blown schizophrenia and severe personality disorders.
