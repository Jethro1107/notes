### Acute monoarthritis

- **Definition** - sudden onset of pain and swelling over a single joint
- **Etiology of acute monoarthritis:**
    - Septic arthritis
    - Crystal-induced arthritis - gout, pseudogout
    - Reactive arthritis
    - Trauma and haemarthrosis
    - Seronegative spondyloarthritis - psoriatic arthritis, ankylosing spondylitis, enteropathic arthritis
    - Monoarticular presentation of CTD
    - Others - leukaemia, osteomyelitis (can presentation as monoarthritis in children)

  
  ![](img/acute_monoarthritis_etiology.png)
- **Salient points of Hx:**
    - **Demographic** - age and gender:
        - Young men - reactive arthritis
        - Middle-aged men - gout
        - Older women - pseudogout
    - **HPI** - site (pattern of joint involvement), onset, associated Sx
        - <u>Site</u> - consider the pattern of joint involvement and pre-dillection of joints for specific pathologies (e.g. MTP joint for gout, knee and wrist for pseudogout)
        - <u>Onset</u> - development to maximal intensity varies for different pathologies
            - Crystal-induced arthritis - rapid onset (6-12h)
            - Septic arthritis - slower onset and continuously progressive until treatment
            - Haemarthrosis - rapid onset precipitated by trauma
            - Pigmented villonodular synovitis - gradual onset
    - **Triggers** - any prior triggers:
        - <u>Trauma</u> - haemarthrosis
        - <u>Previous diarrhoeal illness or recent sexual contact</u> - reactive arthritis
        - <u>Dehydration/ surgery/ intercurrent illness</u> - crystal-induced arthritis
    - **PMH:**
        - Pre-existing joint pathologies - RA (acute monoarthritis strongly suggestive of sepsis), OA (risk factor of pseudogout)
        - CKD - risk factor for gout
    - **SHx** - alcohol (RF for gout) +/- sexual Hx
    - **FHx**
- **Ix:**
    - **Joint aspiration and synovial fluid analysis** - mandatory to <u>r/o septic arthritis</u>:
        - Appearance - colour, turbidity, viscosity
        - WBC and differentials
        - Microbiology - Routine G stain, C/ST
        - Polarised light microscopy - birefringence in crystal-induced arthritis
    - **CBC** - leukocytosis
    - **RFT** - ?esp. if contemplating NSAID use
    - **Inflammatory markers** (ESR, CRP) - non-specifically elevated in sepsis, crystal-induced arthritis and reactive arthritis
    - **Serum uric acid levels** - helpful, but non-diagnostic for gout
    - **Blood culture/ culture from other possible primary site infections** - esp. for patients suspected to have septic arthritis
    - **Clotting profiles** - identify bleeding tendancies predisposing to haemarthrosis
    - +/- Serology - ANA, RF, ACPA
- **Mx:**
    - IV ABx - after blood culture if clinically suspect sepsis
    - Mx of underlying cause

### Polyarthritis

- **Definition** - Pain and swelling affecting \>= joints or joint groups
- **DDx of polyarthritis** - differentiated by patterns of joint involvement and extra-articular manifestations:
    - <u>Common causes</u>:
        - **Rheumatoid arthritis** - symmetrical small and large joint involvements (UL & LL)
        - **Viral arthritis** - symmetrical small joint involvement (a/w rash and prodromal illness)
        - **Noadal OA** - symmetrical DIP, PIP, CMC and large joint involvement (a/w Herberden's nodes)
        - **Seronegative spondyloarthropathies:**
            - Psoriatic arthritis - varying clinical presentations (a/w nail dystrophy and psoriasis)
            - Ankylosing spondylitis, Axial spondylitis, enteropathic spondylitis - irregular asymmetrical oligoarthritis with LL predilectation (a/w inflammatory back pain)
        - **SlE** - symmetrical small joint involvement (Raynaud's phenomenon, cutaneous lupus, photosensitivity)
    - <u>Less common causes</u>:
        - JIA
        - Chronic gout
        - Systemic sclerosis and polymyositis
        - Hypertrophic osteoarthropathy
        - Haemochromatosis
        - Acromegaly
- **Salient points of Hx:**
    - HPI - characterise pattern of joint involvement in polyarthritis and associated Sx:
        - Pattern of joint involvement - Symmetry, small and large joint involvement, axial involvement 
        ![](img/pattern_of_joint_involvement_polyarthritis.png)
        - Associated symptoms - mucocutaneous manifestations, occular manifestations, abdominal organs, systemic symptoms: 
        ![](img/extra-articular_features_in_polyarthritis.png)
- **Ix:**
    - Routine bloods - CBC, LRFT
    - Inflammatory markers - ESR, CRP
    - Immunological screen - ANA, RF, ACPA
    - +/- US - demonstrate synovitis if not evident clinically
- **Mx:**
    - All efforts directed at diagnosis

### Generalised musculoskeletal pain

    - <u>Disease of the bones</u> - osteomalacia, Paget's disease of the bone
    - <u>Disease of the joints</u> - osteoarthritis, joint hypermobility
    - <u>Rheumatological disease</u> - myositis, polymyalgia rheumatica

### Back pain

- **Epidemiology:**
	- _Prevalence_ - affects 60-80% of people at some time in their lives
	- _Incidence_ - 7% of UK adults consult GP for low back pain
	- _Demographic_:
		- Age - affects all ages, where age of onset determines the likely Dx
- **Etiology of back pain:**
	- 
	![[Pasted image 20260426205832.png]]
	- **Mechanical back pain** - most common cause of acute self-limiting low back pain in patients 20-55y
	- **Spinal pathologies:**
		- _Anatomical spine pathologies_ - lumbar spine stenosis, prolapsed disc, spondylolisthesis, vertebral fractures, Paget's disease of the bone
		- _Inflammatory spine pathologies_ - Seronegative spondyloarthropathies, e.g. AS, SpA, PsA
		- _Destructive spine pathologies_:
			- Infections - spondylodisciitis, spinal epidural abscesses
			- Malignancies - e.g. prostate, lung, breast, myeloma
		- _Other spine pathologies_:
			- Diffuse idiopathic skeletal hyperostosis
			- Arachnoiditis
- **Mechanical low back pain:**
	- **Epidemiology** - most common cause of acute, self-limiting low back pain:
		- Considered in 20-55y
		- Usually more common in manual workers, particular those in occupations that involve heavy lifting and twisting
	- **Clinical features of mechanical low back pain**:
		- _Site_ - confined to the lumbosacral region, buttock, and ocassionally the thigh, often lateralised and asymetrical
		- _Onset_ - usually provoked by lifting or bending
		- _Progression_ - generally good prognosis:
			- 30% w/ symptomatic improvement in 2 days
			- 90% w/ full recovery within 6 weeks
			- 10-15% patients develop chronic back pain which is a/w psychogenic elements
		- _Provacation_ - often exacerbated by activity (and specific ranges of movements) and relieved by rest
		- _Quality_ - mechanical in nature, mildly tender
		- _Radiation_ - never radiates beyond the knee (presence suggests sciatica)
		- 
		![[Pasted image 20260426210508.png]]
	- **Signs:**
		- Asymmetrical paraspinal muscle spasm
		- Asymmetrical muscle tenderness (not midline tenderness)
- **Red flags for possible spinal pathologies:**
	- 
	![[Pasted image 20260426210808.png]]
- **Ix** - Ix not required for acute mechanical low back pain, but should be considered for those w/ 1) persistent pain (> 6 weeks) or 2) red flag features:
	- **Imaging:**
		- _MRI whole spine_ - demonstration of:
			- Structural changes - e.g. spinal stenosis, cord compression, nerve root compression
			- Inflammatory changes - e.g. infections, metastasis, spondyloarthropathies
		- _Plain X Ray of the lumbar spine_ - role in identification of:
			- Vertebral fractures
			- Degenerative changes
		- _PETCT_ - considered if suspecting malignancies
	- **Blood tests** - CBC, LRFT, ESR/ CRP, SPE, PSA
- **Mx of mechanical low back pain:**
	- _Reassurance and education_ - emphasise on self-limiting nature of condition and that exercise is helpful rather than damaging
	- _Advice_:
		- Emphasise role of exercise
		- Bed rests not helpful and may increase risk of chronic disability
		- Return to work and normal activity should take place as soon as possible
	- _Prescription_ - regular analgesics (e.g. NSAIDs) to improve mobility and facilitate exercise
	- _Referral_ - for physiotherapy aimed at return to normal activities
	- _Investigations and follow up_ - monitoring of progress and consider investigations if persistent back pain

### Regional musculoskeletal pain
#### Neck Pain

- **Etiology of neck pain:**
	- **Mechanical neck pain:**
		- _Traumatic_ - whiplash injury, RTA
		- _Postural_ - torticollis, falling asleep in abnormal position
		- _Degenerative_ - cervical spondylosis, intervertebral disc herniation
	- **Non-mechanical neck pain:**
		- _Rheumatological disorders_ - RA, PMR, JIA, seronegative spondyloarthropathies, fibromyalgia
		- _Infections_ - septic arthritis, spondylitis, spondylodisciitis
		- _Metabolic disorders_ - osteoporosis, osteomalacia, paget's disease of the bone
		- _Neoplastic disorders_ - pancoast tumour, metastasis to the spine, multiple myeloma, lymphoma intrathecal tumours
	- **Referred pain from distant sites:**
		- _Heart_ - angina pectoris typically lateralised in a dermatomal fashion (C3-4)
		- _Oral cavity_ - pharynx and dental problems
		- _Diaphragm_ - e.g. irritation of diaphragm but HBP pathologies
		- _Others_ - aortic aneurysm, cervical LN
	- 
	![[Pasted image 20260426190837.png]]
- **Approach to clinical assessment:**
	- r/o traumatic causes (e.g. whiplash injury)
	- Screen for focal neurological S/S a/w cervical spondylosis
	- Screen for constitutional symptoms for sinister pathologies, e.g. fever (infections), weight loss (neoplasia)
	- r/o referred pain from the heart
- **Principles of Mx:**
	- _Orthotics_ - neck collars for injuries
	- _Pharmacological Tx_ - analgesics for symptomatic relief
	- _Investigations and referral_ - MRI for those w/ focal neurological S/S and referral

#### Hand and Wrist Pain

- **Etiology of hand and wrist pain:**
	- **Articular pathologies:**
		- _Nodal osteoarthritis_ - commonly first MCP joint or involvement of the DIP joints (Herberden nodes), but pain is not localised and radiates down the hand
		- _Rheumatoid arthritis_ - involvement of the MCP, PIP, and wrist joints
	- **Non-articular pathologies:**
		- _Tenosynovitis_ - flexor tenosynovitis or extensor tenosinovitis (De Quervain's disease) are caused by overuse (RSI), often a/w pain, swelling +/- warmth and fine crepitations
		- _Raynaud's phenomenon_ - vasoactive changes over the digits producing pain
		- _Radiculopathies_ - cervical spine radiculopathy at C8/T1 levels produces pain and weakness over the hands
		- _Nerve entrapment syndrome_ - various nerve entrapment syndromes such as CTS, and high/ low ulnar nerve palsy have characteristic sensory and motor Sx
		- _Reflex sympathetic dystrophy_  - gradual onset of chronic pain and swelling, a/w localised osteoporosis, and evidence of regional autonomic dysfunction such as sweating, colour and temperature changes
#### Hip Pain

- **Clinical features of hip pain:**
	- Usually maximal deep in the anterior groin
	- Variable radiation to the buttock, anterolateral thigh, knee, or shin
	- Maximal pain at the lateral aspects suggests an extra-articular disease, e.g. trochanteric bursitis, gluteal enthesopathy
	- ![[Pasted image 20260525121723.png]]
- **DDx of hip pain:**
	- **Local causes of hip pain:**
		- _Intra-articular pathologies_ - e.g. OA, septic arthritis, NOF fractures
		- _Extra-articular soft tissue pathologies_ - most commonly tronchateric bursitis:
			- ![[Pasted image 20260525122135.png]]
	- **Referred pain from the spine** - lumbar spine stenosis:
		- _T12-L1 root entrapment_ - results in pain in the lateral thigh
		- _L2-4 root entrapment_ - results in pain in the inguinal region and the lateral thigh
	- **Intra-abdominal/ pelvic disorders** - usually aggravated by resisted hip flexion:
		- Psoas abscess
		- Retroperitoneal haemorrhage

#### Knee Pain

- **Approach to DDx of knee pain:**
	- _Referred vs local_ - differentiated based on provocating factors
	- _Intra-articular vs periarticular_ - intra-articular pathologies may result in more diffuse and mechanical pain, while periarticular pathologies are well-localised to the structure
	- _Mechanical vs inflammatory_ - differentiate by features of inflammation (e.g. stiffness/ swelling, erythema, heat); note locking/ catching sensation as a red flag of mechanical knee pain
- **DDx of knee pain** - most common cause being Knee OA:
	- **Intra-articular pathologies:**
		- _Mechanical causes_ - e.g. OA knee, meniscal tear, osteochondritis dessicans, ligamentous sprain/ injuries, knee overuse syndromes (e.g. chondromalacia patellae)
		- _Inflammatory causes_ - e.g. chondrocalcinosis (CPPD), gout, septic arthritis
	- **Periarticular causes:**
		- ![[Pasted image 20260525122525.png]]
		- _Bursitis_ - typically results in anterior knee pain:
			- Pre-patellar bursitis (housemaid's knee) - a/w occupational kneeling
			- Infra-patellar bursitis (clergyman's knee) - usually felt superficial/ deep to the patellar tendon a/w occupational kneeling
			- Anserine bursitis - felt over upper medial tibia, often in middle-aged/ older adults w/ OA
		- _Tendinopathy/ enthesopathy_ - usually well-localised:
			- Inferior MCL enthesopathy - localised tenderness of upper medial tibia, aggravated by valgus stress to partly flexed knee
			- Patella tendon enthesopathy  - tenderness to the patellar tendon and tibial tuberosity
		- _Baker's cyst_ - fluid-filled sac in the posterior aspect of the knee secondary to intra-articular pathologies (mimicks DVT)
	- **Referred pain** - from hip and spine pathologies
- **DDx of anterior knee pain:**
	- Osgood-Schlatter disorder
	- Patellar tendinopathy
	- Pre-patellar/ infra-patellar bursitis
	- Patellofemoral pain syndrome (chondromalacia patellae)

## Principles of management of musculoskeletal disorders

- **Principles of Mx of musculoskeletal disorders:**
    - Patient education
    - Pain control
    - Optimise function
    - Modify the disease process where possible
    - Identify and treat related comorbidity
- **Factors to take into acount for the Mx of musculoskeletal disorders:**
    - Person's daily activity requirements, and work and recreational aspirations
    - Risk factors and associations of the musculoskeletal condition (e.g. obesity, muscle weakness, non-restorative sleep)
    - Patient's perception and knowledge of the condition
    - Medications and coping strategies already tried by the patient
    - Comorbid disease and its therapy
    - The availability, costs, and logistics of appropriate evidence-based intervention
- **Modalities of Mx:** 
	![](img/core_interventions_for_patients_with_painful_rheumatic_condition.png)
- **Patient education** - informed about the nature of condition, Ix, Tx and prognosis:
    - <u>Efficacy</u> - modest improvement to patients' wellbeing:
        - Understandng the nature of condition can reduce pain and disability
        - Improves self-efficacy and reduce healthcare cost
        - Effects may be partly due to better Tx adherance
	    ![](img/role_of_education_and_exercise_in_mx_of_arthritis.png)
    - <u>Forms of patient education</u>:
        - One on one discussion
        - Written literature or pamphlets
        - Patient-led group education classes
        - Interactive computer programs
        - Involvement of patients' family members
- **Lifestyle modification** - exercise, weight loss, joint protection:
    - **Exercise prescription** - two types (prescription w/ frequency, intensity, time, and type):
        - <u>Aerobic fitness training</u> - can produce long-term reduction in pain and disability possibly by **encouraging restorative sleep**, and benefits common comorbidities
        - <u>Local strengthening exercise</u> (small amounts often >> protracted sessions) - exercise for muscles that act over comprimisd joint by improving strength, proprioception, coordination, and balance over the diseased joint
    - **Weight loss** - reduction in mechanical strain on weight-bearing joint
    - **Joint protection** - avoid impact-loading and repetitive use of comprimised joint, e.g.:
        - Cessation of contact sports
        - Altered use of machinery or tools at workplace
        - Simple pacing activities (division into shorter segments w/ brief breaks)
        - Walking stick on contralateral side to take weight off diseased joint
- **Pharmacological therapy** - prescription according to WHO analgesic ladder:
    - **Paracetamol** - first choice for mild pain, and if successful, the preferred long-term analgesic:
        - <u>Regimen</u> - prn 1g up to qid
        - <u>MOA</u> - not fully known:
            - Inhibition of prostaglandin synthesis centrally
            - Effects on prostaglandin synthesis peripherally is less
        - <u>S/E</u> - generally well tolerated and few adverse effects:
            - GI events
            - Cardiovascular events
    - **NSAID** - typically useful in the Mx of pain that has an inflammatory component:
        - <u>Clinical indications</u> - useful for pain w/ inflammatory component:
            - Pain relief
            - Long-acting NSAIDs in the evening may help with early morning stiffness
		      ![](img/reccomendations_for_use_of_nsaids.png)
        - <u>MOA</u> - inhibition of prostaglandin and leukotriene synthesis by COX inhibition:
            - COX1 is constituitively expressed and fulfils 'housekeeping' function in platelets, gastric mucosa and kidneys (underlies S/E)
            - COX2 is inducible during inflammation, causing local pain and inflammation, and also acts centrally for pain modulation and fever
        - <u>S/E of non-selected NSAIDs</u>:
            - **GI S/E** - increased risk of dyspepsia, upper GI ulcer, bleeding or perforation (OR 4-5 but variable between NSAIDs)
            - **Increased risk of bleeding** - especially already on other antiplatelets and anticoagulants
            - **Renal impairment and fluid retention** - esp. in already comprimised renal function due to inhibition of renal prostaglandin production
            - **Immune-related S/E** (rare) - rash, asthma (aspirin-induced asthma), anaphylaxis, and acute interstitial nephritis can occur but are rare
        - <u>Varying ulcerogenicity of NSAIDs</u>: 
	        ![](img/varying_ulcerogenicity_of_nsaids.png)
        - <u>Risk factors of NSAID-related ulcers</u>: 
	        ![[risk_factors_of_nsaids.png]]
- **Non-pharmacological therapy:**
    - **Physical therapy and occupational therapy** - e.g. local therapies, hydrotherapy, splints and orthosis can improve patients well-being

## Osteoarthritis

- **Definition** - Osteoarthritis is a form of arthritis characterised by progressive degenrative loss of articular cartilage, with attempts of repair of cartilage and remodelling
- **Joint involvement** - Hip, Knees, PIP and DIP joints, neck, lumbar spine
- **Epidemiology** - most common cause of arthritis, major cause of pain and disability in older people:
    - Prevalence (increases w/ age) - 45% knee OA and 25% hip OA in all people (11% F and 8% M \> 50y require hip or knee replacement)
- **Pathophysiology** - caused by repetitive adverse loading of joints (overloading/ underloading):
    - **Risk factors for osteoarthritis:**
        - Aging - strong association, single most important risk factor
        - Constitutional factors - Heredity, sex, obesity, high BMD
        - Mechanical factors - prior trauma, anatomical factors (joint shape and alighments), usage (occupational, recreational)
        - ![[Pasted image 20260525213839.png]]
    - **Changes in articular cartilage** - typically at <u>points of maximal-loading</u>:
        - **Abnormal loading leads to death of condrocytes** - results in net degradation of cartilage matrix causing susceptibility to load-bearing injury
        - **Focal loss of articular cartilage** - <u>fibrillations</u> (fissuring of surface), <u>cracks</u> (vertical clefts) and eroisions,
    - **Changes in subchondral bone** - caused by abnormal loading directly onto bone:
        - Subchondral sclerosis - increased bone remodelling and deposition of new bone
        - Subchondral cysts - microfractures with fluid entering bone
        - Marginal osteophytes - formation of fibrocartilage, that undergoes endochondral ossification to form osteophytes, a maladaptation in hopes to increase surface area to better distribute stress
- **Clinical features of osteoarthritis:**
    - Pain and functional restriction in patient \>45y:
        - Pain - cause not completely understood but related to increased pressure in subchondral bone, low-grade synovitis, or from bursitis and enthesopathy secondary to altered joint mechanics:
            - Site - typically monoarthritis, or few joint involved
            - Onset - chronic, insiduous onset over mo or y (w/ variability over time, i.e. 'good days, bad days')
            - Character and provacation - mechanical pain related to movements or weight bearing, relieved by stress
            - Morning stiffness - brief morning stiffness (\< 15 min) and brief gelling (\< 5 min) after rest
        - Functional restriction - effects on ADL has equal if not greater effects than pain
- **Signs of OA:**
    - Look - deformity, muscle wasting and weakness, bony swelling around joint margins
    - Feel - absence of inflamm, palpable crepitus due to rough articular surfaces (ocassionally audible)
    - Move - Restricted ROM (capsular thickening or blocking osteophytes)
- **Ix:**
    - Plain X Ray - reveals joint-space narrowing (JSN), subchondral sclerosis, subchondral cyst, and marginal osteophytes formation:
        - Knee OA - weight-bearing (standing) AP, lateral (tibiofemoral joint) and skyline (patellofemoral joint) knee radiographs
        - Hip OA - non-weight-bearing PA pelvis radiographs
        - Spine OA - ?flexion-extension view
    - Routine bloods (CBC, LRFT, autoantibodies) - all should be within normal limits
    - Additional workup for early-onset OA - consider secondary causes (identifiable from Hx and XR)
- **Diagnosis** - typically clinical (EULAR):
    - 3 symptoms - persistent pain, limited morning stiffness, reduced function
    - 3 signs - bony enlargement, crepitus, restricted ROm

### Knee OA

- **Defintion of KOA** - OA targeting patello-femoral, medial tibio-femoral compartments, but eventually affects the entire joint
- **Epidemiology of KOA:**
    - Common in HK - varus deformity predisposes to OA of medial tibio-femoral compartments
    - Demographic:
        - F - more likely have bilateral symetrical involvements
        - M - unilateral OA attributable to trauma
- **Clinical features of KOA:**
    - **Pain** - chronic pain typically localised to the anterior or medial aspect of the knee and upper tibia:
        - Patello-femoral pain - worse on going up and down stairs or inclines
        - Posterior pain - complications in the popliteus (Baker's cyst)
    - **Functional restriction** - difficulties in rising from a chair, getting in and out of cars, putting on shoes and socks etc.
    - **Attacks of Calcium Pyrophosphate Deposition Disease** (CPPD) - overt inflammatory synovitis which predicts more rapid clinical and radiographic progression
- **Signs of KOA:**
    - Look - varus/ valgus deformity or fixed flexion deformity, disuse quadriceps wasting
    - Feel:
        - Joint-line or peri-articular tenderness
        - Bony swelling around joint lines
    - Move - restricted flexion/extension w/ coarse crepitus
    - Gait - antalgic gait or varus thrust:
        - Antalgic gait - jerky, asymetrical gait w/ less time on affected side due to pain
        - Varus thrust - accentuated varus deformity on loading that reduces when leg lifts off ground (due to chronic stretching of LCL)
### Clinical Features

- **Principles of clinical manifestations in OA** - main presenting symptoms are 1) pain, and 2) functional restriction in a patient > 45y, more often > 60y:
	- **Pain** - mechanism poorly understood, attributed to 1) increased loading on subchondral bone (acounts for night pain), 2) trebecular microfractures, 3) capsular distension, 4) low-grade synovitis, and 5) complicating bursitis/ enthesopathy secondary to abnormal joint mechanics:
		- _Site_ - typically mono-arthritis, ocassionally oligoarthritis
		- _Onset_ - insiduous onset over mo or years
		- _Progression_ - variable or intermittent over time ("good days, bad days")
		- _Provoking and relieving factors_ - mainly related to movement and weight-bearing, relieved by rests
		- _Associated Sx_:
			- Minimal S/S of inflammation including marked swelling, erythema, or hotness except secondary bursitis/ enthesopathy
			- Morning stiffness may be present but is brief (< 15-30 min), as with gelling after rest (< 5 min)
	- **Disability** - functional restriction of the hands, knees, or hips is an equal, if not greater, problem than pain
- **Signs of osteoarthritis:**
	- _Inspection_:
		- Deformities usually w/o instability
		- Muscle wasting may be marked
	- _Palpation_:
		- Bony swelling around joint lines
		- Joint-line or peri-articular tenderness
		- Palpable (ocassionally audible) coarse crepetus due to rough articular surface
	- _Movement_ - painful, restricted active and passive ROM due to capsular thickening or blocking of osteophytes
	- ![[Pasted image 20260525215128.png]]
- **Correlation between structural/ radiographical changes and symptomatology:**
	- Degree of correlation varies markedly according to site
	- Strength of correlation: hip > knee and spine > small joints
	- Biomechanical factors (e.g. body weight, quadriceps waisting), and adverse psychosocial factors (e.g. work stress, anxiety, depression) may correlate more strongly w/ pain and disabilities than the degree of radiographic changes
#### Generalised nodal OA

- **Predisposition to generalised nodal OA** - strong genetic component which predicts OA risks at other sites:
	- Marked female preponderance
	- Daughters of affected mothers has 1 in 3 chance of developing OA
	- People w/ nodal OA go one to develop OA at other sites, especially _knee OA_
- **Clinical features of generalised nodal OA** - some remain asymptomatic, most undergo a phase of episodic symptomatology w/ osteoarthritic changes until OA fully develops, where symptoms subsides:
	- **Pain** - fluctuating, episodic pain w/ movement (some are asymptomatic)
	- **Deformities** - prominent bony swellings seen on P/E (see below)
	- **Functional impairment** - overall function preserved (limited to mild pain when opening bottles)
- **Signs of generalised nodal OA:**
	- _Inspection_ - bony enlargement as a result of osteophyte formation, w/ misalignments due to asymmetrical focal finger loss:
		- **Lateral deviation of digits** - due to asymmetrical digit loss
		- **Herberden's nodes** - bony swelling of the PIP joints
		- **Bouchard's nodes** - bony swelling of the DIP joints
		- **Squaring of the thumb base** - deviation of the first CMC joint
		- ![[Pasted image 20260525220057.png]]
		- ![[Pasted image 20260525220116.png]]
	- _Palpation_ - presence of crepitus on joint movement, bony swelling
#### Knee OA

- **Joints involved in KOA** - focal involvement initially but eventually spreads to affect the whole joint:
	- Patellofemoral joint
	- Medial tibio-femoral compartment
	- Lateral tibiofemoral compartment
- **Clinical features of KOA:**
	- _Pain_ - typically over the anterior (patello-femoral) or medial aspects of the knee and upper tibia (posterior knee pain suggests Baker's cyst)
	- _Disability_ - difficulties in:
		- Prolonged walking
		- Rising from chair and getting in or out of a car
		- Bending to put on socks and shoes
- **Signs of KOA:**
	- **Inspection:**
		- _Deformities_ - varus deformities (rarely valgus deformities) +/- fixed flexion deformities
		- _Swelling_ - bony swelling around the joint lines
		- ![[Pasted image 20260525221004.png]]
	- **Palpation:**
		- _Bony swelling_ - around joint line w/ minimal effusion
		- +/- _Joint-line tenderness_ - likely peri-articular inflammation (e.g. anserine bursitis or MCL enthesopathy)
	- **Movement** - restrited flexion/ extension w/ coarse crepitus
	- **Gait** - antalgic gait w/ reduced stance phase on painful side (jerky and asymmetric)
	- **Power** - weakness of the quadriceps muscles
- **Causes of fluctuating knee pain in KOA** - secondary pathologies may develop on top of background of KOA:
	- Calcium pyrophosphate disease (CPPD) - association w/ OA most common at the knee
	- Baker's cyst - posterior knee pain and risk of rupture
	- Peri-articular inflamamtion - increased risk of secondary anserine bursitis and MCL tendinopathy
#### Hip OA

- **Area of involvement of hip OA** - asymmetrical joint involvement as in any OA, a/w various patterns:
	- _Superolateral OA_ (common) - targets superior aspects of the joint and a/w superolateral migration of the femoral head; typically unilateral presentation and poorer prognosis
	- _Central OA_ - targets medial aspects of the joint and a/w axial migration of the femoral head; typically bilateral onset, largely confined to F and a/w better prognosis
- **Clinical features of hip OA:**
	- _Pain_ - typically felt maximally deep on the anterior aspects of the groin w/ various radiations to the buttocks and knees
	- _Disability_ - similar as in OA knee, difficulties in:
		- Prolonged walking
		- Rising from chair and getting in or out of a car
		- Bending to put on socks and shoes
		- Pain in intercourse if restricted hip abduction in F
- **Signs:**
	- **Inspection:**
		- _Deformities_ - fixed flexion deformity/ external rotation deformity +/- ipsilateral leg shortening w/ severe joint attrition and superior femoral migration
		- _Wasting_ - wasting of the quadriceps and gluteal muscles
	- **Palpation** - anterior groin tenderness maximal just lateral to the femoral pulse
	- **Movement** - painful, restricted active and passive ROM seen earliest w/ internal rotation (most Sn)
#### Spine OA (Spondylosis)

- **Areas of involvement in spondylosis:**
	- Cervical spine (cervical spondylosis)
	- Lumbar spine (lumbar spondylosis)
- **Clinical features of spondylosis:**
	- _Pain_ - localised to the neck/ lower back +/- radiating pain w/ radiculopathy
	- _Focal neurological deficits_ - w/ radiculopathy or myelopathy
- **Signs of spondylosis:**
	- _Inspection_ - loss of lordosis
	- _Palpation_ - bony tenderness
	- _Movement_ - painful, restricted active and passive ROM
	- _Neurological examination and SLR test_ - reflection of myelopathy or radiculopathy

- **Principles of Mx** - OA treatment pyramid:
    - First line Mx - education, exercise and weight control
    - Second line Mx - First line + Pharmacological pain relief, aids and passive Tx by physio
    - Third line Mx - Second line + Surgery

- **Education** - explain the nature of condition, relevant risk factors (age, FHx, obesity, trauma):
	- Structural changes are permanent but they do not correlate well w/ pain and function, which can improve
	- Symptoms can be improved by muscle strengthening, analgesiam weight loss w/ joint replacement surgery reserved for refractory cases
	- Prognosis is as follows: nodal OA > knee OA > hip OA
- **Analgesics and anti-inflammatory drugs:**
	- _Non-pharmacological approaches_ - acupuncture and TENS demonstrate effective for knee OA
	- _Pharmacological approaches_:
		- Topical NSAIDs
		- Oral NSAIDs
		- Paracetamol/ compound analgesics (e.g. co-codamol)
		- Antineuropathic drugs (poor evidence base)

		- Duration of effects usually short lived

## Crystal-induced arthritis

- **Definition** - crystal deposition in and around the joints, manifesting as acute inflammatory arthritis, or chronic arthritis with progressive joint damage
- **Types of crystal-induced arthritis:**
    - **Gout** - acute and chronic gouty arthritis caused by deposition of <u>monosodium urate</u>
    - **Pseudogout** (CPDD) - arthritis caused by deposition of calcium pyrophosphate (typically in pathological joints)
    - Calficic periarthritis/ calcinosis - arthritis caused by deposition of basic calcium phosphate
    - Others - e.g. cholesterol, calcium oxalate, extrinsic crystals

  
  ![](img/crystal_associated_arthritis.png)
- **General pathophysiology of crystal-induced arthritis** - crystals may be the primary pathology (gout), or a precipitating factor on top of pathological joint (CPDD):
    - **Sufficient substrate concentration** - substrate concentration needs to be sufficient, but does <u>not necessarily be elevated</u> for crystals to be formed
    - **Formation of crystals** - balance of promoting or inhibitory tissue factors +/- physical factors (temp, pH):
        - Physiological joints are typically super-saturated w/ solutes, but depend on natural inhibitors to prevent crystal formation
    - **Inflammation** - typically related to a trigger:
        - Trauma - causing the release of crystals to trigger inflammation
        - Changes in substrate concentration - affects equilibrium of the reaction
        - Overlying inflammation - e.g. acute phase response in critical illness

### Gout

- **Definition** - <u>inflammatory arthritis</u> caused by deposition of <u>monosodium urate monohydrate crystals</u> in and around synovial joints
- **Epidemiology** - most common inflammatory arthritis in M and older F:
    - Prevalence - 1-2% (varies between populations)
    - Demographic - common in 30-60y, incidence increases w/ age, with male preponderance (M:F = 8:1)
- **Normal uric acid metabolism:**
    - Uric acid pool -1/3 from dietary source, 2/3 from purine metabolism:
        - Dietary purines - red meat, seafood, beer (guanosine)
        - Purine metabolism - <u>xanthine oxidase</u> degrades hypoxanthine to xanthines to uric acid
    - Uric acid excretion - 2/3 via renal excretion (filtration, and active secretion), 1/3 via biliary excretion
- **Pathophysiology of gout** - caused by reduced excretion, over-production or increased dietary intake:
    - **Reduced renal excretion** - increased renal tubular reabsorption (genetically predetermined, SLC2A9), chronic kidney disease, thiazide and loop diuretics
    - **Over-production** - tumour lysis syndrome (Myeloproliferative and lymphoproliferative diseases), psoriasis, rare inherited disorders (e.g. Lesch-Nyhan syndrome)
    - **Miscellaneous risk factors:**
        - Osteoarthritis - reduction in levels of proteoglycans and tissue inhibitors of crystal formation
        - Metabolic syndrome

  
  ![](img/gout_pathophysiology.png)
- **Clinical spectrum of gout:**
    - Asymptomatic hyperuricaemia - much of individuals w/ hyperuricaemia develop clinical gout
    - Acute gouty arthritis - classical presentation of acute inflammatory monoarthritis +/- systemic illness
    - Intercritical gout - completely asymptomatic between attacks, but attacks become more frequent, and may become polyarticular
    - Chronic tophaceous gout - chronic gouty tophi with persistent joint pain and joint damage
- **Clinical features of gout:**
    - **Acute gouty arthritis** - classical presentation of acute inflammatory monoarthritis, but is self-limiting with complete resolution within 5-14 d:
        - **Acute onset of joint pain:**
            - <u>Site</u> - first MTP joint (50%), ankle, midfoot, knee, small hand joints, wrists, elbow (proximal and axial joints typically spared)
            - <u>Onset</u> - rapid, reaches maximal intensity within 2-6 h (typically wakes up patient in the morning)
            - <u>Character</u> - persistent, inflammatory pain
            - <u>Associated features</u> - signs of marked synovitis (erythema, swelling, heat, tenderness), and systemic illness (fever, malaise, confusion)
            - <u>Severity</u> - extremely severe, worst pain ever, can wake up individuals from sleep, unable to wear socks
        - **Pruritis and desquamation** - typically manifests as the <u>acute attack subsides</u>
    - **Intercritical gout** - most do not have a second attack, but repeated attacks typically occur in years
    - **Chronic tophaceous gout** (60% patients after 10y) - especially after frequent (\< 1y) repeated attacks:
        - **Chronic pain** - due to chronic inflammation over gouti tophi deposition
        - **Deformity and functional impairments** - due to joint damage and gouty tophi deposition
        - **Tophi deposition** - irregular firm nodules in periarticular tissues, helix of ears, tendon, extensor surface of fingers, hands, forearm and elbow, and rarely in the larynx, tongue and heart
- **DDx of gout:**
    - Septic arthritis - must r/o
    - Infective cellulitis
    - Reactive arthritis
- **Ix and Dx of gout:**
    - **Joint aspiration** - demonstration of MSU crystals (-ve birefringent) is diagnostic:
        - Appearance - turbid
        - WBC and differentials - 2000-75000 (90% neutrophils)
    - **Routine bloods** - demonstrate inflammation, etiology and associated conditions:
        - <u>CBC</u> - neutrophilia
        - <u>ESR and CRP</u> - non-specifically elevated
        - <u>RFT</u> - look for chronic renal failure as cause
        - <u>BG, lipid profile</u> - assessment of associated metabolic syndrome
    - **Uric acid** - helpful but non-diagnostic (elevation not diagnostic of gout, normal levels does not r/o gout)
    - Ultrasound - double-contour sign
    - Radiographs:
        - Acute gout - typically normal
        - Chronic tophaceous gout - well-demarcated erosions and soft tissue swellings

1.  Management of gout

    - **Principles of Mx:**
      - Acute attacks - symptomatic relief by anti-inflammatory drugs
      - Intercritical gout - preevnt attacks from occuring by urate-lowering therapy
    - **Mx of acute gout:**
      - **Oral NSAIDs** - **indomethacin** preferred as <u>standard of Tx</u> (efficacy and S/E profile), aspirin or other salicylates avoided
      - **COX-2 inhibitor** - celocoxib as effective as indomethacin
      - **Colchicine:**
        - <u>MOA</u> - Inhibition of microtubule polymerisation in neutrophils +/- leukotriene B4-mediated neutrophil chemotaxis –\> prevent phagocytosis of urate crystals and inflammatory sequelae
        - <u>Dosage</u> - 0.5 mg bid or tid
        - <u>S/E</u> - nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea (narrow therapeutic index)
      - **Steroids** - administered only when diagnostic clear and septic arthritis is excluded
        - <u>RoA</u> - intra-articular injections, oral, intra-muscular, perenteral
      - **Anti-IL agents** - Anakinra, canakinumab
    - **Mx of intercritical gout:**
      - **Principles of Tx:**
        - Indefinent urate lowering agent + prophylactic NSAID or colchicine (prevention of breakthrough attacks) in initial 3-6 mo
        - Correction of predisposing factors:
          - Dietary modification - avoid large amounts of red meat, seafood, offals
          - Reduction of alcohol intake, especially beer
          - Substitution of thiazine diuretics w/ ACEi (mild uricosuric effects)
      - **Treatment target** - aims at serum uric acid (SUA) levels below that at which urate crystals can deposit:
        - British Society of Rheumatology - SUA \< 360 micromol/L
        - European League Against Rheumatism - SUA \< 300 micromol/L
      - **Types of urate-lowering agents:**
        - Xanthine oxidase inhibitors - Allopurinol, Febuxostat
        - Uricosuric agents - probenecid, sulphinpyrazone
        - Uricaase analogs - pegloticase, (rasburicase)
      - **Allopurinol** - 1st line Tx:
        - <u>MOA</u> - Xanthine oxidase inhibitor (inhibits synthesis of uric acid)
        - <u>Dosing</u> - Starting dose 100 mg (50 mg in renal impairments or elderly) with stepwise increase in dosage by 100/50 mg (same as above) until 1) treatment target reached, 2) maximal dose reached (900 mg), or 3) maximally tolerable dose reached
        - <u>C/I</u> - HLAB\*5801 haplotype (**genetic testing required**), pregnancy, nursing mothers, children
        - <u>S/E</u>:
          - Xanthine stones - maintain high urine output +/- urine pH (sodium bicarbonate or potassium citrate tablets)
          - Acute flares - warmed about and told to continue therapy even if attack occurs
          - Allopurinol-related S/E - GI disturbances, Hypersensitivity reaction (HLA-B\*5801), teratogenicity
      - **Febuxostat** (more potent than allopurinol) - indicated if insufficient lowering by allopurinol, allopurinol contraindicated, or poorly tolerated:
        - <u>MOA</u> - Xanthine oxidase inhibitor
        - <u>Dosing</u> - starting dose 80mg
        - <u>S/E</u>:
          - Xanthine stones
          - Acute flares - more likely than allopurnol, requires a longer duration of prophylactic anti-inflammatory drugs (6 mo)
          - Febuxostat related S/E - dLFT (requires monitoring), CVS risk (periodic monitoring of CVS risk factors), nausea, joint pain, rash
      - **Uricosuric agents:**
        - <u>MOA</u> - inhibiion of UAT1 –\> increased renal excretion
        - <u>S/E</u>:
          - Uric stones - avoided by adequate hydration
          - Acute flares
        - <u>C/I</u>:
          - Over-producers
          - Chronic kidney disease
          - Urolithiasis
      - **Pegloticase:**
        - <u>MOA</u> - recombinant uricase conjugated to monomethoxypolyethylene glycol to increase half life
        - <u>Indications</u> - for refractory gout to standard therapy
        - <u>Dosage</u> - IV q2w for up to 6mo (due to tachyphylaxis)
        - <u>S/E</u>:
          - Infusion reactions - treated with anti-histamines or steroids
          - Acute flares\* Bone and joint infections

## Fibromyalgia

- **Definition** - common cause of generalised regional pain and disability in absence of organic pathology
- **Epidemiology:**
    - <u>Prevalence</u> - 2-3% in US and UK (increasing prevalence w/ age)
    - <u>Demographic</u>:
        - **Age** - increasing incidence w/ age (up to 7% of F \> 70y)
        - **Sex** - extreme female preponderance (F:M = 10:1)
- **Risk factors for fibromyalgia** - life events that causes psychosocial distress and altered pain modulation:
    - Marital dysharmony
    - Alcoholism in family
    - Injury or assault
    - Low income
    - Self-reported childhood abuse
- **Pathophysiology of fibromyalgia** - poorly understood, but two abnormalities may be interrelated:
    - **Sleep abnormality** - non-restorative sleep disorder where patients have reduced non-REM sleep that is a/w restorative functions (cf poor sleep in REM and non-REM phases in depression):
        - <u>Evidence</u> - points towards insufficient delta (non-REM) sleep:
            - Patients w/ fibromyalgia demonstrate reduced delta sleep in pattern distinct from that seen w/ depression
            - Normal volunteers with deprivation of delta, but not REM sleep have reproducible S/S of fibromyalgia
        - <u>Proposed mechanisms</u> - viscious cycle:
            - Non-restorative sleep results in poor aerobic fitness, overall reduced activity and S/S of fibromyalgia
            - S/S of fibromyalgi are disruptive to sleep, and thus prevents delta sleep

      
      ![](img/sleep_disturbance_in_fibromyalgia.png)
    - **Abnormal peripheral and central pain modulation** - altered pain percept in characteristic sites is commonly seen in fibromyalgia due to abnormal peripheral and central pain modulation:
        - <u>Abnormal peripheral pain processing</u> - **peripheral hypersensitisation**, and abnormal cord gating of pain (pain amplification by **spinal cord 'wind-up'**)
        - <u>Abnormal central pain processing</u> - as suggested by high substance P, low serotonin in CSF; abnormal perfusion to caudate and thalamus; and abnormal responses in functional MRI
        - <u>Abnormal systemic factors</u> - low basal free cortisol and reduction in evening trough, and altered descending inhibition of HPA axis, and growth-hormone somatomedin axis
- **Clinical features of fibromyalgia:**
    - **Multiple regional pain and tenderness** - widespread pain and tenderness that is often worse in the neck and back:
        - <u>Site</u> - typically neck and back
        - <u>Onset and progression</u> - gradual onset and chronic
        - <u>Quality</u> - diffuse
        - <u>Severity</u> - variable, but characteristically **unresponsive to analgesia and PT**
    - **Marked fatigability and disability** - most prominent in the morning affecting daily living (unable to perform tasks such as chores and shopping)
    - **Other usual Sx related to poor sleep quality:**
        - <u>Mood</u> - low affect, irritability and weepiness
        - <u>Cognitive function</u> - poor concentration, forgetfulness
        - <u>Sleep</u> - broken, non-restorative sleep

  
  ![](img/clinical_features_of_fibromyalgia.png)
- **Signs of fibromyalgia:**
    - <u>Hyperalgesia over classical sites on moderate digital pressure</u>: 
    ![](img/tender_sites_of_fibromyalgia.png)
- **Ix** - to r/o other causes of Sx:
    - **Bloods** - CBC, ESR/CRP, TFT, Ca, ALP, ANA: 
    ![](img/ix_screen_for_fibromyalgia.png)
- **Mx:**
    - **Principles of Mx:**
        - <u>Patient and family education</u> - on nature of disease and relation w/ poor sleep
        - <u>Lifestyle modifications</u> - establishment of sleep hygiene, graded increase aerobic exercise, self-help strategies
        - <u>Pharmacological therapy</u> - anti-depressants, with limited evidence on SNRIs, anticonvulsants (pregabalin, gabapentin) and tramadol

### Septic arthritis

- **Definition** - a <u>rheumatological emergency</u> caused by infection of the synovial fluid and joint, characterised by rapid destruction of joint, with significant morbidity and mortality (10%)
- **Epidemiology:**
    - Incidence - varies w/ population, and more common in extremes of age:
        - General population - 1-2/100000/y
        - Pre-existing joint disease and joint replacement - 30-70/100000/y
- **Risk factors of septic arthritis:**
    - Extremes of age - \< 5y and \> 65y
    - Pre-existing joint disease - RA (principally), crystal-induced arthritis, severe OA, charcot joint or neuropathic joint
    - Prosthetic joints
    - Immunosuppression - DM, drugs, CTD, malignancy
    - Parenteral drug use
    - STIs - predisposition to disseminated gonococal infections
- **Microbiology of septic arthritis** - classification by demographic:
    - <u>Infant</u> (\< 1mo) - birth tract organisms (GBS, E. coli, S. aureus)
    - <u>Children \> 2y</u> - H. influenzae type b
    - <u>Children \> 2y and adults</u> - S. aureus
    - <u>Elderly and chronically ill</u> - G- bacilli
    - Special populations:
        - <u>Sexually active young adults</u> - N. gonorrhoeae (up to 3% of untreated gonorrhoea)
        - <u>IV drug abusers</u> - S. aureus, G- bacilli, pseudomonas aeruginosa
        - <u>Prosthetic joints</u> - S. aureus
        - <u>Spread from skin and soft tissue infections</u> - Streptococcus
        - <u>Foul-smelling/ gas forming</u> - anaerobes
- **Pathophysiology of septic arthritis:**
    - <u>Route of infection</u>:
        - Haematogenous spread (most common) - from skin or upper respiratory tract
        - From contiguous foci - osteomyelitis, skin and soft tissue infections
        - Direct inoculation (rare) - from puncture, or prior joint aspiration
    - <u>Rapid joint destruction</u> - esp. with G+ oragnisms which erode joint cartilage within days
- **Clinical features of septic arthritis** - requires high index-of-suspicion:
    - **Acute (or subacute) monoarthritis:**
        - Site - typically over large, weight bearing joints (e.g. knees and hips)
        - Onset - abrupt onset
        - Character - inflammatory pain with overlying erythema, swelling, and <u>rest pain</u> and <u>pain on movement</u>
    - **Systemic features** - fever, systemically ill, confustion etc.
- **Ix:**
    - **Blood culture** - immediately draw blood for blood culture prior to empirical ABx
    - **Joint aspiration** - pivital for diagnosis:
        - Appearance - Turbid to bload stained
        - WBC and differentials - \> 100,000 with predominantly nutrophils
        - Microbiology - G stain and cultures:
            - Cultures - 90% +ve (only 30% +ve for gonoccocal infections, requiring genital tract cultures)
            - G stain - 50% +ve
    - **CBC and inflammatory markers** - leukocytosis and raised ESR/CRP (but may be absent in elderly or immunocomprimised)
    - **LRFT and routine chemistries** - end-organ damage and baseline prior to initiation of ABx
    - **Serial CRP/ESR** - assessment of Tx responsiveness
- **Mx:** 
![](img/emergency_mx_of_septic_arthritis.png)
    - **IV ABx** - empirical IV ABx commenced after blood culture, and modified pending microbiological results:
        - <u>Regimen</u> - Flucoxacillin (2g q6h) for S. aureus and streptococcal coverage +/- Cefuroxime (1.5g q8h) for G- coverage (elderly, faril, immunocomprimised)
        - <u>Duration</u> - IV ABx for 2 weeks, followed by oral ABx for 4 weeks (no evidence for optimal duration)
    - **Pain relief** - oral or IV analgesics
    - **Aspirate joints:**
        - Serial joint aspiration 1-3x per day to dryness
        - Surgical Mx - repeated joint irrigation and debridement by arthroscopy or open arthrotomy

## Rheumatoid arthritis

- **Definition** - A clinico-pathological entity manifesting as chronic inflammatory, symmetrical, polyarthritis, the most common chronic inflammatory arthritis
- **Epidemiology:**
    - Prevalence - lowest prevalence in black Africans and Chinese, highest in Pima Indians
    - Demographic - female preponderance (F:M = 3:1)
- **Pathophysiology of RA** - genetic, epigenetic and environmental factors implicated:
    - **Genetic predisposition** (30 gene foci) - evident in higher concordance rate in monozygotic (12-15%) comapred with dizygotic twins, and increased frequency in 1st-degree relatives
        - <u>Risk genes related to T cell functions</u> - MHC class II (particularly HLA-DR4), PTPN22, CD40L, CTLA4
    - **Proposed pathogenesis** - triggered by <u>infectious agent</u> in a genetically susceptible host:
        - Proposed precipitating infections - Periodontal and oral pathogens, GI microbiomes, EBV, CMV etc.
        - <u>Synovial infiltration by immune cells</u> - lymphocytes, plasma cells, and dendritic cells infiltrate the synovium after citrulinated proteins are released from jointes through microtraumas:
            - <u>Th1 cells</u> - production of INF-alph, inducing synovial macrophage activation
            - <u>Th17 cells</u> - activate various immune cells (e.g. macrohages and B cells)
            - <u>Plasma cells</u> - production of cytokines and autoantibodies (RF, ACPA)
            - <u>Synovial macrophages</u> - release of pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g. TNF-alpha, IL-1, IL-6, IL-15) that activate synovial fibroblasts
        - <u>Synovial fibroblast activation and pannus formation</u> - synovial fibroblast activated releases MMPs, inflammatory cytokines, including RANKL, which drives osteoclastic bone resorption
        - <u>Pannus formation</u> - formation of granulation tissue that erodes over and under articular cartilage, and surrounding bony structure (subsequently fibrousis or bony ankylosis occurs)

### Clinical features of RA

- **Classical clinical presentation** - inflammatory joint pain, swelling and stiffness affecting small joints of the hands, feet and wrists +/- large joint involvement and extra-articular features:
    - <u>Acute onset</u> - with acute onset of florid polyarthritis, morning stiffness and pitting oedema (more common in older age)
    - <u>Palindromic onset</u> - relapsing and remitting episodes of pain, stiffness and swelling that last for hours to days before diagnosis
- **Signs of rheumatoid arthritis:**
    - <u>Features of symetrical inflammatory polyarthritis</u> - symetrical swelling of MCP and PIP joints (DIP joints typically spared), that are <u>tender</u> on pressure and have stress pain on passive movement (**erythema is unusual and often implies sepsis**)
    - <u>Characteristic deformities of the hands</u> (RA hands) - reflect long-standing, uncontrolled disease:
        - **Ulnar deviation** - Deviation of fingers towards ulnar side at MCP joints
        - **Swan neck deformity** - Hyperextension of PIP joints and flexion of DIP joints
        - **Boutonniere deformity** - Hyperextension of DIP joints and flexion of PIP joints
        - **Z deformity of the thumb** - Hyperflexion of the IP joint with fixed flexion and sublaxation of the MCP joint
        - **Trigger fingers** - rheumatoid nodules over the flexor tendon sheaths
    - <u>Characteristic deformities of the foot</u>:
        - **Cock-up toe deformities** - dorsal sublaxation of MTP joints
        - **Calcaneovalgus** (eversion) - damage to subtalar joint

1.  Extra-articular features

    - **Systemic manifestations** - fever, fatigue, weight loss, anorexia common and may occur throughout disease course
    - **Extra-articular musculoskeletal manifestations** - osteoporosis and muscle wasting as a resut of <u>systemic inflammation</u>
    - **Principles of extra-articular features** - often due to serositis, granuloma and nodule formation, or vasculitis:
      - Timing of manifestation - typically occurs in sero-+ve erosive disease, but occasionally occurs at manifestation (these atypical presentation are more common in M)
    - **Cutaneous manifestations** - Rheumatoid nodules:
      - <u>Pathology</u> - similar to granulomas, with central fibrinoid material surrounded by palisade of mononuclear cells, <u>occurs principally in areas of pressure or friction</u> (extensor surface of forearm, achilles, toes) exclusively in sero-+ve patients
      - <u>Complications</u> - ulcerations, fistulae, sinuses, secondary infection
    - **Vascular manifestation** - small-vessel vasculitis:
      - <u>Clinical manifestation</u> - variable, ranging from benign nail-fold infarcts to wide-wpread cutaneous ulceration and skin necorsis
      - Rarely medium-sized artery involvement (e.g. coronary, renal, mesenteric)
    - **Cardiac involvement** - in 30% of sero-+ve RA patients but rarely symptomatic:
      - <u>Pericardial effusion and constrictive pericarditis</u> - caused by serositis, rarely symptomatic
      - <u>Cardiomyopathy</u> - caused by granulomatous depositions
      - <u>Heart block</u> - caused by granulomatous depositions
      - <u>CAD</u> - caused by the rare coronary vasculitis
      - <u>Aortic regurgitation</u> - caused by granulomatous aortitis
      - <u>ASCVD</u> - generally increased risk due to presence of conventional risk factors, systemic inflammation, and NSAID/ corticosteroid use
    - **Pulmonary involvement:**
      - <u>Pleural effusion</u> - often asymptomatic, but manifests as pleurisy and SOB
      - <u>Interstitial lung disease</u> - often manifests as progressive SOBOE in advanced RA
    - **Neurological involvement:**
      - Nerve entrapement syndromes - caused by hypertrophied synovium or joint sublaxation:
        - Bilateral carpal tunnel syndrome (most common)
        - Ulnar nerve compression - typically at elbow or wrist
        - Lateral popliteal nerve compression - at head of fibula
        - Tarsal tunnel syndrome
      - Diffuse symetrical peripheral neuropathy or mononeuritis multiplex - occurs in patients with rheumatoid vasculitis
      - Cervical cord pathologies - can cause sudden death
    - **Haematological involvement:**
      - <u>Anaemia</u> - often with multifactorial causes:
        - Warm AIHA - presents with haemolytic anaemia
        - Iron-deficiency anaemia - due to NSAID-induced GI bleeding
        - Anaemia of chronic disease - occurs in active disease and often with a/w reactive thrombocytosis
      - <u>Eiosinophilia</u>
      - <u>Generalised or local lymphadenopathy</u> - persistent lymphadenopathy should be biopsied due to increased risk of lymphoma
      - <u>Felty syndrome</u> - splenomegaly + neutropenia
    - **Occular involvement:**
      - Keratoconjunctivitis sicca (secondary Sjogren's) - dry eyes as most common symptom
      - Scleritis and episcleritis
      - Scleromalacia - painless bilateral thinning of the sclera (affected area appears bluish-grey due to underlying choroid)

### Investigations and Diagnostic Evaluation of RA

- **Principles of diagnosis** - clinical diagnosis based on compatible Hx and P/E findings:
    - **Diagnostic criteria** - EULAR's diagnostic criteria for RA (2010) used in diagnostic uncertainty (\>=6 considered to have definite RA):
        - Joint involvement - 0-5
        - Serology - 0-3
        - Duration - 0-1
        - APP - 0-1

    
    ![](img/EULARs_RA_criteria.png)
- **Ix** - typically for diagnostic confirmation and assessment of disease severity:
    - Routine investigations - CBC, LRFT, urinalysis (monitor drug safety)
    - Inflammatory markers (ESR, CRP) - typically elevated but normal results do not exclude diagnosis (can be used as marker of disease severity)
    - +/- US and MRI - demonstrate soft tissue swelling to demonstrate presence of synovitis
    - X-Ray - for assessment of degree of joint damage and clinical features of cervical involvements
- **Assessment of disease activity** - DAS28 score:
    - Joint involvement - number of tender and swollen joints in UL and knees
    - ESR - marker of disease activity
    - Patient subjective analysis on visual analogue scale (0-100)

### Management of RA

- **Treatment modalities in RA:**
    - NSAIDs and analgesics - for pain relief
    - Corticosteroids - initial Mx to rapidly control disease flares and induce remission
    - Disease-modifying anti-rheumatic drugs - prinicipally methotrexate, part of steroid-sparing therapy
    - Biologies - typically reserved for high disease activity non-responsive to adequate trials of traditional DMARDs
    - Orthopaedic Mx

  
  ![](img/RA_principles_of_management.png)
- **Disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs** (DMARDs):
    - **Methotrexate** (MTX) - anchor DMARD in RA:
        - <u>Inidications</u> - indicated for all unless proven not effective or intolerable
        - <u>MOA</u> - folate antagonist:
            - Inhibition of DNA synthesis
            - Inhibition of cell division
        - <u>Dosing and regimen</u> - titrate to minimal dose until symptomatic control or toxicity is limiting:
            - Starting dose of 7.5-10 mg PO weekly
            - Escalation by increments of 2.5 mg every 2-4 weeks
            - Maximal recommended dose of 25 mg per week
        - <u>Timing of benefits</u> - should show benefits within 1-2 mo of Tx although a complete course of 6 mo should be prescribed before concluding that it is ineffective
        - <u>S/E</u>:
            - N/V (typically within 24-48h of administration)
            - Malaise
            - Effects on skin and skin appendages (e.g. rash, stomatitis, alopecia)
            - Hepatotoxicity
            - Pneumonitis (rare but can occur iat anytime during Tx)
        - <u>Caution</u> - warn patients to immediately seek early advice if develop any new respiratory Sx (initiation of high-dose steroids may be required)
        - <u>Monitoring</u> - CBC, LFT
    - **Sulfasalazine** (SSZ) - another commonly prescribed DMARD either alone or w/ methotrexate or other drugs:
        - <u>MOA</u> - unknown
        - <u>Dosing and regimen</u>:
            - Starting dose of 500 mg/d
            - Titrate upwards to maintenance dose of 2-4g/d
        - <u>S/E</u>:
            - GI upset
            - Rash
            - Hepatitis
            - Bone marrow suppression (neutropenia or pancytopenia)
            - Orange discolouration of urine and contact lens
        - <u>Monitoring</u> - CBC, LFT
    - **Leflunomide** - as single agent or in combination w/ other drugs:
        - <u>MOA</u> - inhibition of lymphocyte proliferation and activation
        - <u>Dosing</u> - maintenance dose of 10-20 mg/d
        - <u>Advantages</u> - best tolerated and w/ low marrow toxicity
    - **Hydroxychloroquine** - rarely used as single agent, and causes occular toxicity
    - **Other older DMARDs** - rarely used now because there are other drugs (see above) with better risk-benefit profiles:
        - Gold
        - Penicillamine
        - Cyclosporin A
- **Corticosteroids** - role mainly as induction therapy, or Tx of disease flares in patients who are already established on DMARDs:
    - **Role:**
        - **Induction therapy** - limited evidence in which is better:
            - <u>Short-course high dose steroids</u> - 60 mg daily and tapered over 3mo for DMARDs to take effect
            - <u>Prolonged course low-dose steroids</u> - prednisolone 5-10 mg/d for 6-24 mo
        - **Tx of disease flares on maintenance therapy** - IM steroids often used
        - **Intra-articular steroids** - some role in 'problem joints' where symptoms persist despite otherwise good disease control
    - **Screening for osteoporosis** - considered for patients who are taking \> 7.5 mg/d for \>= 3 mo
- **Biologics** - reserved for those w/ high disease activity despite adequate trial of traditional DMARDs:
    - **Indications** - requires demonstration of 1) high disease activity (DAS28), despite 2) failure of adequate trial of traditional DMARDs:
        - <u>Persistent high disease activity</u> - DAS \> 5.1 in the UK
        - <u>Adequate trial of at least two others DMARDs</u> - one of which must be methotrexate for at least 6 mo
    - **Selection of biologics:**
        - <u>Anti-TNF-alpha</u> - first line biological agent in RA, and are often pro-prescribed w/ methotrexate (even though theoretically works w/ monotherapy)
        - <u>B-cell depleting agent</u> (e.g. rituximab) - typically reserved for those who fail to respond to TNF blockade
        - <u>Anti-CTLA4 inhibitor</u> (Abatacept) - can be considered for biologic-naive patients as a/w good safety profile
        - <u>Anti-IL6</u> (Tocilizumab) - 1st-line in methotrexate intolerant patients as can be used as monotherapy
        - <u>Anti-IL1</u> (Anakinra) - not often used as less effective than other biologics

    
    ![](img/commonly_used_biologics_in_ra.png)
    - **Anti-TNF therapy** - co-prescribed w/ methotrexate:
        - <u>MOA</u> - TNF-alpha blockade
        - <u>S/E</u>:
            - Reactivation of latent TB (requires CXR before prescription)
            - Severe infections
            - Progression of prior mlaignant disease
            - Increased risk of basal cell carcinoma of the skin

## Juvenile idiopathic arthritis

- **Definition** - heterogenous group of inflammatory arthritis in children, with different subtypes identified
- **Subtypes of JIA:**
    - <u>Systemic JIA/ Still's disease</u> (5%) - clinical syndrome w/ fever, rash, arthritis, hepatosplenomegaly and serositis, and is often Auto-Ab -ve
    - <u>Oligoarticular JIA</u> (60%) - most common form of JIA that has a femal preponderance, a/w \<=4 large joint involvement in an asymetrical pattern, w/ association of uveitis and is strongly ANA +ve
    - <u>Polyarticular JIA</u> (20%) - considered an extended form of oligoarthritis and is also ANA +ve
    - <u>Rheumatoid factor +ve</u> - polyarthritis w/ +ve RF
    - <u>Enthesitis-related JIA and psoriatic arhtritis</u> (10%) - considered juvenile forms of ankylosing spondylitis, and is HLA-B27 +ve

    - **Principles of Mx** - same principles as other adult inflammatory disease:
        - <u>Immunosuppressive therapy</u> - corticosteroids and methotrexate are indicated for systemic JIA, oligoarticular or polyarticular JIA
        - <u>Biologics and small molecules</u> - anti-TNF therapy, IL-1 inhibitors and tocilizumab indicated for poor responders
- **Prognosis** - most responsive to therapy and resolve at puberty, but adolescence onset have poorer prognosis:

## Seronegative spondyloarthropathies

- **Definition** - Heterogenous group of inflammatory arthritis that are RF and ACPA -ve, which shows considerable overlap in clinical features and shared immunogenetic association with HLA-B27 (although non-diagnostic)
- **Types of seronegative spondyloarthropathies (SpA):**
    - Ankylosing spondylitis - essentially all evolve into this disease entity defined by presence of radiological sacroiliitis
    - Axial spondyloarthritis
    - Reactive arthritis (including Reiter's Syndrome)
    - Psoriatic arthritis
    - Arthropathy associated with inflammatory bowel disease
- **Clinical features common to seronegative spondyloarthritis:**
    - Peripheral arthritis - assymetrical inflammatory oligoarthritis (LL \> UL)

    - Axial involvement - sacroiliitis and inflammatory spondylitis

    - Marked extra-synovial inflammation - differentiating features from RA:

        - Enthesitis - inflammation of tendons (e.g. Archilles tendinitis, plantar fasciitis)
        - Inflammation of joint capsule, periosteum, cartilage and subchondral bone

    - Extra-articular manifestations:

        - Mucosal inflammation - conjunctivitis, buccal ulceration, urethritis, prostatitis, bowel ulcerations (IBD)
        - Psoriasis - Pustular skin lesions and nail dystrophy
        - Anterior uveitis
        - Aortic root inflammation and fibrosis - aortitis, AR, conduction defects
        - Erythema nodosum - tender red nodules over extensor surfaces (especially skin)

    
    ![](img/erythema_nodosum.png)

        - Absent of extra-articular features of Rheumatoid arthritis (including rheumatoid nodules)

### Ankylosing Spondylitis

- **Definition** - Seronegative spondyloarthropathy characterised by chronic inflammatory arthritis predominantly affecting sacroiliac joints and spine, which can cause ankylosis (bone fusion)
- **Epidemiology:**
    - Prevalence - \< 0.5% population
    - Demographic - onset typically between 20-30y, male preponderance (M:F = 3:1)
    - Burden - 75% of patient remain in employment with good Mx (even if ankylosis occurs, as long as spine is fused in erect position)
- **Pathophysiology** - ill-defined interation between environmental pathogens, host immune system in genetically susceptible individuals:
    - <u>HLA-B27 association</u> - 3 proposed theories:
        - Antigen presentation - B27 presents arthritogenic or enthesitogenic peptide to cytotoxic T cells
        - Protein misfold - causes ER stress, leading to release of inflammatory cytokines
        - Propensity to form homodimers - presentation to leukocytes
- **Clinical features of AS:**
    - **HLA-B27 positive** - present in \> 90% of AS patients (although non-sensitive nor-specific to warrant role in diagnosis)
    - **Axial involvement:**
        - **Low back pain** - inflammatory in nature:
            - Site - lower back, with slow ascension along the spine (ocassionally presents in cervical or thoracic spine)
            - Onset - insiduous onset characteristically in young male
            - Radiation - ocassionally to buttocks or posterior thigh
            - Exacerbating and relieving factors - Exacerbated by rest/ inactivity, relieved by movement
        - **Early morning stiffness** - stiffness of the back after long periods of inactivity
        - **Progressive ankylosis and vertebral fractures** - due to disease progression resulting in ankylosis, ocassionally causing severe thoracic and cervical kyphosis (patient unable to see the sun)
        - **Pleuritic chest pain** - due to costovertebral joint involvement
    - **Peripheral arthritis** (40%) - asymetrical oligoarthritis typically affecting large joints:
        - Sites - Hips, knees, ankles and shoulders
        - Onset - only 10% peripheral arthritis antedate spinal Sx, where in a further 10% (1% of all) manifest as JIA from childhood
    - **Features of extra-synovial inflammation:**
        - Enthesitis - manifests as plantar fasciitis, achilles tendinitis
        - Inflammation over bone - tenderness over bony prominence (e.g. iliac crest and greater trochanter) at insertion of tendons
- **Signs of AS:**
    - Reduced lumbar spine motion in all directions:
        - Modified Schober's Test - maximal flexion
        - Lateral spinal flexion
        - Occipital to Wall and Tragus to Wall
        - Cervical rotation
    - Pain on sacroiliac stressing
- **Ix:**
    - **Imaging**:
        - <u>Radiographs</u> - over hip, lateeral thoracolumbar spine and C-spine
        - <u>MRI</u> - earlier detection of sacroiliitis than X-ray and allow for detection of early axial spondyloarthritis
    - **Blood tests:**
        - <u>Inflammatory markers</u> (ESR, CRP) - elevated in active disease but may be normal
        - <u>Other serologies</u> (e.g. RF, ANCA) - -ve results
        - <u>HLA-B27 testing</u> - helpful but non-diagnostic
- **Radiographic features of Ankylosing spondylitis:**
    - <u>Sacroiliac joints</u> (SI joints) - demonstration of presence of sacroiliiitis +/- ankylosis
        - Grade 2 - irregular joint line and loss of cortical margins (+/- narrowing joint space and sclerosis)
        - Grade 3 - partial fusion of SI joint
        - Grade 4 - complete fusion of SI joint

    
    ![](img/radiological_sacroiliitis.png)
    - <u>Lateral thoracolumbar films</u> - squaring of vertebrae
    - <u>Lumbar spine</u> - demonstration of bone erosion, syndesmophyte formation, and subsequent ankylosis:
        - Bone erosion - generalised osteopenia
        - Syndesmophyte formation - calcification of the outermost fibres of the annulus
        - Ossification of anterior longitudinal ligament +/- facet joint function
        - Bamboo shoot appearence - characterised by the aformentioned features

    ![](img/radiograph_AS_spine_1.png) 
    ![](img/radiograph_AS_spine_2.png)
- **MRI features of early Ankylosing spondylitis** - Short T1 inversion recovery (STIR) sequence (more sensitive)
- **Diagnositic criteria of AS** - Modified New York Criteria:
    - Definite AS - 1 radiological criteria + 1 clinical criteria
    - Radiological criteria - Grade 2 sacroiliitis bilaterally or Grade 3-4 sacroiliitis unilaterally
    - Clinical criteria:
        - Characteristic inflammatory back pain \> 3mo, improved by exercise and not relieved by rest
        - Limitations of lumbar spine movement in sagittal and frontal planes
        - Chest expansion reduced
    - Limitations - radiological features delayed by 6-11 y
- **Diagnostic criteria of Axial Spondyloarthritis** - ASAS classification criteria:
    - Patients w/ characteristic back pain with 1) improved by exercise, 2) not relieved by rest, 3) night pain, and 4) age of onset \< 40y - Diagnosis w/ Sacroillitis on imaging + 1 other clinical feature, or HLA positive with \>=2 clinical features in absence of sacroiliitis
    - Patients w/ peripheral Sx (arthritis, enthesitis, dactylitis) - requires combination with other clinical features

  
  ![](img/axial_SpA_diagnosis.png)
- **Disease activity score** - Bath Ankylosing Spondylitis Disease Activity Index: 
![](img/BASDAI.png)
- **Mx:**
    - **Principles of Mx:**
        - Symptomatic relief (pain and stiffness)
        - Maintain maximal ROM
        - Avoid development of deformities
    - **Approach:**
        - Education
        - Lifestyle modification - exercise (swimming) and appropriate physical activity (limit proloned periods of imobility)
        - Pain relief - NSAIDs
        - Anti-TNF-alpha - considered for poor Sx control on standard therapy

### Reactive arthritis

- **Definition** - previously known as Reiter's disease, is a non-specific inflammatory arthritis that is precipiated by prior bacterial dysnetry or sexually transmitted infections
- **Epidemiology** - disease of young men (16-35y):
    - Prevalence - 1-2% of patients with non-pecific urethritis seen in genitourinary clinic have reactive arthritis
    - Demographic - male preponderance (M:F = 15:1)
- **Clinical features of reactive arthritis:**
    - **Clinical course** - acute onset precipidated by <u>prodromal illness</u>, but recurrent or chronic arthritis in 60% patients, with 10% remaining active 20y after initial presentation
    - **Prodromal disease** - Reiter's disease is precipitated by two types of prodromal illness in young men:
        - <u>Bacterial dysentery</u> (20% of HLA-B27 positive men) - following shigella, salmonella, campylobacter or yersenia infection
        - <u>Sexually transmitted infections</u> - especially chlamydia-related urethritis
    - **Classical triad of features defined by Reiter** - reactive arthritis, non-specific urethritis, and conjunctivitis (most present with arthritis only):
        - <u>Reactive arthritis</u> - acute onset of asymetrical oligoarthritis with particular predisposition to lower limb joints (knees, ankles, midtarsal, MTP)
            - <u>Site</u> - asymetrical inflammatory oligoarthritis affecting lower limb joints, ocassionally manifesting as acute monoarthritis
            - <u>Onset</u> - Acute onset
            - <u>Character</u> - Inflammatory in nature
            - <u>Associated symptoms</u> - considerable systemic disturbances (fever, weight loss), and associated enthesitis (Achilles tendinitis or plantar fasciitisa)
            - <u>Severity</u> - typically self-limiting
        - **Non-specific urethritis** - typically manifests as dysuria, urgency, frequency and suprapubic pain
        - **Conjunctivitis** (50%) - inflammation of the conjunctiva frequently in first attack (less frequently manifest as anterior uveitis)
    - **Other joint manifestations** - Dactylitis (sausage digits), enthesitis, sacroiliitis (typically a late feature)
    - **Extra-articular manifestations:**
        - <u>Circinate balantitis</u> (20-50%) - vesicles along coronal margins of the prepuce and glans
        - <u>Keratoderma blennorhagica</u> (15%) - discrete yellow-bown vesico-papules with desquamating margins coelascing to form crusty plaques, typicallly affects palms and soles
        - <u>Nail dystrophy</u> - indistinguishable to those seen in pustular psoriasis
        - <u>Buccal erosions</u> - painless ulcers forming over the buccal mucosa, tongue, palate and lips
        - <u>Anterior uveitis</u> - medical emergency occuring in 30% of chronic reactive arthritis

    
    ![](img/keratoderma_blennorhagica.png)
    - **Ix:**
        - <u>Joint aspiration</u> - typically to r/o septic arthritis or crystal-induced arthritis
            - CBC and differentials - leukocyte rich with neutrophilia, may contain multinucleated macrophages (Reiter's cells)
        - <u>Inflammatory markers</u> (ESR, CRP) - raised during active disease
        - <u>Autoimmune markers</u> - seronegative (-ve for RF, ACPA, ANA)
        - <u>Microbiology</u> - demonstrate entecedent/ concomitant infections:
            - Genital swab - chlamydia PCR
            - +/- serological test - previous dysentry
    - **Dx** - clinical diagnosis, but joint aspiration to rule out other causes
    - **Mx:**
        - Mx of acute attack - rest, NSAIDs, analgesics +/- intra-articular steroids for severe synovitis +/- ABx (single dose azithromycin or short course of doxycycline) for urethritis
        - Mx of recurrent or chronic arthritis - consider DMARDs for persistent makred symptoms, recurrent arthritis, or severe keratoderma blennorhagica

### Psoriatic arthritis

- **Definition** - A seronegative spondyloarthritis with strong associations with psoriasis
- **Epidemiology:**
    - <u>Prevalence</u> - 7-20% of patients with pre-existing psoriasis (0.6% in general population)
    - <u>Demographic</u> - onset at 25-40y
    - <u>Pattern of clinical presentation</u>:
        - Arthritis on top of pre-existing psoriasis (70%)
        - Arthritis predates psoriasis (20%)
        - Synchronous presentation with arthritis and psoriasis
- **Clinical features of PsA:**
    - **Inflammatory arthritis and enthesitis** - several patterns of joint involvement (most are non-errosive except in arthritis mutilans):
        - <u>Asymetrical inflammatory oligoarthritis</u> (40%) - abrupt onset of synovitis and peri-articular inflammation over the hands and feet, characteristically resulting in dactylitis, and ocassionally involving large joints (with large effusions)
        - <u>Symmetrical polyarthritis</u> (25%) - typically manifests in F resembling RA, but has small and large joint involvement of UL and LL, and absent of nodules, and can be deforming due to tenosynovitis and soft tissue contractures
        - <u>DIP joint arthritis</u> - uncommon pattern (characeterostic in men) affecting DIP joint and periarticular tissues, invariably associated with nail dystrophy
        - <u>Psoriatic spondylitis</u> - typically unilateral or asymmetric sacroiliitis which may resemble AS, can occur in conjunction or alone with peripheral joint involvements
        - <u>Arthritis mutilans</u> (5%) - deformiing arthritis of fingers and toes, with characteristic findings of 'main en lorgnette' where didgits can be telescoped or and pulled back to original length

    ![](img/main_en_lorgnette.png) 
    ![](img/dactylitis_and_dip_arthritis.png)
    - **Nail dystrophy** (85%) - nearly invariable feature of PsA, but only present in 30% of those with uncomplicated psoriasis:
        - Pitting - puctate nail depressions
        - Onycholysis - distal nail plate separated from nail bed
        - Subuncal hyperkeratosis - accumulation of tissue between nail and nail bed
        - Oil drops - circular discolouration of the nail bed
    - **Psoriatic lesions** - widespread, over scalp, natal cleft and umbilicus
    - **Ocular manifestations** - conjunctivitis can occur, where uveitis only occurs to HLA-B27 +ve individuals with sacroiliiitis
- **Ix:**
    - <u>Inflammatory markers</u> (ESR, CRP) - non-specifically elevated or normal even in active disease
    - <u>Imaging</u>:
        - Radiographs - may be normal or show cartilage destruction with JSN (arthritis mutilins), differentiated from RA by:
            - Distribution over joints
            - Proliferative erosions with marked new bone formation
            - Absent periarticular osteoporosis and osteosclerosis
        - Radiograph of SI joint - coarse, asymetrical sacroiliitis with coarse, asymetrical syndesmophyte formation
        - +/- MRI or US - detect synovitis and enthesitis
- **Mx:**
    - NSAIDs - for symptomatic management
    - Traditional DMARDS - for persistent synovitis:
        - Preferred agents - Methotrexate (1st line due to cutaneous efficacy), sulfasalazine, cyclosporin, leflunomide
        - Avoid hydroxychloroquine as it may cause exfoliative skin reactions
    - Anti-TNF alpha - as add on therapy fr patients who respond inadequately to traditional DMARDs
    - Other biologics - IL-12/23 inhibitors (Ustekinumab)

## Connective tissue disease

- **Definition** - Heterogenous group of disorders with overlapping clinical features characterised by dysregulation of immune response and auto-antibody production, often directed at the cell nucleus
- **Spectrum of CTD:**
    - <u>Spectrum based on local vs systemic involvement</u>:
        - Focal involvement - Autoimmune thyroid disease, Pernicious anaemia \< RA, PBC
        - Systemic involvement - SLE (prototype)
    - <u>Spectrum based on pathophysiology</u>:
        - Predominantly fibrosing - Systemic sclerosis
        - Predominantly inflammatory - SLE
- **Concept of CTD classification** - certain disease entity is based on established classification-criteria (typically research-driven), but clinically there is often overlap:
    - Overlap syndrome - Diseases with overlapping features of multiple CTD
    - Undifferentiated CTD (uCTD) - Diseases with various rheumatic manifestations but do not ascribe to a particular classification criteria of existing clinical entities
    - Mixed CTD (MCTD) - Specific entity within overlap syndrome characterised by presence o fanti-U1 RNP, and clinical features typically seen in SLE, SSc, and myositis
- **Common clinical features of CTD:**
    - **Clinical findings:**
        - <u>Systemic inflammation</u> - unexplained low-grade fever
        - <u>Arthritis and arthralgia</u> - polyarthritis
        - <u>Mucocutaneous involvements</u> - photosensitive rash, recurrent oral ulcers, alopecia
        - <u>Vascular involvement</u> - Raynaud's phenomenon, nail fold changes, LL petechiae
        - <u>Serositis</u> - pericardial effusion, pleural effusion etc.
        - <u>Sicca symptoms</u> - excocrine gland involvement
    - **Laboratory findings:**
        - <u>CBC</u> - autoimmune cytopenias
        - <u>Inflammatory markers</u> - elevated ESR (CRP paradoxically low in SLE except in sepsis)
        - <u>LFT</u> - **Reversed A/G ratio** (hyperglobulinmaemia)
        - <u>Autoimmune screen</u> - +ve ANA

### Systemic lupus erythromatosus

- **Definition** - The prototypical connective tissue disorder mediated by direct antibody-mediated cytotoxicity, and more importantly, immune complex (IC) deposition, leading to vasculitis and end-organ damage
- **Epidemiology** - rare disease, with genographical variation and female preponderance:
    - <u>Geographical variation</u>:
        - Prevalence: Caribbean blacks (0.2%) \> Orientals \> Caucasians (0.03%)
        - Prognosis: Caucasians \> Orientals \> Caribbean blacks
    - <u>Demographic</u> - Occurs at any age (peak onset at 20-30y), and extreme female preponderance (F:M = 9:1)
- **Pathophysiology** - unclear, but environmental exposure in a genetically predisposed individual is evident: 
![](img/sle_proposed_pathophysiology.png)
    - **Genetic predisposition** - evident in higher concordance rates in monozygotic twins:
        - <u>Defects in apoptosis</u> - excessive apoptosis and inappropriate release in intracellular antigens (supports fact that lupus flares are caused by <u>factors increasing cell turnover</u>, e.g. UV light exposure, infections, increased oxidative stress)
        - <u>Defects in immune complex clearance</u> - e.g. Inherited mutations in complement components (C1q, C2, C4), and immunoglobulin receptor (Fc-gamma-RIIIb) resulting in reduced opsonisation of immune complexes
        - <u>Defects in immune function</u> - evident in genome-wide association studies (GWAS) to identify common polymorphisms in genes that predispose to SLE, much of which have immune-regulatory functions (T cell activation, B cell signalling etc.)
    - **Inappropriate exposure of intracellular antigens** - results in polyclonal B and T lymphocyte activation and autoantibody formation
    - **Circulating immune complex deposition** - main mechanism of wide-spread vasculitis and tissue damage
- **Triggers of lupus flares** - consider factors that induce apoptosis:
    - <u>UV light exposure</u> - direct induction of keratinocyte apoptosis
    - <u>Chemical agent exposure</u> - e.g. hairdye, eosin, heavy metals
    - <u>Infections</u> - EBV, rhinovirus, HIV-1, adenovirus, salmonella
    - <u>Drugs</u>:
        - Triggering agents - Hydrazine, Procainamide, BB, Penicillamine, Isoniazid, Phenytoin, Chorpromazine, Quinidine, Resrpine
        - Exacerbating agents - Lovastatin, Suplphonamide, Oestrogen

1.  Clinical features of SLE

    - **Broad range of symptoms:**
      - Certain symptoms are prevalent at disease onset - e.g. cutaneous manifestations, arthritis or arthralgia
      - Certain symptoms only manifest late in the disease course - e.g. Lupus nephritis
    - **Constitutional Sx** - certain Sx are associated with disease flares, while others are constant:
      - <u>Fever</u> (50%) - typically occurs in active flares
      - <u>Weight changes and genralised lymphadenopathy</u> - a/w activity of disease
      - <u>Fatigue, malaise, fibromyalgia-like Sx</u> - rather constant and not particularly a/w active inflammatory disease
    - **Arthritis and arthralgia** (90%) - typically presents early at diseaase onset:
      - <u>Migratory polyarthritis</u> - typically symmetrical with RA-like distribution (but non-errosive), a/w pain and early morning stiffness, but clinically apparent synovitis and joint swelling is rare
      - <u>Jaccoud's arthropathy</u> (rare) - deforming arthritis due to tenosinovitis-mediated tendon damage
      - <u>Erosive arthropathy</u> - extremely rare deforming arthritis
      - <u>Osteoporosis</u> - often present as a result on long-term steroid use
      - <u>AVN of femoral head</u> - often iatrogenic
    - **Raynaud's phenomenon** - common clinical feature that may precede other Sx for months to years, and becomes more prevalent later in disease course
    - **Cutaneous features** - attributable to <u>cutaneous lupus</u> or <u>vasculitic phenomenon</u>:
      - **Cutaneous lupus** - 3 distinct types of cutaneous involvement classically <u>precipitated by UV light exposure</u>:
        - <u>Acute malar rash</u> (20%) - characteristic butteryfly facial rash over both malar areas connected by nasal bridges, with sparing of nasolabial folds, which are erythematous, raised, <u>tender</u> and <u>pruritic</u>
        - <u>Subacute cutaneous lupus erythematosus</u> - characteristic annular of psoriaform, migratory and non-scaring
        - <u>Discoid lupus</u> - characteristic hyperkeratosis and follicular plugging, with scarring alopecia if occurs in the scalp
      - **Vasculitic phenomenon** - periungal erythema (dilated capillary loops under nail), livedo reticularis, and other vasculitis
      - **Non-scarring alopecia** - may occur in active disease
      - **Erythema nodosum** - raised nodular papules on palms and soles
    - **Renal involvement:**
      - **Lupus nephritis** - glomerular disease caused by IC deposition within glomerulus:
        - 6 pathological types - 1) Normal, 2) Mesangial, 3) Focal proliferative, 4) Diffuse proliferative, 5) Membranous, 6) Sclerosing
        - Clinical presentation (variable) - Glomerular haematuria, proteinuria, nephrotic syndrome, nephritic syndrome, RPGN, CKD
      - **Tubulointerstitial nephritis** - characterised by tubular dysfunction
    - **Serosal involvemnt** - serositis principally in pleura and pericardium, causing increased vascular permeability:
      - <u>Pleural involvement</u> - pleural effusion
      - <u>Pericardial involvement</u> - pericardial effusion
    - **Cardiovascular involvement:**
      - <u>Pericarditis</u> - most common manifestation
      - <u>Myocarditis and cardiomyopathy</u>
      - <u>Libman-Sacks endocarditis</u> - hypercoagulability (associated APLS) results in deposition of sterile fibrin on heart valves
      - <u>Generalised increased atherosclerotic risks</u> - as a result of systemic inflammation, steroid therapy, and hypercoagulability (increased arterial thrombus formation), and is a main cause of morbidity and mortality
    - **Pulmonary involvement:**
      - <u>Recurrent chest infections</u> - must r/o in lupus patient presenting with SOB
      - <u>Parenchymal disease</u> - pneumonitis, Shrinking Lung Disease, and pulmonary fibrosis, in lupus patient presenting in progressive SOB
      - <u>Pulmonary vascular disease</u> - pHTN, PE (especially in APLS)
      - <u>Pleural disease</u> - pleural effusion and pleurisy
    - **Neuropsychiatric involvement:**
      - Unexplained etiology - depression, psychosis, migraneous headaches
      - Explained etiology - cerebral ischaemia, transverse myelitis, chorea, cerebellar ataxia, meningitis
    - **Haematological involvement:**
      - **Cytopenias** - varying degrees:
        - <u>Anaemia</u> - may be ACD or AIHA
        - <u>Leukopenia</u> (lymphopenia or neutropenia) - caused by anti-leukocyte antibody
        - <u>Thrombocytopenia</u> - due to APLS or anti-platelet antibody
      - **Generalised lymphadenopathy and splenomegaly** - occurs in lupus flares
      - **Arterial and venous thrombosis** - a/w vasculitic phenomenon and APLS
    - **GI involvement:**
      - <u>Oral ulcers</u> - common and may or may not be painful
      - <u>Mesenteric ischaemia</u> - serious complication of lupus due to vasculitis

2.  Investigations in SLE

    - **Principles of Ix:**
      - Diagnosis - based on classification criteria which combines clinical and <u>labooratory</u> findings
      - Assessment of severity - certain investigations are markers of disease severity
      - Detection of complications
      - Prognosis
    - **Common Ix employed:**
      - CBC with differentials - autoimmune cytopenias
      - LFT - reversed A/G ratio, +/- unconjugated bilirubinaemia
      - RFT - renal dysfunction
      - Inflammatory markers:
        - ESR - marker of disease activity
        - CRP - not elevated in lupus flares, but raised levels can be seen in serositis, and more importantly, infections
      - Immunological screens:
        - Rheumatoid factor (RF) - r/o RA and Sjogren's syndrome
        - Antinuclear antibodies (ANA) - sensitive, but non-specific test (ANA negative lupus is extremely rare but can occur in anti-Ro +ve lupus)
        - +/- Other immune markers - Anti-dsDNA, Anti-ENA, Anti-phospholipid Ab
      - Complements (C3/4) - negatively correlates with disease activity
      - Specific organ Ix - CXR, ECG, renal biopsy
    - **Typical findings in lupus flares:**
      - Routine bloods - raised ESR, leucopenia and lymphopenia characteristiaclly, +/- anaemia, thrombocytopenia
      - Immune markers - raised Anti-dsDNA and reduced C3/4
    - **Antinuclear antibodies:**
      - <u>Definition</u> - group of antibodies that react with various nuclear antigens
      - <u>Test properties</u> - extremely sensitive but non-specific (low FPR but non-diagnostic)
      - <u>Types</u>:
        - Anti-dsDNA
        - Anti-histone
        - Anti-ssDNA
        - Anti-ENA - Anti-Sm, Anti-RNP, Anti-Ro, Anti-La, Anti-P
    - **Role of ANA subtypes** - diagnostic (part of classification criteria) and prognostic:
      - **Anti-DsDNA** (60-70%) - specific and diagnostic, correlating with disease activity
      - **Anti-ENA Ab** - non-diagnostic, but offers prognostic values:
        - <u>Anti-Sm</u> (10-20%) - diagnostic and specific
        - <u>Anti-Ro</u> (60%) - associated with cutaneous manifestations and Sicca symptoms
        - <u>Anti-La</u> (40%)
        - <u>Anti-RNP</u> - associated with SSc and low-grade myositis

3.  Diagnosis of SLE

    - **Classification criteria** - several classification have be developed for SLE as a means for patient inclusion and categorization in <u>research studies</u>, but limited by <u>imperfect sensitivity and specificity for diagnosis</u>:
      - <u>2019 EULAR/ACR criteria</u> - latest classification criteria with improved performance in detecting new-onset or early SLE, requiring entry requirement of +ve ANA test, and weighted scoring based on 7 clinical and 3 immunological categories
      - <u>2012 SLICC criteria</u> - addressing weakness of 1997 ACR criteria, particularly because it focuses on multi-system involvement
      - <u>1997 American Rheumatism Association (now ACR) criteria</u> - prototypical classification criteria by presence of 4 out of 11 features either serially or simutaneously

4.  Management of SLE

    - **Principles of Mx:**
      - <u>General measures for all patient</u> - patient education, preventive care, and assessment of comorbidities
      - <u>Hydroxychloroquine for all patients</u>
      - <u>Escalation of Tx based in disease severity and involvement</u>
    - **General measures for all patients**:
      - Patient education
      - Preventive care:
        - Photoprotection - avoid sun exposure, UV light exposure and employ sunblock (SPF 25-50)
        - Smoking cessation - reduce CVS risks and increased efficacy of hydroxychloroquine (controversial evidence)
        - Immunisation
      - Screening and Mx of CVS risk factors - aim to reduce CVS risk factors (HTN, dyslipidaemia)
      - Screening for osteoporosis
      - Screening for malignancies
    - **Mx of mild-to-moderate disease** - diseases restricted to skin and joints:
      - Common regimens - Anagesics, NSAID, and hydroxychloroquine (200-400 mg) +/- low-dose steroids in combination w/ steroid sparing therapy (Azathioprine, methotrexate, MMF, belimumab)
    - **Mx of life-threatening disease** - treatment of renal, CNS, cardiac involvement:
      - Induction therapy (2-3 weekly intervals for 6 cycles) - High-dose pulse methylprednispolone (IV 10mg/kg/d) and steroid sparing agents (cyclophosphamide or MMF if isolated renal involvements)
    - **Maintenance therapy** - following acute flare:
      - Graded reduction of glucocorticoids - 40-60 mg/d oral prednisolone on cessation of pulse therapy tapered to \< 10-15 mg/d within 3 mo
      - Steroid-sparing therapy - AZA (2-2.5 mg/kg/d), methotrexate (10-25 mg/w), MMF (2-3g/d)

### Systemic sclerosis

- **Definition** - a generalised connective tissue disease characterised by sclerosis of the skin, viscera, and vasculature, characterised invariably by Raynaud's, sclerodactyly and digital ischaemia
- **Epidemiology:**
    - Prevalence - 10-20/100000
    - Demographic - occurs at any age (peak onset at 40-50y), female preponderance (F:M = 4:1)
- **Subclassification of systemic sclerosis:**
    - LCSS (70% cases) - cutaneous involvement distal to elbow or knee (apart from face) associated with better prognosis, and a/w CREST syndrome
    - DCSS - cutaneous involvement proximal to elbow and knee with trunk involvement a/w poor prognosis (70% 5y survival)
- **Clinical features of systemic sclerosis:**
    - **Raynaud's phenomenon** - universal feature preceding other features for years caused by small vessel vasculitis in extremities, and can manifest as <u>critcal limb ischaemia</u>
    - **Skin manifestations** - initial involvement in extremities and faces, where some develop diffuse diseases:
        - <u>Progressive sclerosis of distal extremities</u> - initially as 1) non-pitting oedema of the flexor tendon sheath and fingers, which subsequently progresses as 2) shiny and taut skin and loss of skin creases (results in **hand deformity**)
        - <u>Fibrosis in the head and neck</u> - thinning of lips and radial furrowing (radial grooves periorally)
    - **Musculoskeletal involvement:**
        - Non-specific arthralgia, morning stiffness and flexor tenosynovitis - common feature
        - Reduced functional capacity of the hands - typically as a result of skin rather than erosive joint diseases
        - Muscle weakness - low-grade myositis
    - **Gastrointestinal involvement:**
        - <u>Esophageal involvement</u> - manifest as GERD, achalasia (dysphagia and odynophagia)
        - <u>Gastric involvement</u> - manifest as early satiety, GOO, or antral vascular ectasia/ water melon stomach (recurrent UGIB in 20% SSc patients)
        - <u>SB involvement</u> - manifests as malabsorption or intermittent bloating, pain and constipation
    - **Pulmonary involvement** - major cause of morbidity and mortality:
        - <u>Pulmonary HTN</u> - 6x more common LCSS than DCSS, presenting as 1) rapidly progressive **dyspnoea** (more rapid than in ILD), 2) **RHF**, 3) **Angina**, 4) in association with **severe digital ischaemia**
        - <u>Interstitial lung disease</u> (fibrosing alveolitis) - mainly affects DCSS who have topoisomerase 1 Ab
    - **Renal involvement** - main cause of mortality:
        - <u>Malignant HTN and renal failure</u> - as a result of hypertensive renal crisis, especially in DCSS who have topoisomerase 1 Ab
- **Ix and diagnostic evaluation** - a clinical diagnosis:
    - Inflammatory markers:
        - ESR - typically raised
        - CRP - typically normal unless extensive organ involvement or co-existing infections
    - LFT - reversed A/G ratio
    - Autoimmune screen:
        - ANA - 70% +ve
        - Topoisomerase 1 Ab - 30% +ve in DCSS
        - Anti-centromere Ab - 60% +ve in LCSS
- **Mx** - no specific treatments to halt or reverse fibrotic changes, hence Mx is mainly targeted to ameliriorate effects of diseased organs:
    - Raynaud's syndrome - keep warm with heated mittens
    - GERD - PPI and anti-reflux agents
    - HTN - aggressive Tx with ACEi even in renal impairment
    - Joint involvement - NSAID or other analgesics
    - Pulmonary HTN - bosentan\*\* Mixed connective tissue disease

### Mixed connective tissue disease

- **Definition** - CTD with features of SLE, systemic sclerosis, and myositis
- **Clinical features of MCTD:**
    - SLE component - symetrical non-erosive polyarthritis with synovitis over the hands
    - SSc component - Raynaud's phenomenon
    - Myositis component - muscle pain and weakness
- **Ix:**
    - Autoimmune markers - Anti-RNP +ve in most patients (but can be present in isolated SLE)
- **Mx** - manage individual components of the syndrome

### Sjogren's syndrome

- **Definition** - autoimmune disorder characterised by lymphocytic infiltration of the salivary and lacrimal glands, leading to glandular fibrosis and <u>exocrine failure</u>
- **Epidemiology:**
    - <u>Demographic</u> - peak onset in 4-6th decade of life, female preponderance (F:M = 9:1)
- **Types of Sjogren's syndrome:**
    - <u>Primary Sjogren's syndrome</u> - Sjogren's occuring in isolation in the absence of other connective tissue disease
    - <u>Secondary Sjogren's syndrome</u> - Sjogren's occuring with other autoimmune disease, particularly SLE
- **Associated conditions** - occuring with other autoimmune disease in Secondary Sjogren's syndrome:
    - Systemic lupus erythematosus
    - Progressive systemic sclerosis (DSSc)
    - Autoimmune liver disease - autoimmune hepatitis, primary biliary cirrhosis
    - Myasthenia gravis
    - Lymphoma (<u>40x risk</u>)
- **Clinical features of Sjogren's syndrome:**
    - **Sicca symptoms** - collection of Sx explained by lymphocytic infiltration of exocrine glands, resulting in <u>progressive exocrien failure</u>:
        - <u>Keratoconjunctivitis sicca</u> - dry eyes due to insufficient lubricating tears, often complicating with **conjunctivitis**, **blepharitis**, and **filamentary keratitis**
        - <u>Xerostomia</u> - dry mouth due to insufficient salivation, such that patient often requires a sip of water to swallow food
        - <u>Salivary gland enlargement</u> - firm enlargement of the parotid, submandibular and sublingual glands bilaterally due to **glandular fibrosis**, with high incidence of **dental carries**
        - <u>Vaginal dryness</u> - rarer manifestation but can be distressing
    - **Extraglandular manifestations:**
        - <u>Low-grade fever</u> - due to underlying inflammation
        - <u>Fatigue</u> - may be relenting and non-responsive to Tx, often related to non-restorative sleep often because of xrostomia
        - <u>Interstitial lung disease</u> - connective tissue disease w/ strongest correlation to ILD, although not the most common
        - <u>Renal involvement</u> (extremely common) - typically manifests as **tubulointerstitial disease** (as distal RTA), and rarely **glomerulonephritis** (a/w cryoglobulinaemia)
        - <u>Vasculitis</u> - associated vasculitic phenomenon, which results in cutaneous manifestations (purpuric rash) or peripheral neuropathy
        - <u>Cytopenias</u> - anaemia, leucopenia, thrombocytopenia that may be immune-related
        - <u>Lymphadenopathy and lymphoreticular lymphoma</u> - 40x risk of lymphoma compared w/ general population

  
  ![](img/features_of_sjogrens.png)
- **Ix:**
    - **Routine bloods** - CBC, LRFT, Ig pattern, ESR, autoimmune pannel:
        - <u>CBC</u> - cytopenia (typically immune mediated)
        - <u>LFT</u> - reversed A:G ratio
        - <u>RFT</u> - derranged RFT in GN, while characteristic hypokalaemic acidosis in distal RTA
        - <u>Ig</u> - hypergammaglobulinaemia
        - <u>ESR</u> - non-specifically elevated
        - <u>Autoimmune pannel</u> - +ve of more than 1 AutoAb (ANA, RF, Anti-Ro, Anti-La, thyroid, gastric parietal cells)
    - **Schirmer tear test** - measures tear flow over 5 min using absorbent paper strips placed in lower eyelid (N \> 6mm)
    - **Rose Bengal staining** - visible punctate epithelial abnormalities
    - **Lip Bx** - when Dx is doubtful:
        - <u>Diagnostic findings</u> - lymphocytic infiltration in minor salivary glands
- **Mx:**
    - **Principles of Mx:**
        - <u>Symptomatic relief</u> - symptomatic relief for sicca symptoms
        - <u>No role of immunosuppression</u> - does not improve sicca Sx and no Tx has known disease-modifying effect
        - <u>Tx of complications</u> - e.g. RTA and GN
        - <u>Screening for lymphoma</u> - Bx when presenting w/ lymphadenopathy and salivary gland enlargement to r/o lymphoma
    - **Symptomatic Tx:**
        - <u>Keraconjunctivitis sicca</u> - hypermellose during the day with general eye care to avoid opthalmic complications
        - <u>Xerostomia</u> - artificial saliva, gels are often not effective, while chewing sugar-free gums may help
        - <u>Vaginal dryness</u> - treat w/ lubricants (e.g. KY jelly)

### Polymyositis and dermatomyositis

- **Definition** - two related disorders that are characterised by an inflammator process affecting skeletal muscles:
    - <u>Dermatomyositis</u> - inflammatory process of the skeletal muscles and exposed areas of the skin
    - <u>Polymyositis</u> - inflammatory process isolated to the skeletal muscles
- **Epidemiology:**
    - <u>Incidence</u> - 2-10/1,000,000/y (both conditions are rare)
    - <u>Demographic</u> - presentation in 4-6th decade of life
- **Associated conditions:**
    - May present in association w/ other autoimmune diseases, such as SLE, SSc and Sjogren's syndrome
    - Associated w/ 3x increased risk of malignancy that persists for at least 5y after Dx
- **Clinical features of dermatomyositis and polymyositis:**
    - **Muscle weakness** - due to inflammatory destruction of muscles, usually resulting in widespread <u>symmetrical proximal muscle weakness</u>:
        - <u>Site</u>:
            - Symmetrical, proximal muscle weakness, lower limbs classically affected more than upper limbs (focal disease may occur)
            - Respiratory and pharyngeal muscles (see below)
        - <u>Onset and progression</u> - subacute, gradually over few weeks
        - <u>Function limitations</u>:
            - **LL weakness** - difficulty rising from a chair, climbing stairs
            - **UL weakness** - lifting, and combing hair
        - <u>Association</u> - a/w muscle pain
        - <u>Timing</u> - progressive, no diurnal variation
    - **Respiratory muscle and pharyngeal muscle weakness:**
        - <u>Type 2 respiratory failure</u> - due to insufficient power for ventilation, may require intubation and ventilatory support
        - <u>Aspiration</u> - risk of aspiration pneumonia increased due to pharynegal muscle weakness
        - <u>Dysphagia</u> - oropharyngeal dysphagia reflecting pharyngeal muscle weakness
        - <u>Dysarthria</u> - indistict, poor articulation due to weakness of pharyngeal muscles, or facial muscles affecting phonation
    - **Characteristic skin lesions** - characteristic rash in sun-exposed areas only in dermatomyositis:
        - <u>Gottron's papules</u> - scaly, erythematous or violaceous, psoriaform plaques over **extensor surface of PIP and DIP joints**
        - <u>Heliotrope rash</u> - violaceous discolouration of the **eyelid** in combination with **periorbital oedema**
        - <u>Shawl sign</u> - characteristic rash over neck, upper back, chest and shoulders in the distribtuion of shawl caped over
        - <u>Periungal nail-fold capillaries</u> - often visibly enlarged, and tortuous

    
    ![](img/skin_signs_of_dermatomyositis.png)
    - **Systemic manifestations:**
        - <u>Fatigue</u> - may or may not be attributed to muscle weakness
        - <u>Fever</u> - usually low-grade fever present
        - <u>Weight loss</u> - due to an active inflammatory process
    - **Chronic exertional dyspnoea** - relentless progressive shortness of breath caused by interstitial lung disease (30% of patients), with strong associations w/ Anti-Jo1 Ab (antisynthetase)
- **Ix:**
    - **Bloods** - CK:
        - <u>CK</u> - elevated (but normal levels do not exclude Dx), and correlates to disease activity
    - **EMG** - confirms presence of myopathy and effectively r/o neuropathy
    - **Myositis immune panel**
    - **Muscle Bx** - pivotal for Dx:
        - <u>Approach</u>:
            - Blind (common)
            - MRI-guided (considered if initial Bx is normal due to patchy myositis)
        - <u>Findings</u> - fibres necrosis, regeneration and inflammatory cell inflammation
    - **Malignancy screen** - undertaken routinely:
        - CT T+A+P (assessment of ILD as well)
        - OGD + colonoscopy
        - Mammography
- **Mx:**
    - **Principles of Mx:**
        - <u>Remission induction</u> - high dose oral steroids or IV steroids (+/- IVIG)
        - <u>Maintenance therapy</u> - taper steroid to maintenance dose +/- steroid-sparing therapy (Azathioprine, methotrexate)
        - <u>Malignancy screen</u> - see above
        - <u>Monitoring</u> - for complications (e.g. malignancy, interstitial lung disease) and relapse

### Takayasu's disease

- **Definition** - large vessel vasculitis predominantly affecting the aorta and its major branches, and ocassionally the pulmonary arteries
- **Epidemiology:**
    - <u>Incidence</u> - worldwide distribution but most common in Asia
    - <u>Demographic</u>:
        - Age - peak onset at 25-30y
        - Sex - female preponderance (F:M = 8:1)
- **Pathophysiology of Takayasu disease:**
    - **Distribution of vasculitis** - predominantly affects the <u>aorta and its major branches</u> (carotid, ulnar, brachiaal, radial and axillary arteries), ocassionally the <u>pulmonary arteries</u>, classified into:
        - <u>Type 1 Takayasu disease</u> - localised to the aorta and its branches
        - <u>Type 2 Takayasu disease</u> - localised to the descending thoracic and abdominal aorta
        - <u>Type 3 Takayasu disease</u> - combined features of type 1 and type 2
        - <u>Type 4 Takayasu disease</u> - involvement of pulmonary artery
    - **Granulomatous inflammation of the vessel wall** - leads to:
        - <u>Vessel occlusion</u> - results in coarctation or occlusion on angiography
        - <u>Weakening of the vessel wall</u> - results in aortic incompetence and aneurysmal dilatation
- **Clinical features of Takayasu disease:**
    - <u>Non-specific Sx</u> - fever, arthralgia and weight loss
    - <u>Claudication</u> - UL claudication as as a result of vessel occlusion
- **Signs of Takayasu disease** - examination of the arterial system:
    - **Examination of the arterial system:**
        - <u>Pulses</u> - weak or absent
        - <u>Auscultation</u> - bruits present
    - **Cardiovascular examination** - <u>early systolic murmur best heard at LLSB</u> suggestive of AR
    - **BP measurement** - reveals HTN
- **Ix:**
    - **Routine bloods** - CBC, CRP/ESR:
        - <u>CBC</u> - NcNc anaemia
        - <u>CRP/ESR</u> - non-specifically elevated
    - **Angiography** - diagnostic and distribution of types:
        - Coarctation
        - Vessel occlusion
        - Aneurysmal dilatation
- **Mx:**
    - **Principles of Mx:**
        - <u>Immunosuppression</u> - high dose steroids and immunosuppressants
- **Prognosis** - 5y survival up to 83% with appropriate Mx

### Kawasaki disease

- **Definition** - vasculitis of the coronary vessels, presenting as an acute systemic disorder affecting children \< 5y
- **Epidemiology** - most common in Japan or other Asian countries (e.g. China, Korea)
- **Clinical features of Kawaski disease** - clinically resembles a viral exanthem:
    - Fever
    - Generalised rash of the palms and soles
    - Inflamed oral mucosa and conjunctival injections
    - Cardiovascular complications caused by coronary arteritis, MI, myocarditis, pericarditis, acute limb ischaemia etc.
- **Mx:**
    - ASA 5mg/kg/d for 14d
    - IVIG 400 mg/kg/d for 4d

### Polyarteritis nodosa

- **Definition** - vasculitis of medium sized arteries w/ multisystem involvement
- **Epidemiology:**
    - <u>Incidence</u> - 2/1000000/y
    - <u>Demographic</u>:
        - Age - peak incidence between 40-50y
        - Sex - male preponderance (M:F = 2:1)
        - Other risk factors - HBV is an important risk factor (up to 10x risk)
- **Clinical features of PAN** - highly variable as can affect host of medium-vessel artieries:
    - **Non-specific Sx** - fever, weight loss, myalgia, arthralgia
    - **Cutaneous manifestations** - most commonly present w/ palable purpura, ulcerations, infarcts, and livedo reticularis
    - **Neurological manifestations** - <u>symetrical sensory and motor neuropathy</u> caused by involvement of the vasa nervorum
    - **Renal impairment and severe HTN** - likely caused by <u>multifocal acute renal infarction</u> (GN is rare in PAN)
- **Ix:**
    - **Angiography** - potentially diagnostic:
        - <u>Multiple aneurysms</u> and <u>smooth narrowing</u> of mesenteric, hepatic, or renal artery
    - **Muscle or sural nerve Bx** - histologically reveals:
        - Necrotising inflammation
        - Vessel occlusion
- **Mx** - high-dose steroids and immunosuppressants

### Giant cell arteritis and polymyalgia rheumatica

- **Terminology** - GCA and PMR are considered the same disease because <u>most patients presenting with GCA have PMR Sx</u>, and <u>most untreated PMR will develop GCA</u>:
    - **GCA** - a granulomatous arteritis that predominantly affects medium-sized arteries of the head and neck (e.g. temporal artery)
    - **PMR** - Symmetrical muscle pain and stiffness affecting shoulder and pelvic girdles
- **Epidemiology:**
    - Prevalence - 10-20/100000 in those \> 50y
    - Incidence - rare \< 60y, female preponderance (F:M = 3:1)
- **Clinical features of GCA and PMR:**
    - **Constitutional Sx** - fever, weight loss, malaise, night sweats
    - **Clinical features of GCA:**
        - <u>Headaches</u> - cardinal Sx of GCA and is often localised to the temporal or occipital region, often a/w scalp tenderness
        - <u>Ischaemic jaw pain</u> - jaw pain on chewing or talking caused by ischaemia of the masseter muscles
        - <u>Visual manifestations</u> - arteritic anterior ischaemic optic neuropathy (AAION) caused by occlusion of the posterior ciliary artery
            - Clinical presentation variable - Sudden mono-nuclear vision loss (classical), loss of visual acuity, reduced clour perception, papillary defects
            - Fundoscopic findings - pale, swollen optic disc with haemorrhage
    - **Clinical features of GCA PMR** - symmetrical muscle pain and stiffness affecting shoulder and pelvic girdles, but absent of weakness and muscle wasting:
        - <u>Onset</u> - typically sudden over a few days, or insiduous
        - <u>ROM</u> - stiffness and painful restriction of active ROM, but passive ROM preserved
- **DDx of PMR** - consider the age of onset:
    - Rheumatic diseases:
        - Inflammatory myopathy - Pm, Dm, Inclusion-Body myositis
        - Systemic vasculitis
        - Rheumatoid vasculitis
    - Fibromyalgia
    - Hypothyroidism
    - Spine pathologies - cervical spondylosis
- **Ix:**
    - ESR - extremely elevated (\> 100)
    - Temporal artery Bx - findings of fragmentation of internal lamina, with necrosis of the media with mixed inflammatory cell infitrates
- **Mx:**
    - **Induction therapy** - urgent high dose corticosteroids for 3-4d to prevent visual loss:
        - <u>Regimen</u> - 60-80 mg/d for GCA, 15-30 mg/d for PMR alone (evidence for different dosage is weak)
    - **Graded dose reduction of steroids:**
        - <u>Initial target</u> - \< 10-15 mg/d within 8 weeks (titrated against symptoms and ESR)
        - <u>Subsequent target</u> - slower dose reduction at 1 mg/mo until acceptable maintenance dose is required (5-7.5 mg/d)
    - **Maintenance therapy** - low-dose steroid +/- steroid sparing agents (e.g. azathioprine, methotrexate)
    - **Mx of symptom recurrence** - increase steroid dosage to documented dosage that could control Sx

### Antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody-associated vasculitis

- **Definition** - Life-threatening systemic <u>small-vessel vasculitis</u> characterised by inflammatory iniltrates and fibrinoid necrosis of small vessels, a/w presence of circulating <u>ANCA</u>
- **Epidemiology:**
    - Incidence - 10-15/1,000,000/y
- **Subtypes of ANCA-associated vasculitis:**
    - <u>Microscopic polyangiitis</u> (MPA) - necrotising small-vessel vasculitis with renal (RPGN), pulmonary (alveolar haemorrhage), cutaneous, gastrointestinal involvements
    - <u>Granulomatosis with polyangiitis</u> (Wegner's) - vasculitis characterised with granuloma formations affecting nasal passage, airways and kidneys
- **Ix:**
    - <u>CBC</u> - leukocytosis in active disease
    - <u>Inflammatory markers</u> (ESR, CRP) - raised in active disease
    - <u>Immunological markers</u>:
        - ANCA - correlates w/ disease activity
        - Myeloperoxidase Ab (Anti-MPO) - +ve in MPA
        - Proteinase-3 Ab (Anti-PR3) - +ve in WG
    - <u>Complements</u> - normal or slightly elevated in active disease
    - <u>Imaging of upper airway and chest</u> (CT/ MRI) - useful for localising lesions for Bx
    - <u>Kidney or upper airway lesion Bx</u> - typically required for diagnosis

### Churg-Strauss syndrome

- **Definition** - small vessel vasculitis a/w eosinophilia and asthma with multisystem involvement
- **Epidemiology:**
    - <u>Incidence</u> - 1-3/1000000/y
- **Clinical features of Churg-Strauss syndrome** - acute presentation classically with triad of 1) skin lesions, 2) assymetrial mononeuritis multiplex, and 3) eosinophilia:
    - **Prodromal period** - may last for many years characterised by:
        - Allergic rhinitis
        - Recurrent nasal polyps
        - Poorly-controlled late-onset asthma
    - **Small airway and pulmonary disease** - occurs prior to and during active vasculitic phase of disease:
        - <u>Asthma</u> - cardinal feature of EGPA, often late-onset asthma that is difficult to control that is present preceding the Dx
        - <u>Pulmonary infiltrates</u> - eosinophilic pulmonary infiltrates may be seen on CXR
        - <u>Pleural effusion</u> - eosinophilic serositis may be present
    - **Upper airway disease** - upper airway involvement is often present prior to Dx, and clinically mimics aspirin-exacerbated respiratory disease (Samter's triad) in absence of eosinophilia:
        - <u>Allergic rhinitis</u> - characterised by nasal congestion and discharge
        - <u>Recurrent nasal polyposis</u> - causing nasal obstruction
    - **Skin lesions** - most common feature of the vasculitic phase:
        - <u>Palpable purpura</u> - as a result of a vasculitic phenomenon
        - <u>Nodules</u> - tender subcutaneous nodules on extensor surfaces of extremities reflecting eosinophilic granulomas
    - **Asymmetrical mononeuritis multiplex** (75%) - sensory and motor peripheral neuropathy at presentation which may progress into a polyneuropathy if left untreated
    - **Mesenteric vasculitis** (50%) - Abdominal Sx may be present suggesting mesenteric vasculitis
- **Ix:**
    - **Routine bloods** - CBC, ESR/CRP:
        - <u>CBC</u> - eosinophilia during active disease
        - <u>ESR/CRP</u> - non-specifically raised
    - **Autoantibodies** - may have +ve MPO or PR3 but should be considered a <u>distinct entity from ANCA-associated vascuitides</u>
    - **Bx of affected site:**
        - Evidence of small-vessel vasculitides
        - Eosinophilic infiltration of the vessel wall
- **Mx:**
    - <u>Induction therapy</u> - high-dose steroid + cyclophosphamide
    - <u>Maintenance therapy</u> - low-dose steroid and azathioprine, methotrexate or MMF

### Osteoporosis

- **Definition** - mineral bone disease characterised by <u>reduced bone density</u>, resulting in <u>micro-architectural deterioration of bone tissue</u>, and leads to an increased risk of fracture
- **Epidemiology:**
    - <u>Prevalence</u> - osteoporotic related fractures affect around 30% of F and 12% of M in developed countries
    - <u>Demographic</u>:
        - **Age** - increasing incidence of osteoporosis and osteoporotic related fractures w/ age ![](img/changes_in_bone_mineral_density_with_age.png) 
        ![](img/risk_of_osteoporotic_related_fractures_with_age.png)
        - **Sex** - F affected more than M due to rapid decline of bone mineral density
- **Etiology of osteoporosis** - primary vs secondary osteoporosis:
    - **Primary osteoporosis** - age-related osteoporosis, post-menopausal osteoporosis:
        - <u>Age-related osteoporosis</u> - poor peak bone mass and accelerated bone loss until BMD reaches an osteoporotic range, often attributed to both genetic and environmental causes
        - <u>Post-menopausal osteoporosis</u> - accelerated bone loss is caused by estrogen deficiency resulting in uncoupling of bone formation and bone resorption (early menopause is an important risk factor)
    - **Secondary osteoporosis:**
        - <u>Endocrine diseases</u> - hypogonadism, hyperPTH, hyperthyroidism, Cushing's syndrome
        - <u>Drug-induced osteoporosis</u>:
            - **Hormonal replacement** - corticosteroids, thyroxine over-replacement, GnRH agonist
            - **DM medications** - thiazolidinediones (through inhibition of osteoblastic differentiation)
            - **Estrogen-related Tx** - e.g. aromatase inhibitors
        - <u>Inflammatory disease</u> - RA, IBD, Ankylosing spondylitis
        - <u>Gastrointestinal disease</u> - malabsorption (pancreas, SB disorders), chronic liver disease
        - <u>Pulmonary disease</u> - chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, cystic fibrosis
        - <u>Miscellaneous disorders</u>:
            - **Myeloma** - due to RANKL release resulting in increased osteoclastic activity
            - **Anorexia nervosa** - due to malnutrition resulting in Ca deficiency, low body weight, and hypogonadism
            - **Highly trained athletes** - may be caused by hypogonadism
            - **HIV infection** - due to low body weight, chronic immune activation, and antiretroviral therapy
            - **Gaucher's disease and systemic mastocytosis** - caused by release of bone resorption factors

    
    ![](img/secondary_causes_of_osteoporosis.png)
- **Risk factors for primary osteoporosis** - genetic and environmental factors underlie <u>failure of attaining peak bone mass</u> and <u>accelerated bone loss</u>:
    - **Genetic factors** - accounts up to 80% population variance in peak bone mass and other factors increasing risk of osteoporosis, mostly in the RANK and Wnt signalling pathways
    - **Environmental factors:**
        - <u>Factors affecting peak bone mass</u> - Ca intake and exercise during growth and adolescence not only increases peak bone mass, but shown to regulate post-menopausal bone loss
        - <u>Factors affecting bone loss</u>:
            - **Exposure** - e.g. smoking, excessive alcohol intake
            - **Menstrual Hx** - e.g. early menopause a/w increased bone loss due to uncoupling of bone formation and bone resorption
- **Risk factors for osteoporotic-related fractures** - caused by 1) severity of osteoporosis, and 2) risk of falls:
    - **Factors increasing severity of osteoporosis** - see above
    - **Factors increasing risk of falls:**
        - <u>Drugs</u> - sedatives, hyponotics, anti-convulsants, antihypertensives, insulin or sulphonylurea (hypoglyceamia)
        - <u>Neurologicla disease</u> - stroke, parkisonism etc.
        - <u>Opthalmic disease</u> - visual impairments due to cataracts, AMD, DR etc.
        - <u>Other geriatric issues</u> - sarcopenia, fraility
- **Clinical features of osteoporosis:**
    - **Asymptomatic** - remains asymptomatic until a fracture occurs
    - **Fracture** - classically a progression of fracture from Colle's fracture, to vertebral fracture, to hip fracture:
        - <u>Colle's fracture</u> - often from slip and fall, presenting as local pain, tenderness and deformity after episode of minimal trauma
        - <u>Verterbral fracture</u> - presents as acute LBP (ocassionally radiate to anterior chest or abdominal wall mimicking MI), or chronic pain, a/w kyphosis and gradual height loss
        - <u>Hip fracture</u> - often from slip and fall resulting in shortened and externally rotated leg
- **Approach to assessment of suspected osteoporosis:**
    - <u>Hx and P/E</u> - identification of predisposing causes, secondary osteoporosis, detailed fall Hx and assessment of fall risk
    - <u>Fracture risk assessment tools</u> - assess 10y fracture risk to guide Mx
    - <u>Ix</u> - r/o secondary causes and Dx of osteoporosis
- **Salient points of Hx:**
    - **Predisposing risk factors:**
        - <u>Environmental risk factors</u> - smoking, alcohol
        - <u>Premature menopause</u> - assess menstrual Hx in F
        - <u>Body weight</u> - BMI \< 18
    - **Sx of common causes of secondary osteoporosis:**
        - <u>Thyrotoxic Sx</u> - heat intolerance, sweating, hand tremors, weight loss
        - <u>Cushing's syndrome</u> - aquired proximal myoathy, weight gain, and new-onset/ worsening HTN
        - <u>Myeloma</u> - bone pain
    - **PMH** - identify medical causes of osteoporosis:
        - <u>Endocrine disease</u> - e.g. thyroid disease, Cushing's disease, primary hyperparathyroidism
        - <u>GI disease</u> - IBD, disease of SB and pancreas predisposing to malabsorption
        - <u>Rheumatological disease</u> - RA, ankylosing spondylitis, SLE etc.
        - <u>Previous prolonged glucocorticoid use</u> - glucocorticoid-associated osteoporosis
        - <u>Other drugs</u> - e.g. PPI, thiazolidinediones
    - **Detailed fall Hx** - fall risk can be increased as a result of 1) disease, 2) drugs, or 3) fraility state:
        - <u>Hx of neurological disease</u> - old strokes, parkinsonism, cerebellar disorders
        - <u>Medications predisposing to falls</u> - sedatives and hypnotics, anticonvulsants, beta-blockers (postural hypotension), diuretics (postural hypotension)
        - <u>Faility</u> - dependent on comprehensive geriatric assessment
- **P/E:**
    - <u>Height and weight</u> - for documentation to assess for any gradual height loss suggestive of vertebral fractures
    - <u>Posture</u> - kyphosis
    - <u>Dental examination</u> - a/w increased risk of osteonecrosis of jaw if considered pharmacotherapy
    - <u>General examination</u> - look for signs of:
        - 1\. Thyroid disease
        - 2\. Cushing's syndrome
        - 3\. Neoplasia
        - 4\. Rheumatological disease (e.g. RA)
    - <u>Neurological examination</u> - particularly power of limbs
    - <u>Get-up and go test</u> - considered for all elderly: 
    ![](img/get_up_and_go_test.png)
- **Fracture assessment tools** - e.g. FRAX:
    - <u>Validation</u> - only validated for post-menopausal F or M \> 50y who have not been treated
- **Ix:**
    - **Routine bloods** - CBC, LRFT, TFT, Ig pattern, ESR, CaPO4, Serum 25(OH)D, PTH, sex hormone levels:
        - **Baseline Ix:**
            - <u>CBC</u> - shows NcNc anaemia (suspecting myeloma)
            - <u>LFT</u> - alcoholism
            - <u>RFT</u> - CKD-MBD (and assess whether bisphosphonates C/I)
        - **Endocrine Ix:**
            - <u>TFT</u> - hyperthyroidism
            - <u>Ca, PO4, Serum 25(OH)D and PTH</u> - assessment of primary or seconary hyperparathyroidism (+/- vitamin D deficiency)
            - <u>Sex hormones and gonadotrophin</u> - indicated in M or F \< 50y presenting w/ osteoporosis
    - **Dual energy X-ray absorptiometry** (DEXA) - considered in patients w/clinical risk factors, 10y fracture risk or presentation w/ fragility fractures:
        - <u>T-score</u> - BMD expressed relative to gender and ethnicity-matched young adlt mean, with dose-response correlation w/ fracture risk
        - <u>Interpretation and diagnostic findings</u>:
            - Normal - T-score \>= -1.0
            - Osteopenia - T-score \>= -2.5, but \<= -1.0
            - Osteoporosis - T-score \<= -2.5
            - Severe osteoporosis - T-score \<= 2.5 in the presence of \>= 1 fragility fracture
    - **Additional Ix:**
        - Radiographs, urine or serum protein electrophoresis - for suspected myeloma
        - ALP (for suspected fractures)
        - Prolactin
        - Anti-tissue transglutaminase Ab (coeliac disease)
- **Mx:**
    - **Principles of Mx:**
        - **Tx goals** - reduce risk of fracture and associated morbidities by 1) improving BMD, and 2) reducing fall risk
        - **Modalities of Mx:**
            - <u>Non-pharmacological Tx</u> - dietary and lifestyle modifications for all
            - <u>Pharmacological Tx</u> - antiresorptive agents vs bone-forming agents
            - <u>Surgical Tx</u> - for osteoporotic-related fractures
        - **Monitoring** - monitoring and re-assessment of need for therapy
    - **Non-pharmacological Mx:**
        - **Address risk factors of osteoporosis** - smoking cessation, and moderation of alcohol
        - **Adequate Ca and vitamin D intake:**
            - <u>Calcium intake</u> - 800-1200 mg/d through dairy products +/- calcium suplements (taken w/ food as acid promotes Ca absorption)
                - Common foods with high calcium content - Cheese, Sardines, Bean curd sheets, Almonds yogurt, tofu, milk, broccoli
                - Calcium supplements (taken w/ food as acidity promotes calcium absorption):
                    - Selection - calcium carbonate and calcium citrate
                    - S/E - dyspepsia and constipation
            - <u>Vitamin D</u>:
                - Mean vitamin D requirements - 800-1000 IU/d, up to 2000 IU/d in selected individuals (obese, malabsorption, limited sun exposure)
                - Desired 25OHD levels - 75 nmol/L
                - Adequate sun exposure - around 10-15 minutes of sunlight exposure to exposed areas (arm, hands, face) is considered adequate
        - **Weight-bearing and muscle-strengthening exercises** - reduces fall risk
        - **Multidisciplinary fall prevention teams** - considered for those w/ unsteadiness or recurrent falls
    - **Pharmacological Mx:** 
    ![](img/effects_of_drugs_on_risk_of_osteoporotic_fractures.png)
        - **Indications for pharmacological Mx:**
            - Fragility fractures
            - Osteoporotic range T-score (\<= -2.5)
            - Osteopenic range T-score but high risk of fracture according to FRAX (10y risk of major fracture \>= 20%)
        - **Escalation of pharmacological Mx** - based on fracture risk estimation:
            - **1st-line** - Oral bisphosphonate (Aledronate)
            - **Other Tx:**
                - <u>HRT or SERMs</u> - indicated for young (\< 65y) post-menopausal women with osteopenic range T-score and no prior fractures suffering from climacteric symptoms
                - <u>IV Bisphosphonates</u> (Zoledronic acid) - indicated for those who have esophageal disorders (achalasia, strictures), or cannot tolerate upper GI side effects
                - <u>Denosumab</u> - indicated when all bisphosphonates are C/I (principally due to CKD)
                - <u>Anabolic agents</u> (PTH analogues, Romosozumab) - Very high fracture risks
        - **Hormonal replacement therapy:**
            - <u>Indications</u> - for post-menopausal women w/ climacteric Sx (no longer used for osteoporosis alone) and no prior fractures
            - <u>MOA</u>:
                - Replacement of oestrogen, resulting in reduced bone resorption.
            - <u>Additional benefits</u> - control of climacteric Sx and control of CVD risk factors (although outcome events not reduced)
            - <u>Disadvantages</u>:
                - Increased risk of endometrial cancer
                - Slightly increased risk of CA breast
                - Venous thromboembolism
        - **SERMs (raloxifene):**
            - <u>Indications</u> - Young post-menopausal women with low risk of hip fractures
            - <u>MOA</u> - Oestrogen-agonistic effects on bone (40% reduction of vertebral fracture risks), and oestrogen antagonist effects on endometrium and breasts
            - <u>S/E</u> - hot flushes, leg cramps, VTE
            - <u>Precaution</u> - discontinue temporarily in event of prolonged immobilization (illness or long haul flights etc.) to prevent VTE
        - **Bisphosphonates:**
            - <u>RoA</u> - Oral q1w or IV
            - <u>Selection</u> - Aledronate, Risedronate, Ibandronate or Zoledronate (IV)
            - <u>MOA</u> - Pyrophosphate analogues, Induction of apoptosis of osteoclasts through inhibition of intracellular signally pathway as osteoclasts attempts to resorb bisphosphonate-bound bones (osteoblastic activity slightly inhibited due to coupling, but +ve bone remodeling balance)
            - <u>Precautions</u>:
                - Poor intestinal absorption - 1) taken on an empty stomach in the morning after overnight fast with plain water, 2) no food within 30-45 min of administration, 3) remain standing after 30-60 min.
            - <u>C/I</u>:
                - eGFR \< 30 ml/min/1.73m2
                - Esophageal disorders (strictures, achalasia), Diffuse SSc involving esophagus, inability to follow dosing requirements (for oral bisphosphonates)
            - <u>S/E</u>:
                - Upper GI irritation (oral) - caution in those with GERD or strictures
                - Acute phase reaction (1st dose of IV bisphosphonate) - self-limiting influenza like illness
                - Muscle and bone aches
                - Serious side effects - ONJ (1-10/100000), AFF (20-50/100000)
        - **Monoclonal Ab against RANKL (Denosumab):**
            - <u>RoA</u> - SC q6mo
            - <u>MOA</u> - Monoclonal antibodies against RANKL, inhibits osteoclast differentiation and activation
            - <u>C/I</u> - eGFR \< 15 ml/min
            - <u>S/E</u>:
                - Rash
                - hypoCa
                - ONJ, AFF
        - **PTH analogues:**
            - <u>Selections</u> - Teriparatide (1-34 PTH analogue), abaloparatide (1-84 PTH analogue)
            - <u>RoA</u> - Sc 20 mcg OD, limited to 2y of Txdue to concerns with osteosarcoma in rats (continue with bisphosphonates to maintain BMD gain)
            - <u>MOA</u> - Low-dose, intermittent PTH receptor stimulates bone formation
            - <u>S/E</u>:
                - Mild leg cramps
                - Injection site pain/ bruisng
                - Hypercalcaemia (less for abaloparatide)
        - **Romosozumab:**
            - <u>RoA</u> - Sc q1mo, limited to 1y of Tx
            - <u>MOA</u>:
                - Effects of sclerostin on bone metabolism - Inhibition of osteoblast differentiation and activity, stimulates osteocyte RANKL expression and hence osteoclast differentiation and activity –\> promotes negative remodeling balance
                - Romosozumab as an anti-sclerostin MAb - increase bone formation, inhibit bone resorption
            - <u>C/I</u> - History of MI or stroke (black-box warning)
            - <u>S/E</u>:
                - Injection site reactions
                - Hypocalcaemia (small risk)
                - ONJ, AFF (\< 0.1%)

### Osteomalacia and rickets

- **Definition** - two interrelated conditions related to 1) vitamin D deficiency, 2) resistance to vitamin D, and 3) hypophopshataemia:
    - <u>Osteomalacia</u> - syndrome in adults manifesting as defective bone mineralisation, bone pain, and increased bone fragility and fractures
    - <u>Rickets</u> - syndrome in children with similar manifestations and is characteristic of enlargement of growth plates and bone deformities (bowing of knees)
- **Etiology of osteomalacia and rickets** - 4 main causes:
    - **Vitamin D deficiency** - classical vitamin D deficiency (lack of sun exposure), gastrointestinal disease (malabsorption)
    - **Failure of 1,25(OH)2D synthesis:**
        - <u>Chronic renal failure</u> - impaired conversion of 25(OH)D to 1,25(OH)2D due to CKD
        - <u>Vitamin D-resistant rickets type I</u> (AR disorder) - LOF mutation in renal 1-alpha-hydroxylase enzyme
    - **Vitamin D receptor defects** - Vitamin D-resistance rickets type II (AR disorder w/ LOF mutation in vitD receptor)
    - **Defects in phosphate and pyrophosphate metabolism** - different genetic disorders or paraneoplastic manifestations
    - **Iatrogenic and others:**
        - <u>Bisphosphonate therapy</u> - drug-induced impairment of mineralisation
        - <u>Aluminium</u> (phosphate binders or dialysate) - aluminium induced impairment of mineralisation
        - <u>Fluoride</u> - F inhibits mineralisation

  
  ![](img/causes_of_osteomalacia.png)

1.  Vitamin D deficiency

    - **Definition** - most common cause of osteomalacia and rickets, arising from either lack of sunlight exposure, dietary deficiency, or malabsorption of vitamin D in patients with GI disease
    - **Physiology of vitamin D metabolism:**
      - **Normal source of vitamin D:**
        - <u>Skin</u> (70%) - derived from 7-dehydrocholesterol under the influence of UV light
        - <u>Diet</u> (30%) - derived from dietary intake
      - **Biological activation of skin and dietary vitamin D** - 2 step hydroxylation of vitamin D (cholecalciferol) upon entering circulation into biologically active form:
        - <u>Liver hydroxylation</u> - 25 hydroxylase activity to convert vitamin D into 25(OH)D (biolocailly inactive)
        - <u>Renal hydroxylation</u> 1-alpha hydroxylase activity to convert 25(OH)D into 1,25(OH)2D (biologically active)
      - **Physiological effects of vitamin D** - increased Ca levels, decreased PO4 levels by acting on 1) gut, 2) kidneys, and 3) bone:
        - <u>Effects on gut</u> - increased Ca absorption
        - <u>Effects on kidneys</u> - increased Ca reabsorption in DCT, increased PO4 excretion
        - <u>Effects on bones</u> - increased bone remodelling
      - **Control of 1,25(OH)2D levels** - regulated by a negative feedback loop orchestrated by PTH glands:
        - <u>Sensors</u> - CaSR in parathyroid chief cells:
          - 1\. Vitamin D deficiency results in hypoCa, resulting in less stimulation on CaSR
          - 2\. This results in reduced degradation of parathyroid vesicles, promoting PTH release
        - <u>Effectors</u> - increased 1-alpha hydroxylase activity, resulting in increased conversion of 25(OH)D to 1,25(OH)2D

      
      ![](img/physiology_of_vitamin_d_metabolism.png)
    - **Pathophysiology of vitamin D deficiency:**
      - **Inadequate source of vitamin D** - results in **inadequate cholecalciferol and 25(OH)D levels**:
        - <u>Classical</u> - inadequate sunlight exposure or diet
        - <u>GI disease</u> - malabsorption resulting in reduced absorption of Ca and vitamin D
      - **Prolonged vitamin D deficiency** - reserves of 25(OH)D becomes progressively depleted from secondary hyperPTH such that 1,25(OH)2D levels remains low despite PTH stimulation, leading to **hypoCa**, and **progressive demineralisation of skeletion**
    - **Clinical features of rickets** - seen in children:
      - Delayed development
      - Muscle hypotonia
      - Craniotabes
      - Bossing of frontal and parietal bones
      - Delayed anterior fontanelle closure
      - Enlarged epiphyses (most prominent in radius)
      - Rickety rosary (swelling of costochondral junction)
    - **Clinical features of osteomalacia:**
      - <u>Asymptomatic</u> - mild osteomalacia may only manifest biochemically
      - <u>Fractures</u> - ocassionally presents w/ fractures that mimic osteoporosis
      - <u>Focal bone and muscle pain and tenderness</u> - due to fissure fractures of the ribs and pelvis
      - <u>Proximal muscle weakness</u> - results in waddling gait and struggle to climb stairs or get out of a chair
    - **Ix:**
      - **Routine bloods** - LFT, Ca, PO4, PTH, 25(OH)D:
        - <u>LFT</u> - issolated raised ALP reflecting increased bone remodelling
        - <u>Ca</u> - low, or low-normal
        - <u>PO4</u> - low, or low-normal
        - <u>PTH</u> - typically elevated in response to hypoCa
        - <u>25(OH)D</u> - low or undetectable (diagnostic finding)
      - **Radiographs:**
        - <u>Focal radioluscent areas</u> - pseudofractures or Looser's sign seen along pelvis and long bones 
        ![](img/loosers_zone_osteomalacia.png)
        - <u>Thickened epiphyseal plate</u> - seen in children
      - **Radionucleotide bone scans** - may reveal multiple hot spots in ribs and pelvis which may be <u>mistakened for metastasis</u>
      - **Bone Bx** - performed when other Ix inconclusive, pathognomic features of increased thickness and extent of osteoid seams
    - **Mx:**
      - **Principles of Mx:**
        - <u>Patient education</u> - adequate sunlight exposure
        - <u>Oral vitamin D replacement</u> - initial high dose to replenish 25(OH)D stores, and subsequent maintenance dose depending on underlying comorbidities
        - <u>Monitoring</u> - repeat Ix to ensure adequate healing of bone disease
      - **Vitamin D replacement:**
        - <u>Regimen</u>:
          - 250-1000 microgram per day for 3-4 mo
          - Maintenance dose of 10-20 microgram per day once recovery of bone disease biochemically (GI disease may require higher dose)
      - **Monitoring:**
        - <u>25(OH)D</u> - rapid elevation of serum 25(OH)D levels
        - <u>PTH</u> - rapid reduction of PTH levels
        - <u>ALP</u> - initially rises due to increased mineralisation of bone, but will return to reference range once bone disease heals

### Paget's disease of bone

- **Definition** - common condition characterised by focal areas of increased and disorganised bone remodelling
- **Epidemiology:**
    - <u>Prevalence</u> - rare in Asians
    - <u>Demographic</u>:
        - Age - increasing incidence w/ age (up to 8% in UK population \> 85y)
        - Demographic - common in Western countries but rare in Asia
- **Bones affected by PDB:**
    - Pelvis
    - Femur
    - Tibia
    - Lumbar spine
    - Skull
    - Scapula
- **Pathophysiology of PDB:**
    - **Environmental trigger in a genetically predisposed individual:**
        - <u>Genetical predisposition</u> - mutations in the SQSTM1 gene are common cause of classical PDB
        - <u>Environmental trigger</u> - conflicting evidence:
            - Measle infections - due to histological evidence of inclusion bodies within osteoclasts
            - Overuse injury - often in sites of muscle insertion that are subjected to repetitive trauma or overuse
    - **Disorganised bone remodelling** - primary abnormality is increased osteoclastic bone resorption, accompanied by increased osteoblastic activity, vascularity and fibrosis, where overall effect is <u>architectually abnormal bone w/ reduced mechanical strength</u>
    - **Secondary OA changes** - if the primary abnormality occurs in subchondral bone, the joint may be comprimised resulting in <u>Paget's arthropathy</u>
- **Clinical features of PDB** - classical presentation is w/ bone pain, deformity, deafness and pathological fractures:
    - <u>Asymptomatic</u> - patients may be identified through abnormally raised ALP
    - <u>Bone pain</u> - multiple areas of bone pain, w/ increased warmth over affected areas
    - <u>Bone deformities</u>:
        - Lower limb - often discrepency between the limbs that is visible to the patient
        - Skull - may complain that hats no longer fit due to cranial enlargement
    - <u>Deafness</u> - results from CHL due to osteosclerosis of the temporal bone
