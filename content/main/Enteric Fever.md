# Enteric Fever

- **Definition** - clinical syndrome w/ charateristic progression of Sx caused by systemic infection by S. typhi, S. paratyphi A or B acquired via the faecal-oral route
- **Epidemiology:**
    - Endemic and an important cause of fever in India, sub-Saharan Africa and Latin America
    - Relatively rare outside these countries, but must be considered if +ve travel Hx
- **Microbiology of Salmonella spp:**
    - Aerobic GNR (Enterobacterales)
    - Obligate intracellular organisms (requires floroquinolones due to good intracellular penetrance)
- **Pathophysiology and natural Hx of enteric fever:**
    - <u>Acquisition of infection</u> - acquired via the **fecal-oral route**
    - <u>Transient bacteremia and infiltration int Peyer's patches</u> - initially transient bacteraemia for a few days explaining fever and other systemic Sx
    - <u>Infiltration into Peyer's patches</u> - subsequently bacilli localises into the Peyer's patches, resulting in swelling and ulceration, explaining the abdominal Sx
    - <u>Persistent infection after recovery</u> - 5% patients become chronic carriers and continue to secret bacteria via stool for \> 1y after clinical recovery

- **Clinical features of enteric fever** - non-specific presentation of systemic features in first week:
    - **First week** - features of <u>septicaemia</u>:
        - <u>Fever</u> - insiduous onset of fever, characterised by rises in a **step-ladder fashion** for 4-5 days
        - <u>Relative bradycardia</u> - the pulse is often slower than would be expected from the height of the temperature and should suggest enteric fever from the start
        - <u>Systemic upset</u> - w/ malaise, increasing headache, drowsiness, myalgia
    - **End of first week** - onset of abdominal Sx and additional systemic features:
        - <u>Constipation</u> - caused by transient obstruction due to swelling by the Peyer's patches at the ileocecal valve (less prominent in children), which is often superceded by 1) abdominal distension, or 2) diarrhoea
        - <u>Abdominal distension and tenderness</u> - caused by small bowel obstruction
        - <u>Diarrhoea and vomiting</u> - vomitingdue to the obstruction, while diarrhoea may occur due to bacterial liquefaction of the contents proximal to the obstruction
        - <u>Rose spots on trunk</u> - on the upper abdomen, and on the back, as sparse, slightly raised rose-red spots which are blanchable
        - <u>Splenomegaly</u> - spleen tip may be palpated by 7-10th day
    - **Complications** - typically by second week:
        - <u>Intestinal perforation</u> - sudden onset of acute, diffuse abdominal pain
        - <u>Intestinal haemorrhage</u> - caused by ulcerations and bleeding of the small bowel mucosa (N.B. a drop in body temperature during this disease course is falsely reassuring and may be caused by haemorrhage)
        - <u>Bones and joint infections</u> - particularly associated w/ sickle cell disease

- **Ix:**
    - **CBC** - non-specific lymphopenia
    - **Microbiology:**
        - <u>Blood cultures</u> - may be +ve in the invasive stage of disease (i.e. within first week)
        - <u>Stool culture</u> - only turns +ve in late-stage of disease (i.e. 2nd-3rd week)
        - <u>Widal's test</u> - serological method not commonly applied, but a 4x increase in titre demonstrates active infection (non-Sp, non-Sn)
- **Mx:**
    - **Principles of Mx:**
        - <u>ABx therapy</u> - 14 days; generally fluoroquinolones and 3rd-generation cephalosporin due to growting resistance against other ABx agents
        - <u>Monitoring for complications</u> - ABx therapy does not lower rate of complications, recrudescence of disease and development of carrier state
        - <u>Cholecystectomy</u> - may be neccessary for persistent gall-bladder carriage
    - **ABx therapy** - 14d course (prolonged 4 week course if chronic carriage):
        - **Effective regimens** - best if guided by ABx sensitivity testing:
            - <u>Fluoroquinolones</u> (ciprofloxacin 500 mg bid) - rising resistance in Indian subcontinent
            - <u>3rd generation cephalosporin</u> (ceftriaxone and cefotaxime) - slightly increased treatment failure rates
        - **Other regimen** - losing effect due to increased resistance:
            - Chloramphenicol
            - Ampicillin
            - Co-trimoxazole
