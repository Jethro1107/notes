# GC179 Anal pain, perianal lesions and sepsis

## Clinical presentation of anorectal diseases

- **Common clinical presentation of anorectal diseases:**
    - <u>PR Bleeding</u> - usually fresh flood, but clarify onset, duration, character
    - <u>Anal pain</u> - onset, duration, characteristics
    - <u>Anal discharge</u> - mucus/ blood in stool
    - <u>Pruritis ani</u>
    - <u>Prolapse</u>
    - <u>Peranal mass</u>
    - <u>Incontinence</u>
- **PR bleeding** - HPI:
    - <u>Onset and duration</u> - acute vs chronic?
    - <u>Characteristics</u>:
        - Colour:
            - Fresh, bright red blood (distal colon or anorectal disease)
            - Altered blood (more proximal in colon)
            - Black, tarry stool (EGI to proximal colon)
        - Relationship w/ BM:
            - Mixed or separated w/ stool
            - On paper or in bowl
            - Increased w/ bowel movement
        - Relationship w/ anal pain
- **Anal pain** - HPI:
    - <u>Onset and duration</u>
    - <u>Progression</u> - constant vs intermittent
    - <u>Quality</u> - sharp, dull, burning
    - <u>Radiation</u>
    - <u>Timing</u> - a/w bowel movements, bleeding, or other factors

## Evaluation and assessment

- Salient points of Hx:
    - HPI - depending on presenting Sx
    - Associated Sx - anorectal Sx, other lower GI Sx (e.g. alered bowel habits, abdominal pain)
    - PMH - previous anorectal disease, previous surgery, medical diseases
    - Relavent SHx, FHx, Sexual Hx
- P/E:
    - General examinations - generalised bleeding tendancy
    - Abdominal examination
    - Anorectal examination:
        - Left lateral position - slightly flex knee and hip
        - Alternatively - prone Jackknife postion on specialised examination table (better inspection)
        - Inspection - perianal lumps
        - DRE - mass, prostate, pelvis
        - Proctoscope - visualisation of haemorrhoids esp. in early stage
- Ix - Dx and assess severity, and exclude disease of the proximal LB:
    - Endoscopy - rigid sigmoidoscopy, flexible sigmoidoscopy, colonoscopy
    - Transrectal US - for staging of rectal cancer, assessment of sphinter muscle w/ faecal incontinence, and assessment of complex fistula
    - Specialised anorectal tests:
        - Anorectal physiology tests - anorectal manometry, EMG, pudendal nerve latency test
        - Other imaging - Pelvic MRI (for complicated abscesses, fistula, or anorectal cancer), defaecography, fistulogram

## Haemorrhoids

- **Definition** - Haemarrhoids are physiological vascular cushions that aids in continence and protects sphincter from trauma during defaecation (pathalogical only if engorged or prolapsed)
- **Epidemiology:**
    - Prevalence - 4.4% US population seen by physician for symptomatic haemorrhoid
    - Burden - 49/100,000/y haemorrhoidectomy performed in US
- **Etio-pathophysiology of haemarrhoids** - some role of +ve FHx:
    - <u>Engorgement +/- prolapse of anal haemorrhoidal cushions</u> - related to increased intraluminal pressure from 1) constipation, 2) straining, 3) low-fibre diet, 4) Pregnancy
    - <u>Degeneration of tissue supporting hamarrhoids and venous congestion</u> - predisposes to bleeding, thrombosis, erosion and/or prolapse
- **Classification by haemorrhoids** - anatomical relations w/ the dentate line:
    - <u>External haemorrhoids</u> - distal to dentate line, lined by <u>squams</u> and consists of <u>nerve endings</u>
    - <u>Internal haemorrhoids</u> - proximal to dentate line, lined by <u>intestinal columnar epithelium</u> with <u>no nerve endings</u>
- **Severity of internal haemorrhoids:**
    - <u>1st-degree haemarrhoids</u> - haemarrhoids do not prolapse through the anal canal
    - <u>2nd-degree haemarrhoids</u> - haemarrhoids prolapse through the anal canal but is **reduced spontaneously**
    - <u>3rd-degree haemarrhoids</u> - haemarrhoids prolapse and requires **manual reduction**
    - <u>4th-degree haemarrhoids</u> - haemarrhoids that **cannot be reduced**
- **Clinical features of haemarrhoids:**
    - Bleeding - a/w bowel movement
    - Prolapse
    - Mucus discharge
    - Pruritis
    - Pain - only if complicated (e.g. thrombosis, prolapse)
- **Clinical assessment:**
    - <u>DRE</u> - exclude other rectal lesions
    - <u>Protoscope</u> - typically for eary disease (e.g. 1st-2nd degree haemorrhage)
    - <u>Rigid or flexible sigmoidoscopy/ colonoscopy</u> - for more proximal bowel lesions
- **Mx:**
    - **Principles of Mx** - dependent on severity of internal haemarrhoids:
        - <u>1st-2nd degree haemarrhoids</u> - diet modification, medical Tx, +/- office procedures
        - <u>3rd-4th degree haemarrhoids</u> - surgery
    - **Non-operative Tx** - diet modification and medical Tx:
        - <u>Diet modification</u> - high fibre diet for risk factor control
        - <u>Sitz bath</u> - warm, shallow bath offered for Sx relief of **prolapsed haemarrhoids**
        - <u>Ointments and suppositories</u> - may be steroid containing
    - **Office procedures** - banding, sclerotherapy and infra-red coagulation
    - **Surgical Tx** - haemarrhoidectomy or others:
        - **Surgical Haemorrhoidectomy** - surgical excision of the haemarrhoids:
            - <u>Indications</u>:
                - 1\. Severe haemarrhoids (3rd-4th degree internal haemmorrhoids)
                - 2\. Mixed internal and external haemarrhoids
                - 3\. Failure of other Tx
                - 4\. Patient preference
                - 5\. Performed in conjunction of other anorectal procedures
            - <u>Complications</u>:
                - General complications - pain, bleeding, infection
                - Procedure-specific complications - faecal impaction, anal tags, anal stenosis, urinary retention, incontinence
        - **Stapled haemorrhoidopexy**
        - **Transanal haemorrhoidal artery devascularisation**

## Fissure-in-ano

- **Definition** - a superficial mucosal tear in the anoderm at the dentate line, commonly in the posterior midline
- **Site of anal fissures:**
    - <u>Posterior midline</u> - classical position occurs in 90% of cases
    - <u>Anterior midline</u> - rarer but occurs in 10% of F w/ anal fissures
    - <u>Atypical position +/- multiple fissures</u> - caused by atypical etiology (e.g. IBD, TB, syphilis, HIV, CMV)
- **Etiology of anal fissures:**
    - <u>Traumatic damage over anal mucosa</u> - e.g. constipation, hard stools, internal sphincter spasm
    - <u>Ischaemic damage</u> - ischaemia over the posterior midline resulting in superficial mucosal tear
    - <u>Atypical etiologies</u> - causing multiple fissures in atypical sites:
        - **Inflammatory bowel disease**
        - **Infections** - e.g. TB, Syphilis, HIV, CMV
- **Clinical features of anal fissures:**
    - **Anal pain** - exacerbated by <u>daefacation</u> or <u>spasms of internal anal sphincter</u>
    - **PR bleeding** - minor fresh rectal bleeding in association w/ defaecation
    - **Features suggestive of chronic fissures:**
        - <u>Sentinel piles</u> - oedematous skin tag adjacent to the fissure may be present
        - <u>Hypertrophic papilla</u> - small raised skin tags from the dentate line that is responsible for causing **pruritis**
        - <u>Internal sphincter muscle at base of sphincter</u>
- **Clinical assessment and Dx** - based on perianal examination (DRE and proctoscopy are not indicated as extremely painful)
- **Mx:**
    - **Medical Tx** - avoid hard stools and constipation, and reduce internal sphincter tone:
        - <u>Avoidance of constipation</u> - Bulk-forming laxatives, stool softeners w/ adequate fluid intake
        - <u>Reducted internal sphincter pressure</u> - topical nitroglycerin, diltiazem, or butulinum toxin injection
    - **Surgical Tx** - lateral internal sphincterotomy:
        - <u>Clinical efficacy</u> - 95% healing rate
        - <u>Complications</u>:
            - Faecal incontinence (0-15%) - most are minor flatus incontinence

## Anorectal abscess

- **Definition** - extension of infection in the cryptoglandular epithelium resulting in abscess developed near the peri-rectal regions
- **Sites of anorectal abscesses:**
    - <u>Perianal abscess</u> (20%) - abscess collection deep to the perianal skin
    - <u>Intersphincteric abscess</u> (18%) - abscess collection in between the internal and external sphincter
    - <u>Ischiorectal abscess</u> (60%) - abscess collection lateral to the sphincters in the ischiorectal fossa
    - <u>Supralevator abscess</u> (2%) - abscess collection in the supralevator space
- **Etio-pathophysiology of anorectal abscess:**
    - <u>Origin from cryptoglandular infections</u> - infection of the cryptoglandular epithelium by local intestinal bacteria can subsequently pass through the internal sphincter and access the inter-sphincteric space
    - <u>Extension of infection into peri-rectal spaces</u> - once entering the intersphincteric space, abscesses can be formed in each of the peri-rectal spaces
    - <u>Rare etiology of anorectal abscess</u> - e.g. Crohn's Disease, TB, Actinomycosis, FB, surgery, malignancies
- **Clinical features of anorectal abscesses:**
    - **Perianal pain** - extreme perianal pain
    - **Perianal swelling** - caused by overlying cellulitis
    - **Anal discharge** - mucous or purulent discharge
    - **Fever** - due to potential bacteraemia
    - **Constipation and urinary difficulties** - ?due to extreme perianal pain that is exacerbated by straining
- **Complications of anorectal absesses** - abscesses may rupture and cause fistulation
- **Mx:**
    - **Incision and drainage of abscess** - definitive Tx
    - +/- **Systemic ABx** - in selected high risk patients, e.g. extensive cellulitis, VHD, prosthetic valves, immunosuppression

## Anorectal Fistulae (Fistula-in-ano)

- **Definition** - abnormal tract communicating the rectum or anal canal to the skin, a complication of anorectal abscess
- **Classification of anorectal fistulae:**
    - <u>Intersphincteric fistulae</u> - fistulae tracks through the internal sphincter and opens to the skin
    - <u>Transphincteric fistulae</u> - fistulae tracks through both anal sphincters
    - <u>Extrasphincteric fistulae</u> - fistulae formation above the anal sphincters (e.g. the rectum)
    - <u>Suprasphincteric fistulae</u> - fistulae tracks into intersphincteric space then up into the supralevator space
- **Clinical features of anorectal fistulae:**
    - **Visible external opening** - visible opening of the fistula that drains mucus and pus
    - **Anal pain** - severe anal pain exacerbated by defaecation
    - **Anal drainage** - drainage of mucus, pus or blood through anus or the fistulae
    - **Perianal erythema and swelling** - due to overlying cellulitis
- **Signs of anorectal fistulae:**
    - <u>Perineal inspection</u> - external opening to the tract, overlying swelling and erythema
    - <u>PR examination</u> - fistulae palpated as induration w/ cord-like structure
- **Ix and Dx** - typically a clinical diagnosis:
    - **Pelvic MRI** - if complicated high fistula, recurrent fistula or when anatomy is not obvious
- **Mx:**
    - <u>Surgical Tx definitive</u> - fistulotomy or other procedures

## Pruritis ani

- **Definition** - itchiness in the perianal skin, caused by irritation of perianal skin by faecal contents
- **Etiology of pruritis ani** - some are idiopathic:
    - <u>Local anorectal conditions</u> - haemarrhoids, fissures, fistula, local neoplasms, poor hygiene
    - <u>Dermatological disorders</u> - contact dermatitis, lichen planus, psoriasis
    - <u>Infections</u> - threadworms, candidiasis
    - <u>Others</u> - e.g. diarrhoea or incontinence of any cause, IBS, Anxiety
- **Pathophysiology of pruritis ani** - scratching lead to excoriation and secondary infection, causing viscious itch-scratch-itch cycle
- **Mx:**
    - <u>Tx of underlying conditions</u>
    - <u>General measures if idiopathic</u>:
        - Reassurance and patient education
        - Stop all local applications - avoid soap, creams, or topical steroids
        - Good perianal hygiene - careful washing after defaecation, and keep perineal area dry and clean
        - Bulk-forming laxatives - avoid faecal soiling
