# GC180: Benign prostatic hyperplasia, bladder outlet obstruction and urinary retention <span class="tag" tag-name="Lecture"><span class="smallcaps">Lecture</span></span> <span class="tag" tag-name="Surgery"><span class="smallcaps">Surgery</span></span>

## Physiology of micturition

- ****Micturation reflex:****
    - ****Control centre**** - Pontine micturation centre/ Barrington's nucleus (under control of cerebral cortex, suprapontine control for voluntary voiding)
    - ****Afferent pathway**** - hypogastric, pelvic and pudendal nerves relaying to higher centres
    - ****Efferent pathway**** - parasympathetic tone stimulates contraction of detrusor muscles (M3 stimulation), while sympathetic tone inhibits voiding
        - Parasympathetic (S2-4) - stimulates detrusor via M3 receptors, inhibits sphincter?
        - Somatic via pudendal nerve (S-4) - voluntary sphincter control for defaecation and micturation
        - Sympathetic (T10-L2) - beta-3 adrenergic inhibitions cause relaxation
- ****Anatomy of lower urinary tract**** (LUT) - principally composed of 1) urinary bladder, 2) urethra, +/- prostate in male:
    - Detrusor (D) - muscularis layer of the urinary bladder
    - Trigone (T) - triangular area near the apex (note anatomical association with ureteric openings), rich in pain receptors
    - Bladder neck - smooth muscles, comprises the proximal sphincter mechanism (PSM-BN)
    - Internal rectosphincter (IR)+ USM + PUM - comprises distal sphincter mechanism, where IR is striated muscle under voluntary control
- Normal voiding function:
    - Storage phase (99% time):
        - Role - accomidation of urine at lower pressure (high pressure can cause reflux and incontinence) and w/ appropriate sensation (feeling the need of micturition at the appropriate volumes only, i.e. needing to micturate at low volume –\> overactive bladder)
        - Bladder outlet - remains closed at rest and during increased intra-abdominal pressure (e.g. cough, sneeze, exercise), faillure –\> stress incontinence
        - Bladder contraction - no involuntary contraction of the bladder
    - Voiding/ emptying phase:
        - Role - coordinated contraction of bladder smooth muscle of adequate <u>magintude</u> and <u>duration</u>
        - Bladder outlet - reduced resistance of smooth (PSM + DSM) and striated muscle (DSM)
        - Absence of anatomical obstruction
    - ****Laplace's Law**** - regulation of bladder pressure:
        - T = (PR)/2d, implies increased volume can maintain low pressure
        - Extremely fibrotic bladders can result in high storage pressures and incontinence

## Pathophysiology of urinary retention

- Rentention of urine:
    - Acute retention of urine (AROU) - sudden onset and painful
    - Chronic retention of urine (CROU) - gradual onset and painless, presents as vague lower abd distension
    - Acute-on-chronic retention of urine
- AROU - defined as sudden inability to pass urine
    - Most common CC in urology, esp in flu season (use of cough medicine –\> alpha-1 stimuation?)
    - ****Epidemiology:****
        - Incidence - 7/1000/y (M), lifetime risk 2%, 3/100,000/y (F, bladder outlet obstruction less common, typically detrusor underactivity)
        - Demographic - increased incidence w/ age
    - ****RFs for male AROU:****
        - Increasing age
        - Increasing prostate size
        - Increasing BPH symptoms
        - Decreasing maximal urine flow rate
    - ****Pathophysiology:****
        - Increased urine flow resistance:
            - Mechanical obstruction at bladder outlet - urethral strictures, clots, stone
            - Dynamic obstruction at bladder outlet - BPH (muscular), increased bladder neck tone
            - Functional - neurogenic bladder (e.g. detrusor sphincter dysinergia)
        - Impaired detrusor contractions:
            - Drug-induced - e.g. antimuscarinics (cough syrup)
            - Bladder overdistension (thinning and weakening of overdistended detrussors) - post-spinal anaesthesia
            - Neuropathic bladder
        - ****Common Etiology in men:****
            - Benign prostatic hyperplasia (53%) - most common cause in male, due to location in the transitional zone
            - CA prostate - peripheral zone, but also possible
            - Urethral stone
            - Bladder neck stenosis
            - Urethra stricture
            - Phimosis
            - Bladder tumour
            - Clot retention (severe gross haematuria)
        - ****Common Etiology in Women:****
            - Detrusor hypocontractility (excluding DM)
            - Neurogenic bladder - e,g CVA, spinal cord injury, parkisonism, Fowler's syndrome (idiopathic cause of young AUOR in women)
            - Idiopathic
        - ****Common preciptating factors:****
            - Constipation
            - Painful peri-anal conditions - e.g. haemorrhoids
            - Excessive fluid intake (esp alcohol)
            - UTI Analgesia
            - Immobility
            - Drugs:
                - Anticholinergic S/E - antipsychotics, antipasmodics, H1 antihistamines, antidepressants, ipratropium, disopyramide
                - Sympathomimetic S/E - cold medications, MDMA
- ****Hald diagram**** - LUTS (symptomatology), bladder outflow obstruction (urodynamic), and benign prostatic hyperplasia (pathological), all referring the same thing
- ****Bladder outlet obstruction**** - urodynamic diagnosis:
    - ****Etiology:****
        - Male - anatomical (BPH, urethral strictures, stones, tumour), functional (sphincter dyssynergia)
        - Female - post-perative (e.g. after stress incontinence)
    - ****Clinical features:****
        - LUTS symptoms:
            - Storage phase - Urgency, Frequency, Overflow incontinence
            - Voiding phase - weak stream, hesitancy, intermittency, terminal dribbling, incomplete emptying
        - Lower abdominal distension
        - Uraemic symptoms - fatigue, uraemic complexion etc.
    - ****Consequence of BOO:****
        - Retention of urine
        - Recurrent UTI
        - Bladder calculi
        - Hydroureters and hydronephrosis
        - ARF (Obstructive uropathy)

## Clinical management of urinary retention

### Salient points of Hx

- Confirm urinary retention - check first catheterized urine volume
- HPI:
    - Characterising current urinary retention:
        - a/w pain - painful = AROU, painless = CROU
        - Preceeding episodes - 1st or recurrent episodes
    - Charatising etiology:
        - Precipitating factors of AROU
        - Preceding Lower Urinary Tract Symptoms (LUTS)
        - Other BPH complications (TRP for BPH)
        - International prostate symptom score (IPSS)
        - LL weakness/ numbness, overflow incontinence, faecal incontinence -suspect acute spinal cord compression
        - Constitutional Sx. multiple bone pain - metastatic CA prostate
- PMH:
    - Long term BPH medications - e.g. alpha-blockers, 5-alpha reductase inhibitors
    - Precipitating drugs - e.g. anticholinergics etc.
    - Past Surgical Hx - prostate surgery, TURp, urethral instrumentation
    - Sexual Hx - history of STD especially urethritis
    - Hx of neurological conditions - stroke, parkinsonism, spinal surgery

### P/E:

- Vtials - assessment of sepsis
- General exam - uraemic complexion (causing AKI?)
- Abdominal examination - palpable urinary bladder (if not yet catheterised), phimosis
- Urethral catheter output - look for colour, any gross haematuria (note possibility of decompression)
- DRE - anal tone and peri-anal sensation, estimated prostate size, features suspicious of prostate cancer, tenderness, faecal impaction, peri-anal conditions
- LL neurological examinations

### Ix:

- Uroflowmetry, Flow rate nomograms (urodynamics)
    - Slow flow rate on uroflowmetry unecessary means BOO (90%), can be due to detrusor hypoactivity
- CBC, RFT
- CSU x C/ST
- KUB
- No PSA in acute setting

### Mx:

- ****Principles of Mx:****
    - Immediate decompression of bladder - Fowley's catheter or suprapubic catheterization (SPC)
        - SPC - indicated if failed urethral catheterization and Hx of urethral trauma, contraindictated in non-distended bladder (still in pelvis), uncorrected bleeding tendancy, known urothelial cancer
            - Complications - bowel perforation, rectal injury, haematuria

# GC209: Urinary incontinence and overactive bladder <span class="tag" tag-name="Lecture"><span class="smallcaps">Lecture</span></span> <span class="tag" tag-name="Surgery"><span class="smallcaps">Surgery</span></span>

## Physiology of micturition

- **Normal baseline for frequency, nocturia, void volume, and residual urine:**
    - <u>Frequency</u> - urinary frequency as defined by \> 8 voids during daytime (IPSS defines as Q2H)
    - <u>Nocturia</u> - \>= 2 voids during sleep time, where each void is preceded and followed by sleep
    - <u>Void volume</u> - 200-400 mL per void
    - <u>Residual urine</u> - \< 150 ml defined as normal (complete emptying, i.e. 0 mL for most people)
- **Compliance of bladder** - normally compliant bladder enables accumulation of urine without significant change in detrussor/vesicle pressure
- **Physiology of micturation:**
    - **Storage phase** - progressive increase in intravesicle volume without changes in vesicle pressure (accomodation of urine at low pressure due to perfectly compliant system):
        - <u>Detrusor relaxation</u> - absence of involuntary bladder contractions resulting in bouts of increased detrusor pressure (cf OAB)
        - <u>Sphincter contraction</u> - sphincter tone gradually increases to prevent leakage of urine, at rest, as in increases in intra-abdominal pressure
        - <u>Compliance of urinary bladder</u> - bladder distends as urine fills, resulting in **non-significant changes in vesicle pressure, detrusor pressure, or intra-abdominal pressure**
    - **Voiding phase** - synchronous increase in intravesicle pressure w/ sphincter relaxation to enable continuous flow of urine from bladder until empty:
        - <u>Detrusor contraction</u> - contraction of detrussor muscles results in **increased detrusor pressure**, and hence **increased vesicle pressure**
        - <u>Abdominal straining</u> - patient's with bladder outlet obstruction (BOO) may requires abdominal straining to **increase intra-abdominal pressure**, to enable sufficient rise in vessicle pressure
        - <u>Sphincter relaxation</u> - coordinated decrease in sphincter tone is necessary for urine to empty from the bladder
        - <u>Absence of anatomic obstruction</u> - absence of anatomical obstruction such that there is **minimal resistance across the pressure gradient**
- **Neurological control of micturation** - normal neurological functions required for <u>urinary continece</u>:
    - **Pontine micturation centre** - initiation of voiding, where integrates:
        - Cortical inhibition from frontal lobe
        - Afferent stretch input from detrusor muscles (full bladder stimulates pontine micturation centre)
        - Cerebellar input for coordinated voiding
    - **Sympathetic outflow** - hypogastric nerve from T10-L2, forming pelvic plexus with parasympathetics, responsible for <u>detrusor muscle relaxation and internal sphincter contraction</u>
    - **Parasympathetic outflow** - pelvic plexus from S2-S4, ersponsible for <u>detrusor muscle contraction and internal sphincter relaxation</u>
    - **Somatic control** - pudendal nerve (onuf's nucleus at S2-S4) enables conscious control of external sphincter
- **Micturation reflex** - spinal cord reflex that is modulated by pontine micturation centre:
    - <u>Mechanism</u> - as bladder fills, and detrussor muscle stretches:
        - **Reduced sympathetic outflow** - reduced detrussor muscle relaxation and decreased internal sphincter control
        - **Increased parasympathetic outflow** - active detrussor muscle contraction and relaxation of itnernal sphincter
        - **Overall effect** - complete relaxation of sphincter muscles and immediately followed by detrussor contraction
    - <u>Higher control</u> - modulation by pontine micturation centre (capable of inhibition of the reflex), which is under cortical control
- **Physiology of continence and control of voiding** - requires normal CNS (including spinal cord), and normal anatomy:
    - **As bladder fills:**
        - Increasing stimulation of the pontine micturation centre due to increased intravesicle volume and stretch
        - Cortical inhibition of the pontine micturation centre as it is not socially acceptable to void at the moment
        - Hence, micturation reflex is suppressed, resulting in overall detrussor muscle relaxation and internal sphincter contraction
        - Conscious contraction of the external sphincter muscle via pudendal stimulation to increased resistance to urinary flow
    - **Voluntary voiding:**
        - Cortical disinhibition of the pontine micturation centre as one reaches the toilet
        - Micturation reflex initiated, resulting in coordinated detrussor muscle contraction and internal sphincter relaxation
        - Concomitent relaxation of the external sphincter muscle to enable emptying of the bladder
    - **Involuntary voiding** - caused by defects of neurological control, or anatomical deficits of the lower urinary tract:
        - <u>Abnormal anatomy</u> - e.g. low bladder compliance, continuous incontinence due to fistula, congenital abnormalities
        - <u>Abnormal neurological control</u> - e.g. dementia, spinal cord injury
- **Principles of urinary incontinence** - considered an interaction between bladder and sphincter:
    - **Bladder abnormality** - either 1) detrusor overactivity, or 2) low bladder compliance:
        - <u>Detrusor overactivity</u> - idiopathic OAB, UMN neurogenic bladder
        - <u>Reduced compliance</u> - e.g. radiation cystitis, interstitial cystitis, ketamine cystitis, hysterectomy/ SCI
    - **Sphincter abnormality** - either 1) intrinsic sphincter, or 2) extrinsic sphincter problem:
        - <u>Intrinsic sphincter</u> - intrinsic sphincter deficiency (due to damage to blood flow/ innervation)
        - <u>Extrinsic sphincter</u> - urethral hypermobility due to insufficient pelvic floor support (often complicating childbirth)

## Urge incontinence and overactive bladder

- **Definition** - also known as detrusor overactivity, abnormal contractions of detrussor muscles during storage phase resulting in urge incontinence
- **Etiology of OAB:**
    - **Idiopathic detrusor overactivity** - unknown cause, more common in women
    - **Non-neurogenic detrusor overactivity** - secondary to local bladder/ lower urinary tract pathologies:
        - <u>Bladder outlet obstruction</u> - BPH, urethral strictures, bladder neck stenosis etc.
        - <u>Local bladder irritation</u> - infective cystitis, bladder stones, bladder tumour (e.g. CIS), local inflammation of unknown cause (interstitial cystitis)
    - **Neurogenic detrusor overactivity** - abnormal bladder contractions of neurogenic origin:
        - <u>Cord pathologies</u> - e.g. SCI, cord compression, transverse myelitis etc.
        - <u>Other CNS pathologies</u> - e.g. stroke, TBI, brain tumour, Parkisonism, MS
- **Clinical features of OAB** - urge incontinence:
    - Strong sense to void followed by involuntary loss of urine
    - Symptom severity could be affected by stress and anxiety
    - Often mixed with stress incontinence in F after childbearing and hence could benefit from pharmacological therapy and bladder re-training
- **Mx:**
    - **Principles of Mx** - escalation of Tx ladder:
        - <u>Conservative Mx</u> - lifestyle modification (e.g. smoking cessation, fluid Mx and weight loss), bladder-retraining, and pelvic floor muscle training
        - <u>Pharmacological therapy</u> - often achieve symptomatic control in 70% of patients
        - <u>Invasive techniques</u>:
            - Intravesical instillation therapy
            - Botulinum toxin A injection
            - Neuromodulation
            - Surgery - e.g. augmentation cystoplasy or urinary diversion
    - **Conservative Mx:**
        - **Lifestyle modification:**
            - <u>Weight loss</u> - 40% reduction in urgency incontinence episode w/ mean weight loss of 8% in 6mo for women w/ BMI \> 25 (PRIDE study)
            - <u>Fluid management</u> - decrease caffeinated drinks and alcohol intake, which reduces frequency, but not incontinence
            - <u>Smoking cessation</u> - weak evidence for UI
        - **Bladder retraining** - 6mo training protocol to enhance central control of voiding as in infancy:
            - <u>Components</u>:
                - **Time voiding** - urinate to a schedule rather than response to urge
                - **Diverse attention during urge** - e.g. deep breathing, mental calculation, squeezing of pelvic floor muscles
            - <u>Efficacy</u> - aimed to decrease urgency and frequency
        - **Pelvic floor muscle training** (PFMT) - short course recommended especially if mixed incontinence (evidence based on a Cochrane review)
    - **Pharmacological therapy** - primarily modulation of autonomic control:
        - **Anti-cholinergics** - NICE guidelines recommend trying at least 2 types of anticholinergic before deemed pharmacological failure:
            - **Selection:**
                - <u>Non-selective</u> - oxybutynin (extended release, transdernal or rectal supository)
                - <u>Selective</u> - tolterodine, solifenacin, darifenacin
            - **MOA** - M3 antagonism within the bladder:
                - Inhibition of PIP3 pathway, resulting in reduced Ca influx
                - Reduced detrusor muscle contraction
            - **Onset of action** - 3-4 weeks before maximal action
            - **S/E:**
                - <u>Effects of M1 antagonism</u> - cognitive impairment
                - <u>Effects of M2 antagonism</u> - tachycardia, constipation (4%)
                - <u>Effects of M3 antagonism</u> - dry mouth (25%), blurred vision, dizziness
            - **C/I:**
                - Uncontrolled acute close angle glaucoma
                - Large bowel involvement in active IBD (risk of toxic megacolon)
                - IO
                - Myasthenia gravis
        - **Efficacy** - meta-analysis (Chapple et al.) shows:
            - 70% efficacy in decreased frequency, urgency, incontinence episode, and increased functional bladder capacity
            - However \> 50% will stop agent within 3 mo due to ineffectiveness or adverse effects
    - **Beta-3 agonists:**
        - **Selection** - Mirabegron
        - **MOA** - activation of AC for conversion of ATP to cAMP:
            - Activation of PKA, resulting in reduced intracellular calcium
            - Results in detrusor smooth muscle relaxation
        - **Dosing** - intiate at 25 mg/d or 50 mg/d, up to 100 mg/d
        - **S/E** - safe medications:
            - HTN
            - Headache
            - UTI
            - Nasopharyngitis
        - **Precaution** - warning if severe HTN, CVA or cardiovascular disease
        - **Effiacy** - same meta-analysis shows similar efficacy as anticholinergics
    - **Combination therapy of beta-3 agonist and anticholinergics** - combination of solifenacin and mirabegron a/w better Sx control (2015 EU Symphony trial)
    - **Estrogen local therapy** - for post-menopausal women
- **Augmentation cystoplasty** (Clam cystoplasty) - requires performing urodynamic study for identification of mixed incontinence:
    - <u>Principles</u> - opening of the bladder like a clam down to the level of ureteral oriface, and defect patched by bowel segment, resulting in:
        - Impaired bladder contraction (decreased amplitude of contraction)
        - Increased capacity of the bladder (lower detrusor pressure)
    - <u>C/I</u>:
        - Renal impairment (CrCl \< 40ml/min)
        - Liver impairment
        - Other bowel pathology - e.g. short gut syndrome, IBD, RT to bowel
        - Inability for clean intermittent self-catheterisation
    - <u>Complications</u>:
        - **Early complications:**
            - <u>General surgical risks</u> - bleeding, infection, collection
            - <u>Procedural-specific risks</u> - anastomotic leakage, ileus
        - **Long-term complications:**
            - <u>Mechanical</u> - need of CISC, spontaneous rupture, mucus perforation
            - <u>Metabolic</u> - hyperchloraemic, hypokalaemic metabolic acidosis (mucus secretion in bladder), osteoporosis
            - <u>Malabsorption</u> - B12 (anaemia), bile acid absorption etc.
            - <u>Malignancy</u> - adenoCa
- **Other therapies:**
    - Intravesical instillation of vanniloids
    - Butulinum toxin injection
    - Posterior tibial nerve stimulation or sacral neuromodulation

## Etiology, pathophysiology and epidemiology of urinary incontinence

- Continence of urine - dependent on:
    - Normal CNS - suppressible micturation reflex to higher centre control
    - Spinal cord control
    - Anatomically normal lower urinary tract
- Continence mechanism - less important in men as prostate serves as continence mechanism
    - Anatomical support by intact pelvic floor to hold bladder neck and urethra in place - especially important in female
    - Intrinsic urethral mechanism - coaptation of mucosa, compression by the submucosa and by the sphincters
- **Definition of Urinary incontinence** - International continence sosciety (ICS) defines urinary incontinence as condition where <u>involuntary loss of urine</u> is a social or hygienic problem and is <u>objectively demonstrable</u>
- **Epidemiology:**
    - **Demographic** - F \>\> M, increases w/ age (50-70y)
    - Etiological prevalence - Stress incontinence (50%), Urge incontinence (11%), Mixed incontinence (36%)
- **Types of incontinence:**
    - **Stress urinary incontinence** - leakage on increased abdominal pressure due to cough, sneeze, exertion or effort, only occuring at bladder pressure increasing and exceeding urethral pressure
    - **Urge incontinence** - leakage accompanied by or immediately by urgency (a sudden strong disire to void), due to detrussor contraction overcoming urethral resistance
    - **Mixed urinary urgency** - leakage associated w/ urgency but also with conditions increasing intra-abdominal pressure
    - **Overflow incontinence** - CROU or functional incontience
- **RFs for urinary incontinence:**
    - Unmodifiable - age, female, white race
    - Modifiable:
        - General - smoking coffeine, obesity, and poor mobility, fluid intake
        - Diseases - childbirth (stress incontinence), infection, DM, neurological disease, anatomical disorders, pelvic, perineal and prostate surgery, RT and medications
- **Etiology of urinary incontinance:**
    - Anatomical:
        - Congenital - duplex ureter w/ insertion distal to external sphincter
        - Infection
        - Iatrogenic - post-prostatectomy, sphincteric injury
        - Birth injury - vesicovaginal fistula (VVF), stress incontinence etc.
        - Neoplastic
    - Alterations of bladder compliance (Neurological or structural changes):
        - Fibrotic bladder - cystitis, SCI, previous hysterectomy
        - Filling at rate exceeding the rate of stress relaxation
        - Filling beyond limits of distensibility
- **Etiology of stress incontinence** - caused by sphincteric weaakness:
    - Female - urethral hypermobility/ intrinsic sphincter deficiency
    - Male - post-prostatectomy
- **Pathophysiology of urinary incontinence** - voiding dysfunction or continence problem must result from abnormality in bladder or sphincteric functions:
    - Bladder abnormalities - typically manifests as urge incontinence:
        - Detrusor overactivity - abnormal contractions in storage phase (e.g. idiopatic overactive bladder, or neurogenic, SCI, parki, stroke)
        - Low bladder compliance - fibrotic bladder from:
            - SCI
            - Cystitis - interstitial cystitis, radiation cystitis, TB cystitis, ketamine cystitis
            - Previous hysterectomy
    - Sphincter abnormalities - typically manifests as stress incontinence:
        - Extrinsic - urethral hypermobility (not well supported by pelvic floor, causing stress urinary incontinence)
        - Intrinsic - intrinsic sphincter deficiency (ISD)

## Functional incontience

- **Definition** - involuntary leakage of urine despite normal lower urinary tract simply due to inability of getting to toilet
- **Epidemiology** - common cause of incontinence in the elderly
- **Causes** - multifactorial:
    - Cognitive impairment - Dementia/ delirium
    - Restricted mobility
    - Lack of carer

## Stress incontinence

- **Definition** - involuntary loss of urine during increase in intra-abdominal pressure, often attributed to sphincteric weakness
- **Etiology** - complicates childbirth in F, and post-prostatectomy in M:
    - <u>Extrinsic sphincter pathology</u> - urethral hypermobility due to lack of pelvic floor support (complicating births)
    - <u>Intrinsic sphincter pathology</u> - intrinsic sphincter deficiency (ISD) as a result of nerve damage or ischaemic damage
- **Mx:**
    - **Principles of Mx:**
        - <u>Conservative Mx</u> - lifestyle modification, usage of incontinence pads, and pelvic floor exercises
        - <u>Medications</u> - estrogen therapy or duloxetine
        - <u>Surgical Tx</u> - occlusive or supportive Tx
    - **Conservative Mx:**
        - **Lifestyle modifications:**
            - <u>Fluid management</u> - avoid caffeinated drinks and alcohol
            - <u>Smoking cessation</u> - weak evidence
            - <u>Weight reduction</u> - cf PRIDE study
        - **Incontinence pads** - adsorbant soaks up urine
    - **Surgical Mx:**
        - **Mid-urethral sling** (supportive) - gold standard for surgical Tx of female SUI:
            - <u>Efficacy</u> - 90% response rate w/ minimal surgical complications
            - <u>Indications</u> - presence of **urethral hypermobility** and **urodynamic SUI** (absence of UI or OAB), but may be extended to:
                - Complex SUI - minimal hypermobility, mixed urinary incontinence, lower LPP
                - Obese and elderly patients
                - Performed in conjunction in surgery for prolapse
            - <u>C/I</u>:
                - Anatomical deficits - urethrovaginal fistula, urethral diverticulum, intra-operative urethral injury
                - Untreated urothelial malignancies
        - **Artificial urinary sphincter** (occlusive)

## Etiology, pathophysiology and prevalence of overactive bladder

- **Definition** - Overactive bladder (OAB), detrussor overactivity refers to urge incontinence:
    - Involuntary loss of urine accompanied or preceded by a sense of urgency (a strong sense to void)
    - Severity affected by stress and anxiety
    - Mixed w/ stress incontinence in many cases
- **Etiology:**
    - Idiopathic detrusor overactivity
    - Non-neurogenic detrusor overactivity:
        - BOO - e.g. BPH, urethral strictures
        - Bladder stones/ foreign body
        - Bladder tumor - CIS bladder
        - Infections and inflammation
    - Neurogenic detrusor overactivity
