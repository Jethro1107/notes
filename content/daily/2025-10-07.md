# Brain Herniation - StatPearls

- **Definition** - a life-threatening consequence of elevated ICP and compensatory failure of the Monro-Kellie doctrine, occurs when part of the brain herniate through a specific foramina and subsequently causes progressive neurological deterioration and death
- **Classification of brain herniation:**
    - Subfalcine herniation
    - Transalar herniation
    - Transtentorial uncal herniation
    - Central transtentorial herniation
    - Cerebellar tonsillar herniation
    - Transcalvarial herniation
- **Principles:**
    - <u>Effects of supratentorial mass</u> (Plum-Posner's rostral-caudal deterioration) - progression from subfalcine herniation, transtentorial uncal hernation, transtentorial central herniation, and finally tonsillar herniation
    - <u>Effects of infratentorial mass</u> - tonsillar herniation (life-threatening), ocassionally w/ concomittent upward transtentorial herniation (parinaud syndrome)
- **Subfalcine herniation** (most common brain herniation syndrome):
    - <u>Etiology</u> - ipsilateral supratentorial mass
    - <u>Pathophysiology</u>:
        - Ipsilateral supratentorial mass pushes the ipsilateral cingulate gyrus beneath the anterior part of falx cerebri (as anterior part is less regid) to the contralateral side
        - Ipsilateral branch of ACA (pericallosal artery) is dragged along and compressed by the falx
        - Ipsilateral ventricle is compressed resulting in obstruction of Foramen of Monro
    - <u>Clinical features</u>:
        - Contralateral leg weakness (due to compression of ipsilateral ACA territory ischaemia)
        - On CT, manifests as midline shift (\> 15mm is of poor prognosis), and contralateral ventricular dilatation suggestive of obstructive hydrocephalus
- **Transtentorial uncal hernation:**
    - <u>Etiology</u> - ipsilateral supratentorial mass
    - <u>Pathophysiology</u>:
        - Ipsilateral supratentorial mass pushes the uncus (medial temporal lobe) descends through the tentorial incisura
        - Uncal herniation results in compression of ipsilateral 3rd nerve, midbrain and PCA
        - Compression of central aqueduct resulting in obstructive hydrocephalus
        - Contralateral cerebral penducle forced against the tentorium edge, creating Kernohan's notch and damges contralateral corticalspinal tract
    - <u>Clinical features</u>:
        - Ipsilateral surgical third nerve palsy (ipsilateral aniscoria)
        - Ipsilateral paresis due to Kernohan's notch (rare)
- **Transtentorial central herniation** (downward):
    - <u>Etiology</u> - diffuse supratentorial mass
    - <u>Pathophysiology</u>:
        - Descent of the brainstem through the incisura, resulting in Duret haemorrhages within the brainstem due to tearing of perforators
        - Distorted RAS resulting in altered sensorium and comatosed state
        - Progressive loss of the rubrospinal tract as a result of progression from decorticate to decerebrate lesion, manifesting as progression from M3 to M2
        - Descent of brainstem results in increased in infratentorial pressure, predisposing to tonsillar herniation
        - Medulla involvement as the cerebellum tonsils herniate through the foramen magnum, resulting in respiratory and cardiac depression and Cushing's triad
    - <u>Clinical features</u>:
        - Progression from decorticate (M3) to decerebrate (M2) responses
        - Cushing's triad with bradycardia, hypertension and abnormal respiration
- **Tonsillar herniation:**
    - <u>Etiology</u>:
        - Primary posterior fossa (infratentorial mass)
        - Secondary from transtentorial central herniation
    - <u>Pathophysiology</u>:
        - Herniation of the cerebellar tonsils through the foramen magnum to compress the medulla, affecting cardiorespiratory centres
    - <u>Clinical features</u>:
        - Cheyne stoke breathing (hyperventilation, ataxia, and finally apnea)
- **Upward transtentorial herniation:**
    - <u>Etiology</u> - primary infratentorial mass
    - <u>Pathophysiology</u>:
        - Compression of superior cerebellar artery and PCA against the tentorium, resulting in ischaemia of midbrain tectum
    - <u>Clinical features</u> - Parinaud syndrome:
        - Upward gaze palsy
        - Convergence retraction nystagmus
        - Light near dissociation (impaired pupillary light reflex but retained accomodation reflex)
