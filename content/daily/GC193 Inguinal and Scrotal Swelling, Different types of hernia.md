## Femoral hernia

- Epidemiology - less common than inguinal hernia (2-8% of all groin hernia), much more common in F
- Pathophysiology - higher chance of strangulation due to tight opening:
    - Myopectineal orifiace theory - area of weakness in pelvic region with normal anatomical gap absent of muscles, more prone to bulging after repeated increases in intra-abdominal pressures
        - Superior - internal oblique and ransversus abdominus
        - Medial - rectus abdominus and rectus sheath
        - Inferiorly - coopers ligament
        - Laterally - ileo–psoas
- Walls of femoral canal:
    - Superiorly border by inguinal ligament
    - Lateraly bordered by femoral vein
    - medially bordered by lacunar ligament (obturator vessel just behind)
    - Inferiorly bordered by pectineus

## Approach to groin swellings

- Anatomy of the groin:
    - Inguinal ligament - thickened aponeurosis of the external oblique extending from the pubic tubercle to the ASIS
    - Inguinal canal - anatomical space defined by:
        - Anterior - external oblique aponeurosis (conjoint tendon)
        - Posteriorly - transversalis fascia
        - Superiorly - aponeurosis of the internal oblique and transversus abdominalis
        - Inferiorly - inguinal ligament
    - Internal ring - opening of the transveralis fascia at the midpoint of the inguinal ligament (ASIS and pubic tubercle)
    - External ring - opening of the external oblique aponeurosis just superior to pubic tubercle
    - Structures of the inguinal canal
        - Male - spermatic cord and the iliolingual nerve
        - Female - round ligament of uterus and the ilioinguinal nerve
        - Contents of spermatic cord (r/o three)
            - Arteries - testicular artery, cremasteric artery, artery to vas
            - Veins - testicular veins, cremasteric vein, veins of vas deferens
            - Nerves - Nerve to cremaster (genital branch of genitofemoral nerve), autonomic nerves
            - Others - lymphatics, fascia, vas deferens
    - Hesselbach triangle - area by which direct inguinal hernias develop:
        - Borders:
            - Inferior - Inguinal canal
            - Lateral - inferior epigastric vessels
            - Medial rectus abdominus
    - Myopectineal orifice (MPO) - area of weakness in the inguino-femoral region of abdominal wall predisposing to hernias:
        - <u>Principles</u> - congenital/ anatomical gaps in the MPO not reinforced by muscle layers, such that they are more prone to repetitive increases of intra-abdominal pressure
        - <u>Borders</u>:
            - Superiorly - arching fibres of the inferior oblique and transversalis
            - Inferiorly - cooper's ligament
            - Medially - rectus abdominus and fibrous rectus sheath
            - Laterally - iliopsoas muscles
- Types of hernia:
    - Inguinal:
        - Indirect - lateral to inferior epigastric vessels (within Hesselbach triangle), passing through the deep ring and enters the inguinal ligament
        - Direct - medial to inferior epigastric (outside Hesselbach triangle), due to herniation through an insufficient transversalis fascia
        - Pantaloon - presence of both direct and indirect hernia
        - Sliding - internal organ (usually sigmoid) forming part of the hernia wall
        - Amyland hernia - herniation of appendix through deep ring
        - Littre - hernation of the Meckel's diverticulum
        - Ritcher's hernia - partial herniation of anti-mesenteric portion of the intestine causing strangulation without obstruction
        - Maydl - herniation of two bowel loops, ocassionally causing small loop obstruction of the intra-abdominal portion
    - Femoral hernia - 2-8% of all adult hernias (most common in females, but the most common hernia in female is still inguinal hernia)
- DDx of groin swellings:
    - Painful swelling - tender LN, incarcerated inguinal hernia, incarcerated femoral hernia, undescended testes
    - Painless swelling:
        - Hernia - inguinal hernia, femoral hernia
        - Skin swelling - e.g. sebaceous cyst, lymphoma, and other lumps and bumps
        - Lymphatics - non-tender LN
        - Vascular - femoral artery anueurysm, saphena varix
- DDx of scrotal swellings:
    - Painful swellings - testicular torsion, torsion of testicular or epididymal appendages, epididymo-orchitis, scrotal haematoma
    - Painless sweling - hernia, hydrocele, varicocele, spermatocele, testicular tumour, epididymal cyst, skin swelling
- Hx:
    - Reducible groin mass - hernia
    - Painful scrotal swelling - epididymoorchitis/ torsion
    - Painless scrotal swelling - tumour, hydrocele
    - Scrotal fullness/ bag of worms - varicocele
- RFs for IH:
    - Intrinsic RFs:
        - Demographic - age, gender
        - FHx
        - PMH - collagen metabolic disorders
        - Previous contralateral hernia
    - Acquired RFs:
        - Prostatectomy
        - Obesity
        - Chronic constipation
        - Pulmonary disease (?cough)
        - PD (increased intra-abd pressure)
- P/E findings:
    - Position - Standing and supine
    - Standing:
        - Inspection - scars (recurrent, or incisional hernia), mass (location), visible cough impulse
        - Palpation - describe the mass, can you get below it if groin, or can u get above the scrotal mass (not hernia), palpable cough impulse (bilaterally)
    - Supine:
        - Inspect, and palpate to localise the swellings
        - Indentify pubic tubercle and ASIS:
            - Pubic tubercle - most supero-lateral part of pubic symphisis
            - Relationship with PT or inguinal ligament
            - Reducible - only in supine mass by removing effects of gravity
            - Occlusion test - just 1cm above the mid pint of inguinal ligament
                - Direct inguinal hernia - non-occludable
                - Indirect inguinal hernia - occludable
        - Abdominal mass
        - Examination of scrotum - any undescended testes
- Dx - clinical diagnosis based on PE +/- ultrasound (dynamic imaging), CT/MRI (rare)
- Mx:
    - Asymptomatic - watchful waiting or surgery (within 5y 70% requires surgery)
    - Symptomatic - surgery
    - Surgery - hernia repair
        - Herniotomy - excision of hernia sacs after reduction of content
        - Herniorrhaphy (tissue repair) - herniotomy + strengthening of posterior wall without mesh
            - Bassini repair - tension repair of the posterior wall
            - Shouldice repair
        - Hernioplasty (mesh repair) - Herniotomy + strengthening of posterior wall with a mesh
            - Open hernia repair - anterior (Lichenstein) vs posterior (Stoppa)
            - Laparoscopic - posterior approach (TEP, TAPP, eTEP)
    - RF of recurrence:
        - Poor surgical technique
        - Low surgical volume
        - Surgical inexperience
    - EHS classiication of hernia:
        - Primary vs Recurrent
        - Lateral (indirect), medial (direct) vs femoral
        - Size - finger-breadth

## Chronic postoperative inguinal pain

- Primary vs secondary:
    - Primary - not related to a prior surgery
    - Secondary groin pain - began after surgical procedure (hernia repair, orthopaedic surgery)
- Epidemiology - 10-12% of IH repair patients:
- Pathophysiology:
    - Nociceptive pain - caused by activation of nociceptors due to tissue injury and inflammation
    - Neuropathic pain - caused by direct nerve injury due to direct contact with mesh and or nerve entrapment by sutures, staples, tacks, folded mesh, or meshoma
- Defined as moderate pain impacting ADL lasting for \> 3 mo post-operatively
- RFs:
    - Age - young age
    - Female gender
    - High preoperative pain
    - Early high postoperative pain
    - Recurrent hernia
    - Open repair
- Mx:
    - Try nerve block over nerve distribution - is it neuropathic? –\> triple neurectomy (loss of sensation)

## Approach to back pain

- **Terminology:**
    - Spinal cord
    - Cauda equina
    - Nerve root
    - Claudication - neurogenic or vascular:
        - Neurogenic claudication - LL symptoms upon walking certain distances, such as burning sensation, parasthesia, or even radicular pain in dermatomal pattern
        - Posture related in neurogenic claudication:
            - Standing (extended) - spinal canal stenosed
            - Sitting (flexed) - relieved Sx
        - Differentiation between neurogenic vs vascular claudications:
            - Walking down hill:
                - Neurogenic claudication - worsened (due to extended position)
                - Vascular claudication - relieved
            - Walking up hill:
                - Neurogenic claudication - relieved (flexed position)
                - Vascular claudication - worsened due to higher energy demand
            - +/- Signs of atrophic changes in vascular claudication
    - Sciatica - pain along distribution of sciatic nerve, a form of radiculopathy, not walking distance dependent (different from claudication)
- **Hx** - dx, severity, need and readiness for surgery, functional status
    - Demographic - younger consider inflammatory, older consider sarcopenia
    - HPI - site, onset, character, radiation, associated Sx, timing of occurence, exacerbating or relieving factors, severity:
        - Site
        - Onset:
            - Acute - fracture, infection
            - Subacute - tumour, TB, infection
            - Chronic - degenerative, infection
        - Radiation of pain/ leg pain (nerve root involvement) - referred pain from lower back to SI, buttock, and posterior thigh, extension below the knee and follows dermatome (radiation pain)
        - Timing of occurance - on movement (mechanical) or at rest (inflammatory), or at night rest pain
        - Aggrevating and relieving factors - posture (flexed vs extended):
            - Pain on lumbar flexion - disc herniation
            - Pain on extension, lateral flexion - facet joint pathologies
        - Associated Sx - Neurological deficits, constitutional Sx:
            - Neurological assessment - numbness, weakness, balance (due to proprioception but more likely due to severe weakness), sphincter control
    - PMH - previous Tx
    - Differentiating pathology - onset and time of occurence:
        - Mechanical vs inflammatory pain
    - PMH - DM and past malignancy, immunosuppression
    - SHx - Smoking, IV drug abuse
    - FHx - of malignancy
    - Functional assessment - degree of limitation of pain (work, sleep), psychological state/ emotional distress
    - Red flag signs - raise suspicion for fracture, tumor, infection, inflammatory or Cauda equina syndrome:
        - Age - \< 20y or \> 55y
        - Hx of trauma
        - Hx of immunosuppression
        - Hx of malignancy
        - Neurological deficit
        - Deformity
        - Night and rest pain
- **P/E:**
    - **Look** - deformity, standing posture, listing (deviated one side of the head), paraspinal muscle atrophy (chronic conditions), assessment of spine movement, gait (r/o upper spinal cord lesions)
        - Listing - combination of site of pain from Hx and listing on PE is useful for determination of impingement of axilla or shoulder of nerve root:
            - Contralateral listing (leaning away from lesion) - at shoulder region of nerve root
            - Ipsilateral listing (leaning towards the lesion) - at axilla of nerve root

      
      ![](~/Documents/notes/img/listing_in_lumbar_spine_pathologies.png)
    - **Feel** - muscle, spinous process, SIJ, hip:
        - Note any tenderness over palpation areas
        - Changes in tone - hypertonia in cord lesions
    - **Move** - neurology and straight leg raising test
        - Flexion and extension
        - SLRT - increased tension by stretching sciatica, reproducible sciatica Sx, not hamstring tightness
        - Laseague's test - continuation of SLRT, reduce angle to point without sciatica Sx, and dorsiflex ankle to stretch sciatic nerve
    - Reflexes
    - Circulation
- **Ix:**
    - CBC - WCC
    - Inflamatory markers - ESR, CRP:
        - ESR - more prolonged than CRP
        - CRP - elevated in 6-8h after onset of infections
    - Assessment of tumour metastasis:
        - ALP, Ca, PO4 - markers of bone turnover
        - Globulin and SPE - myeloma
        - Tumor markers
    - X-ray - correlate clinically in positive Hx:
        - Alignment
            - AP view: spinous process in straight line, pedicles are symetrical bilaterally, lateral border
            - Lateral view - ALL and PLL to look for spondylolisthesis
        - Bone:
            - Vertebrae - rectangular shape with visible pedicles (typically eroded first, winking owl sign) and increasing size down the spine, transverse and spinous process
        - Collapse:
            - Height of vertebrae
        - Disc space - L4/5 should be biggest, followed by L5/S1
        - End-points - irregular suggestive of infections
        - Foramen
        - Girdle (SIJ) - ankylosed? AS suggestive of pain
        - Iliopsoas - in continuity in transverse processes, any disruption in psoas shadow caused by psoas abscess
        - (pars) Interarticularis - best seen on oblique films

- **Functions of the spine:**
    - PRotection of spinal cord
    - Transfer load
    - Trunk support

### LBP

- **Epidemiology:**
    - Prevalence - up to 83% in one's lifetime (WHO deems LBP as most disabling and MSK disorders have highest burden)
    - Incidence - 20% of GP clinic complaints
- **Causes of LBP**
- **Pathoanatomy:**
    - Vertebrae - composed of 3 stories:
        - 1st story - disc level
        - 2nd story - foraminal level
        - 3rd story - pedicle
    - Collapse of vertebrae - insufficient height and compression of neuroforamina
    - Principles - each nerve root exits below the corresponding level of vertebrae (i.e. L1 nerve roots exit just below L1 vertebrae)
    - Disc herniation:
        - Central stenosis (central herniation) - compress
        - Lateral recess - compress traversing nerve root in spinal canal (one level below?, L4-5 herniation compress on L5 nerve root)
        - Far lateral (foraminal) - compress on exited nerve root (corresponding level, L4,5 herniation compress on L4 nerve root exited the foramina)
    - Pathogenesis - Kirkaldy Willis Degenerative cascade 
    ![](~/Documents/notes/img/kirkaldy-willis_degeneration.png)

## Pathologies

- **DDx of LBP:**
    - Mechanical pain (97%) - back sprain (\>70%), lumbar disc degeneration, lumbar disc herniation, spodylolisthesis, fractures (VB, spondylolysis)
    - Non-mechanical pain (3%) - neoplasms, sero-negative spondyloarthropathies, infections, non-spinal diseases (pelvic inflammatory diseases, endometriosis, nephrolithiasis, pyelonephritis, aortic aneurysm)
- Discogenic back pain - back pain related to disc herniation:
    - Clinical features - LBP exacerbated by foward flexion (e.g. sitting, tying shoes)
    - Pathophysiology:
        - Modic changes - biomechanical problems
        - Inflammatory change - cytokine release
        - Ingrowth of nerve and vasculaature to the disc after annular tears
- Facet joint arthrosis - LBP exacerbated by extension (synovitis results in fluid signals seen on MRI)
    - Pathophysiology - part of disc degeneration cascade
- Spondylolysis - LBP with insiduous onset
    - Radicular Sx only if spondylolisthesis
    - Deformities - hamstring spasm (flexed hims and knees), flattened lordosis, shortened stride length
- Sacroiliac joint pain - pain in buttock region (but may be radiated?)
    - Requires clinical differentiation from other causes of back pain by provocative tests
- AS - read up

## Spinal stenosis

- **Definition** - spinal canal narrowing, defined clinical and radiological spinal stenosis:
- **Clinical features:**
    - Radiculopathy - nerve root compression:
        - Neurogenic claudication
        - Leg and/or back pain
        - Motor deficit
        - Sensory disturbance
        - Reflex alterations
    - Cauda equina syndrome - caused by severe stenosis:
        - Acute LBP
        - Sciatica
        - Saddle paresthesia
        - LL weakness and gait disturbance
        - Sphincter incontinence
    - Developmental spinal stenosis - short pedicles –\> predilectation to spinal stenosis

## Management options

- **Pain ladder** - WHO analgesic ladder:
    - Step 1 - non-opioids (paracetamols or NSAID)
    - Step 2 - weak opioids (tramadol, codeine, dihydrocodeine)
    - Step 3 (not advisable) - strong opioids (morphine, oxycodone, metahdone, fentanyl, buprenorphine)
- Epidural injections - LA or steroid injections for facet joint pathologies or spinal stenosis
- Surgery - dependent on predominant Sx (pain, neurology, instability, deformity)

# GC200 RUQ pain, jaundice, and fever, Cholecystitis and Cholangitis <span class="tag" tag-name="Lecture"><span class="smallcaps">Lecture</span></span> <span class="tag" tag-name="Surgery"><span class="smallcaps">Surgery</span></span>

- Rewatch Mx of cholangitis and also CT interpretation (last 20-30 min)
- **Clinical manifestations of acute cholecystitis:**
    - RUQ pain without radiation (ocassionally epigastric pain)
    - Fever - dependent on immunocomprimised state
    - +/- Known Hx of gallstone disease
- **Signs:**
    - Signs of sepsis - fever, tachycardia
    - Abdomen - RUQ tenderness +/- peritoneal signs, Murphy's sign +ve
- **Pathology:**
    - Cystic duct obstruction
    - Acute chemical irritation and inflammation of gallbladder
    - Eventually inflammation impededes venous return, resulting in gangrene and subsequently perforation
- **Acalculous cholecystitis** - cholecystitis without presence of gallstone (\< 10%)
    - Presence of RFs - DM, transplant patients, immunocomprimised
- **Complications of gallstone disease:**
    - Mucocele of gallbladder - accumulation of mucus in gallbladder due to cystic duct obstruction
    - Empyema of gallbladder - infected mucocele
    - Rupture of gallbladder - gangrene and rupture
    - Acute cholangitis - stone in CBD
    - Acute pancreatitis - stone obstructing CBD and pancreatic duct
    - Cholecystoduodenofistula - requires chronic repeated episodes of cholecystitis, a long-standing complication of gallstone disease
    - Liver abscess - rupture into liver bed
- **Epidemiology of gallstones:**
    - Prevalence - 12% in M, 24% in F
    - Symptomatic in 10-30%
        - Bloating epigstic sensation
        - Especially after fatty meal
- **Risk factors for gallstone:**
    - Classical 4Fs - female, 40, fat, fertile
    - Failure to empty bile from gallbladder - pregnancy, previous gastrectomy
    - Cirrhosis
    - Haemolytic anaemias
    - DM - neuropathy impairing gallbladder contraction
    - Transplant - except liver transplant (gallbladder), especially alcalculous cholecystitis
    - PTN - due to long-term cholestasis
- **Ix:**
    - Liver US
    - CT scan
- **Clinical Manifestations of acute cholangitis:**
    - Charcot's triad - fever, obstructive jaundice, epigastric pain:
        - Fever - often a/w chills and rigors (indicates septicaemia, translocation of infected bile due to high intra-ductal pressure)
    - Confusion and shock - Reynald's pentad
- **Cholecystectomy:**
    - Approach - open vs laproscopic
    - Timing - delayed vs early (early preferred now, debate in the 90s)
    - Complications:
        - Biliary leakage from cystic duct stump
        - CBD injury - complete transection or partial transection
        - read up
- **Cholecystotomy** - gallbladder drainage for those who are surgically unfit:
    - Open
    - Percutaneous transhepatic cholecystotomy (PTC) - puncture liver and assess gallbladder via cystic duct
        - Why not transperitoneal? Friable gallbladder wall may risk biliary leakage into peritoneum
- **Recurrent pyogenic cholangitis:**
    - Historical interest - 1st described a century ago in QMH
    - Epidemiology:
        - Rare in west, more common in the East
    - Pathogenesis:
        - Entry of bowel organisms into bile ducts
        - Initiate inflammation in portal triad resulting in hepatocyte necrosis
        - Cholangiohepatis - leading to fibrosis and cholangitis (anscess formation)
        - Stricture formation - causes bile impedence and repeated stone formation (muddy stones)
    - Causative organism - Flatworms (Clonorchis sinesis), uncooked fish (hygiene issue)
- MRCP - T2 weighted image:
    - Bile - hyperintense
    - Stones - filling defects (void)
