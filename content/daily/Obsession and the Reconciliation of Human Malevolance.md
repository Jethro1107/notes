# Obsession and the Reconciliation of Human Malevolance <span class="tag" tag-name="Writing"><span class="smallcaps">Writing</span></span>

"I envy those who do not share my obsession. The murder is spelled out clearly in my oddly distorted vision of the aporism. It is totally absent from theirs. How do they manage?" Rene Girard, The Founding Murder in the Philosophy of Nietzche.

When left to my own device, my mind tends to wander and weave an understanding of reality that becomes my lens for navigating the world. This view, no matter how incomplete, serves as my compass—a fragile guide that tells me how I should exert influence, however small, to bring the world closer to what I believe it should be.

Earlier this year, I found myself in a particularly dark place. A re-emerging obsessive-compulsive complex gripped me for the better part of the year—a cleanliness complex that had haunted me at the tender age of 10. Even with a rudimentary understanding of modern psychology at that time, I knew that OCD was the manifestation of recurrent, persistent thoughts that were ego-dystonic thoughts that felt senseless, absurd, borderline pathological, yet so viscerally real enough to dominate my mental space. I knew that my cognition was distorted, but even if the vision was blurry I knew I was onto something. I just didn't what it was. Perhaps I was unlucky, but by the end of the pandemic, I seemed to witness certain incidence of human selfishness, going out despite quarantine out of personal convenience. I started to construe a deeply cynical model of reality, that mankind harbors a malevolent tendancy. I felt stuck, unable to reconcile the tension between what the world was and what it could be. I did not know how to operate.

What angered me most was not humanity's capacity for violence but the dispassionate ease with which violence could be performed—violence without guilt. The fact that people could commit harm without bearing the punishment, without remorse, filled me with a sense of injustice. The lack of retribution—an eye for an eye—angered me deeply. My OCD’s fixation on control mirrored humanity’s struggle to impose order on chaos, sparking my curiosity about violence in structured societies:

- Where does this capacity for violence come from?
- Is it an inescapable part of human nature?
- Is it rooted in the subconscious depths of the individual psyche, or does it stem from something deeper, something collective—perhaps the Jungian collective unconscious?

The reality I eventually confronted was that violence is not unique to humanity, nor is it a moral aberration. It is, in many ways, a hallmark of life itself. It all returns to the single economic principal of scarcity - limited resources are insufficient to satisfy unlimited wants. Violence, simply is a form of societal response to addressing competing wants in a community that could not divide the objects peacefully. This is ever pervasive in the natural world. From the Darwinian view, violence can be viewed as one of the mechanisms to ensure propagation of a species in a world with scarce resources.

But this explanation, though logical, failed to soothe my unrest about human violence. After all, one could argue that humanity has transcended the brutal laws of natural selection. After all, one could argue that the human species have transcended the brutal laws of natural selection. Human beings no longer need to resort to violence to outcompete one another. We live in societies that have developed institutions, laws, and norms to mitigate conflict and distribute resources more equitably. And yet, violence persists. Why? In many scenarios, humans no longer have to resort to harmful acts in order to out-compete one another. It was while grappling with this question that I encountered René Girard’s works, which provided a profound new lens through which to view the problem of human violence.

In fact the persistence of malevolent behaviour in the phenomenon in which wants are satisfied in all parties requires a deeper contemplation on the assumptions made by economic theory - what is it that we humans 'want'? What drives us to desire? Children in playgroups where toys outnumber the children, it is almost in their nature for them to fight for the same toy. This act is not economical. This was never a zero-sum game to begin with, yet this phenomenon is deeply human and resonates deeply with the inner child within us. Girard believes this can be explained by mimetic desire, the tendancy for one to imitate the desires of others. To put it simply, sometimes, I desire not autonomously, but as a response to your pursuit to the same object. Humans are social animals through and through. One can go into depth regarding whether this premise is true. Girard offers a kalleidoscope of evidence and explanation, which is definitely worth exploring another day. For the time being, the aforementioned premise is purely hermaneutical, and what follows will only be hypothetical until the premise is resolved. This tendancy for mimesis, is not driven by scarcity in the economic sense, but by a deeper, social impulse. We desire not just objects, but the validation, prestige, or status that comes with possessing what others want. Mimetic desire transforms a non-zero-sum game into a battleground.

From this vantage point, I am able to reconstruct an argument for the malevolence that soothes me. The selfishness I perceived was not necessarily rooted in an innate malevolence, but could be interpreted as a spirited, almost Hegelian pursuit of freedom and normalcy. These acts were imitative, driven by a collecting yearning to reclaim a sense of being in a world that felt stifling. Violence, in this light, is not an inherent flaw of humanity, but a byproduct of our social nature, our tendancy to mirror one another's desire.

This realization did not erase my unrest, but it offered a path toward reconciliation. My OCD, with its relentless focus on control and purity, mirrored the broader human struggle to impose order on a chaotic world. The violence I deplored was not a deviation from humanity but an expression of it a flawed, spirted attempt to assert existence in the face of scarcity, both physical and metaphysical. By understanding violence through the lens of mimetic desire, I could begin to see it not as an endpoint but as a symptom of deeper yearnings.

My journey through OCD and my confrontation with human violence have reshaped my compass. The world remains imperfect, and my lens is still fragile, but I am learning to navigate with a newfound humility. The challenge is not to eradicate violence or eliminate desire but to channel them toward creation rather than destruction. In this, I find a tentative hope: that by understanding our desires and their imitative roots, we can weave a reality that aligns more closely with what the world could be.
