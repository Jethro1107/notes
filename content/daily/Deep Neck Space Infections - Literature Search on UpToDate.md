## Endometrial hyperplasia or endometrial intraepithelial neoplasia: Clinical features, diagnosis, and differential diagnosis

- **Definition and terminology:**
    - <u>Endometrial hyperplasia</u> (EH) - a proliferation of endometrial glands which may be w/o atypia (non-neoplastic), or w/ atypia (neoplastic)
    - <u>Endometrial hyperplasia without atypia</u> - hyperplasia with increased gland-to-stromal ratio but absence of atypical nuclear features, which harbours low malignant potential
    - <u>Endometrial hyperplasia with atypia</u> (EIN) - also known as endometrial intraepithelial neoplasia, a/w more marked hyperplasia with disorganisation of architecture, and prominent nuclear atypia, which harbours high malignant potential
- **Epidemiology:**
    - <u>Incidence</u> - not an uncommon pathology (generally EH w/o atypia more common than EIN)
    - <u>Demographic</u> - most commonly presents in **perimenopausal and early post-menopausal women**
- **Risk factors of EH** - similar to those for endometrial carcinoma:
    - **Non-modifiable risk factors:**
        - <u>Age</u> - increasing age a/w increased risk of endometrial hyperplasia and endometrial carcinoma
        - <u>FHx</u> - higher risk if Lynch Syndrome or Cowden syndrome
    - **Modifiable risk factors:**
        - <u>Unopposed estrogene exposure</u>:
            - Endogenous exposure - e.g. obesity, ovulatory dysfunction (i.e. PCOS), early menarche, late menopause, nulliparity
            - Exogenous exposure - unopposed estrogen therapy, tamoxifen
        - <u>DM</u> - increased risk w/ poor glycaemic control
- **Clinical features of EH and EIN:**
    - <u>Asymptomatic</u> - incidental findings w/ atypical glandular cells on cervical cancer screening, incidental discovery of thickened endometrium on TVUSS for another indications
    - <u>Abnormal uterine bleeding</u> - nature of persentation dependent on menopausal status:
        - **Post-menopausal women** - any uterine bleeding regardless of volume is suspicious of endometrial hyperlasia/ malignancy and should be evaluated by TVUSS and subsequentl endometrial sampling
        - **Perimenopausal women** - frequent menstrual bleeding (\< 21d), heavy or prolonged menstrual bleeding or intermenstrual bleeding
        - **Pre-menopausal women** - raised suspicion if menstrual Hx and PMH is a/w increased risk factors of endometrial malignancies
- **Natural Hx of EH and EIN:**
    - <u>Risk of malignant transformation</u>:
        - EIN - 15-40% in 20y
        - EH w/o hyperplasia - \< 10% in 20y
    - <u>Risk of synchronous endometrial carcinoma</u>:
        - EIN - up to 40%
        - EH w/o hyperplasia - \< 1%
- **Ix and Dx** - suspicion on TVUSS, histoligcal Dx based on endometrial sampling, D&C, hysteroscopy

## Endometrial hyperplasia or endometrial intraepithelial neoplasia: Management and prognosis

- **Mx:**
    - **Principles of Mx:**
        - **Approach to Mx:**
            - <u>Expectant Mx</u> - consider watchful waiting if unable to tolerate progestin
            - <u>Progestin therapy</u> - Merina or oral progestin
            - <u>Surgical therapy</u> - hysterectomy
        - **Selection of Mx dependent on histology and menopausal status:**
            - <u>EH w/o atypia</u> - consider progestin therapy irrespective of menopausal status
            - <u>EIN</u> - hysterectomy 1L for those who have completed family (or post-menopausal), progestin 1L for those who have plans for pregnancy

## Overview of the evaluation of the endometrium for malignant or premalignant disease

- **Method of evaluation** - by invasiveness:
    - Transvaginal USG
    - Saline-infused sonography
    - Blind endometrial sampling
    - Endometrial biopsy
    - Dilatation and curettage
    - Hysteroscopy
- **Approach to selection of initial Ix for endometrial evaluation** - dependent on 1) menopausal state, and 2) risk factors:
    - <u>Post-menopausal women</u> - TVUSS as initial screening Ix, with blind endometrial sampling if TVUSS abnormal (consider if excessive risk factors)
    - <u>Pre-menopausal women with HMB</u> - TVUSS often inconclusive as endometrial thickness fluctuates, endometrial sampling should be performed liberally (esp if risk factors present)
- **Blind endometrial sampling** (Office biopsy):
    - <u>Procedure</u> - blind sampling of the endometrium using the Pipelle for **cytological specimen**
    - <u>Test properties</u> - test will perform better if pathology is diffuse instead of focal:
        - High Sn (99.6% in post-M vs 91% in pre-M)
        - High Sp (\> 98%)
    - <u>Advantages</u>:
        - Minimal cervical dilatation required
        - Performed w/o anaesthesia or w/ local anaesthesia
        - Relatively quick procedure
        - Lower risk of uterine perforation compared w/ other sampling techniques
    - <u>Disadvantages</u>:
        - Operator-dependent (operator may fail to enter the uterine cavity or only sample the lower segments)
        - Scant specimen (inconclusive studies)
        - Risk of false negative Bx (dependent on the area of endometrium affected)
        - Absence of histological information
- **Dilatation and currettage** - have been superceded by office Bx as 1st-line Ix:
    - <u>Procedure</u> - dilatation of cervix and curette to suction tissue from uterine lining
    - <u>Indications</u>:
        - Endometrial Bx cannot be performed
        - Non-diagnostic office Bx
        - Persistent bleeding despite demonstrated benign pathology on office Bx
    - <u>Test properties</u> - meta-analysis shows non-inferiority of office Bx compared w/ D&C (hence superceded)
- **Hysteroscopy:**
    - <u>Procedure</u> - insertion of hysteroscopy for visualisation and guided Bx of lesion
    - <u>Test properties</u> - theoretically highest Sn but still not 100%
    - <u>Disadvantage</u> - risk of tumour dissemination due to intraperitoneal spillage of endometrial cells through fallopian tube
- **Transvaginal USG** - non-invasive and allws for additional evaluation of structural lesion of the endometrium (polyps, fibroids), and the adnexa:
    - <u>Role</u>:
        - Assessment of endometrial thickness (role more defined in post-menopausal women)
        - Assessment of other structural lesions (e.g. endometrial polyps or fibroids)
        - Assessment of the adnexa
    - <u>Sonographic measurement of endometrial thickness</u> - performed in sagittal view:
        - Endometrial thickness is expected to be thin (\< 4mm) in post-menopausal women, and pre-menopausal women at beginning of cycle
        - Endometrial thickness varies w/ menstrual cycle (4-16 mm)
        - Test properties of TVUSS for detection of endometrial neoplasia is dependent on the cutoff (90% for 5mm, 95% for 4mm, 98% for 3mm but Sp reducing)
    - <u>General cutoff for futher workup</u> - office Bx:
        - \< 4mm - expectant Mx acceptable as unlikely to be malignant
        - \> 4mm - consider at least endometrial sampling

## Peritonsillar cellulitis and abscess

- **Definition and terminology:**
    - <u>Peritonsillar cellulitis</u> - inflammatory reaction of the tissue between the capsule of the palantine tonsil and the pharyngeal muscle
    - <u>Peritonsillar abscess</u> - a deep neck infection characterised by collection of pus between the palatine tonsil and the pharyngeal muscle
- **Epidemiology** - most common deep neck space inefction in children and adolescents (30/100000/y)
- **Anatomy of the peritonsillar region** - loose areolar tissue overlying the tonsil and surrounded by the <u>superior pharyngeal constrictors</u>: 
![](~/Documents/notes/img/anatomy_of_peritonsillar_region.png)
    - The palatine tonsil is located in between the palatoglossal and palatopharyngeal arch
    - Peritonsillar tissue is the loose areolar tissue overlying the tonsil and surrounded by the <u>superior pharyngeal constrictor</u>
    - Peritonsillar abscess typically occur in the suprior pole of the tonsils, between the superior constrictor and the palatopharyngeal arch
- **Pathophysiology of peritonsillar cellulitis and PTA:**
    - **Source of infection and abscess formation:**
        - <u>Preceding throat infection</u> - peritonsillar infections generally precedes tonsillitis or pharyngitis and may progresses from pharyngitis to cellulitis to abscess
        - <u>Weber gland obstruction</u> - seldomly, peritonnsilar cellulitis and PTA may occur in the absence of infections due to obstruction of minor salivary glands above the tonsils
    - **Effects of PTA:**
        - <u>Upper airway obstruction</u> - large PTA may result in uvula deviation and subsequently result in upper airway compromise
        - <u>Spread of the infection resulting in complications</u> - spread to the infection to the carotid sheath, masseter or pterygoid muscles, or deep neck spaces
- **Etiological agents of PTA** - often microbiological:
    - **G+ species:**
        - <u>Staphylococcus spp.</u> - S. aureus
        - <u>Streptococcus spp.</u> - GAS, Streptococcus anginosus
    - **G- species** (primarily respiratory anaerobes) - Fusobacterium (a/w Lemierre's disease), Prevotella spp, Veillonella spp.
- **Clinical features of PTA** - extremely <u>septic</u> w/ impending <u>upper airway obstruction</u>:
    - <u>Non-specific Mx</u> - **fever**, as well as fatigue, irritability, and decreased oral intake due to systemic upset and discomfort
    - <u>Soar throat and odynophagia</u> - usually unilateral progressive severe sore throat as it develops from simple pharyngitis to PTA, often a/w **drooling**, and **'hot potato'** voice due to extreme pain on swallowing
    - <u>Neck swelling</u> - **painful neck swelling**, with pain radiating to the ipsilateral ear
    - <u>Trismus</u> (67%) - due to irritation and spasm of the internal pterygoid muscles, and serves as a differentiating factor from severe pharyngitis or tonsillitis
- **Signs in PTA** - note P/E should be performed in a setting where airway immediated protected by ET tube:
    - **General examination:**
        - <u>General apperance</u> - septic looking, anxious, drooling, ipsilateral neck mass
        - <u>Posture</u> - sniffing position to open airway
        - <u>Respiration</u> - suprasternal retraction, rsepiratory distress +/- stridor
        - <u>Temperature</u> - pyrexic
    - **Examination of the oral cavity** - may be limited by ipsilateral trismus:
        - <u>Tonsils</u> - extremely swollen
        - <u>Uvula</u> - deviated to the opposite side

    
    ![](~/Documents/notes/img/peritonsillar_abscess.png)
    - **Neck examination** - cervical and submandibular LN
- **Ix:**
    - **Routine bloods** - CBC, RFT:
        - <u>CBC</u> - neutrophilic leukocytosis
        - <u>RFT</u> - assessment of reduced oral intake and dehydration (increased urea)
    - **Blood cultures** - typically not necessary but sensitivity testing may guide ABx selection in complicated cases
    - +/- **USG** - for differentiation of abscess or cellulitis if unable to differentiate on P/E (can be differentiated by resposiveness to ABx therapy if no impending airway obstruction)
    - +/- **CT head and neck** - for evaluation of complications and extension to give deep neck space infections
- **DDx of PTA** - other causes of sore throat, upper airway obstruction and pharyngeal swelling:
    - <u>Severe tonsillopharyngitis</u> - often bilateral involvement, and minimal trismus
    - <u>Retropharyngeal abscess</u> - a/w sore throat, drooling, fever, but trismus is less common
    - <u>Parapharyngeal abscess</u> - typically a/w a bulge behind the palatopharyngeal arch rather than superior to the tonsil (i.e. a medial displacement of the lateral pharyngeal wall)
    - <u>Epiglottitis</u> - classical teaching is that epiglottitis develops **more rapidly** than PTA, although in practice difficult to distinguish
- **Complications of PTA:**
    - **Local complications:**
        - <u>Upper airway obstruction</u> - evident by stridor and respiratory distress due to abscess encrouching onto the airway
        - <u>Aspiration pneumonia</u> - occurs if abscess ruptures into the airway
    - **Complications related to spread to the carotid sheath:**
        - <u>Lemierre syndrome</u> - jugular vein thrombosis w/ suppurative thrombophlebitis with septic embolisation, resulting in **multiple lung abscess**
        - <u>Carotid artery pseudoaneurysm</u> - can complicate w/ carotid artery rupture
    - **Complication related to spread to deep neck spaces** - deep neck space infections and mediastinitis
    - **Systemic complication:**
        - <u>Sepsis</u> - if result in bacteraemia
        - <u>Sequelae of GAS infection</u> - e.g. rheumatic fever, PSGN
- **Mx:**
    - **Principles of Mx** - selection of Mx dependent on 1) suspicion on PTA, and 2) degree of airway obstruction:
        - <u>Secure airway</u> - consult airway specialist to secure airway if comprimised (e.g. in respiratory distress or stridor)
        - <u>General measures</u> - analgesics, IV rehydration
        - <u>Empirical IV ABx</u> - indicated for all patients based on likely etiological organisms, where clinical improvement may be seen w/ peritonsillar cellulitis (no abscess neccesary)
        - <u>Abscess drainage</u> - needle aspiration preferred over incision and drainage
    - **Abscess drainage:**
        - <u>Indications</u>:
            - Airway compromise (after airway is securred)
            - Moderate to large abscess clinically or radiologically
            - Patients w/ peritonsillar abscess or cellulitis not responding to ABx and supportive care
        - <u>Choice of procedures</u>:
            - Needle aspiration - often more tolerable as less invasive and less painful
            - Incision and drainage - more invasive, painful and causes more bleeding
            - Tonsillectomy (see below)
    - **Tonsilectomy** - may be performed immediately (quinsy tonsilectomy), or after resolution of acute intervention (e.g. interval tonsilectomy):
        - <u>Indications for quinsy tonsilectomy</u>:
            - Significant upper airway obstruction or other complications
            - Failure of abscess to resolve w/ otehr drainage techniques
        - <u>Indications for interval tonsillectomy</u>:
            - Severe recurrent pharyngitis or previous episode of PTA
            - Chronic S/S of upper airway obstructioon (e.g. snoring)

## Ludwig angina

- **Definition** - aggressie, rapidly spreading "woody" or brawny cellulitis involving bilateral submandibular, sublingual and submental space
- **Mandibular anatomy** - comprised of multiple potential spaces inclduding 1) sublingual, 2) submandibular, and 3) submental spaces: 
![](~/Documents/notes/img/anatomy_of_potential_spaces_near_the_mandible.png)
- **Mechanism of spread of Ludwig angina:**
    - <u>Dental source of infection</u> - two-thirds of patients have dental source of infection involving 2nd-3rd mandibular molar teech, as these teeth are located **below the attachments of the myohyoid muscle**
    - <u>Other head and neck infections</u>:
        - Suppurative parotitis
        - Sialodenitis of the submandibular or sublingual salivary gland
        - Peritonsillar abscess
- **Etiological agent of Ludwig angina** - polymicrobial infections involving flora of the oral cavity:
    - <u>Gram positive cocci</u> - Viridans streptococci (S. anginosus, S. constellatus), S. aureus
    - <u>G- anaerobes</u> - Peptostreptococcus spp, Fusobacterium spp, Bacterioides
- **Clinical features of Ludwig angina** - note the <u>absence of lymphadenopathy</u> and <u>trismus</u> (unless paraparyngeal space involvement):
    - <u>Non-specific Mx</u> - **fever**, as well as fatigue, irritability, and decreased oral intake due to systemic upset and discomfort
    - <u>Mouth pain and odynophagia</u> - pain in the floor of mouth, often a/w **odynophagia**, **drooling**, and **'hot potato'** voice due to extreme pain on swallowing
    - <u>Stiff neck</u> - due to symmetrical woody induration in the submandibular region w/ associated crepetus
- **Signs in PTA** - note P/E should be performed in a setting where airway immediated protected by ET tube:
    - **General examination:**
        - <u>General apperance</u> - septic looking, anxious, drooling
        - <u>Posture</u> - sniffing position to open airway
        - <u>Respiration</u> - suprasternal retraction, rsepiratory distress +/- stridor
        - <u>Temperature</u> - pyrexic
    - **Examination of the oral cavity:**
        - <u>Tongue</u> - lingulal swelling
        - <u>Floor of mouth</u> - prominent edema of the floor of mouth (i.e. "double-tongue sign"), w/ tenderness 
        ![](~/Documents/notes/img/double_tongue_sign.png)
    - **Neck examination:**
        - <u>Chin/ submandibular region</u> - tender, symmetric, and "woody" induration occasionally w/ palpable crepetus (bull neck)
        - <u>LN</u> - characteristically **absence of LN**
- **Ix** - blood cultures and CT head and neck should be obtained
- **Mx:**
    - **Principles of Mx:**
        - <u>Airway Mx</u> - fibreoptic intubation by nasal route is preferred
        - <u>Emprical IV broad-spectrum ABx</u> - both G+ aerobe, and G- anaerobe coverage
        - <u>Surgical Mx</u> - only if abscesses identified on CT
        - <u>Identification and Tx of source</u> - e.g. identification of an infected teeth
