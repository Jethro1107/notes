# GC144 A child with recurrent infections, Primary Immunodeficiencies (clinical approach to IEI and JIA) <span class="tag" tag-name="Lecture"><span class="smallcaps">Lecture</span></span>

- **LO:**
    - Clinical presentation of IEI
    - Pathophysiology of IEI - defects in different immune pathways can cause susceptibiltiy to different pathogens
    - Ix
    - JIA vs infectious and non-infectious causes of arthralgia in children
- **Definition and terminology:**
    - <u>Recurrent infections</u> - no cut-off for defining recurrent infections, but suspicions of underlying immunodeficiency if 1) extremely frequent, 2) prolonged, or 3) complicated w/ end-organ damage
    - <u>Inborn errors of immunity</u> - i.e. primary imunodeficiencies, a collection of genetic diseases of the immune system that presents w/ recurrent infections
- **Clinical presentation of IEI:**
    - Recurrent infection
    - Autoimmunity
    - Inflammatory
    - Atopy
    - Neoplastic disease
- **Infections in children** - common in children and deemed normal if episodes are 1) not exceedingly frequent, 2) not prolonged, 3) self-limiting, and 4) uncomplicated:
    - **Normal rate of infections in a child** - 8-10 URTIs in a year, even more if:
        - Just started to go to nursery
        - Many siblings at home resulting in increased exposure
    - **Jeffrey Mondell Foundation 10 warning signs of IEI** - covers different presentations of recurrent infections but should not be treated as an exhaustive list:
        - \>= 8 new ear infections within 1y
        - \>= 2 serious sinus infections within 1y
        - \>= 2mo on ABx with little effect
        - \>= 2 pneumonias within 1y
        - Failure of an infant to gain weight or grow normally
        - Recurrent deep skin or organ abscesses
        - Persistent thrush in mouth or elsewhere on skin after 1y/o
        - Need for IV ABx to clear infections
        - Two or more deep-seated infections
        - A family history of IEI
- **Causes of recurrent infections in children:**
    - Non-immunological cause
    - Secondary immunodeficiencies
    - Primary immunodeficiencies (i.e. IEI)
- **Salient points of Hx:**
    - **Birth Hx:**
        - <u>Maternal infections</u> - HIV, CMV, risky behaviour
        - <u>Detatchment of umbilical cord</u> - \> 30d suggestive of leukocyte adhesion defect
        - <u>Newborn screening</u> - for certain IEIs (e.g. TREC for SCID, KREC for XLA)
        - <u>Other birth complications</u> - e.g. neonatal hypocalcaemia
    - **Growth and developmental Hx:**
        - <u>Growth</u> - Poor growth velocity commonly seen in children w/ chronic disease and immunodeficiency
        - <u>Development</u> - Syndromic immunodeficiencies may lead to a delay in attaining developmental milstones (e.g. ataxia-telangiectasia, DiGeorge syndrome)
    - **Immunization Hx** - particularly adverse affects from vaccines, vaccine-failure or vaccine-strain related infections
    - **Infective Hx** - characterise each infective episode throughout life:
        - <u>Age of onset</u> - first severe infection within first few years in life (SCID presents \< 1y), but anything \< 18y may be suspicious
        - <u>Pathogen</u> - identify the associated pathogen in each infective episode as:
            - Look for opportunistic pathogens (esp. vaccine strains)
            - Pathogen-immunophenotype-genotype correlations in IEI
    - **PMH:**
        - Other manifestations of IEI, e.g. autoimmunity/ autoinflammatory disease, non-malignant lymphoproliferation, atopy, childhood malignancies
        - Other medical comorbidities
        - Surgical conditions
        - Past medications (including steroids)
        - Hx of blood product transfusions
    - **FHx** - of recurrent infections, autimmune disease, atopy or malignancies
    - **SHx** - to assess potential exposures
- **P/E:**
    - <u>Growth</u> - failure to thrive
    - <u>Mucocutaneous surfaces</u> - candidiasis
    - <u>Abdomen</u> - hepatosplenomegaly
    - <u>Chest</u> - bronchiectasis
    - <u>Neurological</u> - ataxia
    - <u>Skin</u> - poor wound healing or scars
- **Inborn errors of immunity** - classified into 10 categories by International Union of Immunological Societies:
    - I and II - Combined deficiencies of cellular and humoral immunity
    - III - Predominant antibody deficiencies
    - V and VI - Defects in phagocytes and the innate immune system
    - VII - Complement dysfunctions
- **Approach to recurrent infections:**
    - Normality - 8-10 URTIs per year in young children can be normal, especiallystarting to go through nursery or many siblings at home (provided no end-organ damage)
    - Normal infection are usually:
        - Short duration
        - Self-limiting
        - Uncomplicated
        - Healthy in between episoedes
    - Causes of recurrent infections:
        - Non-immunologic defects
        - Secondary immunodeficiencies
        - Primary immunodeficiencies
    - 10 warning signs of IEI - Jeffrey Modell foundation:
        - Only focus on infections
        - Phenotypes now expand to non-infectious manifestations
- Components on normal immunity:
    - Ab
    - T cell
    - Phagocytic cells
    - Complements
- Pathogens in different IEI - extremely important slide (organise based on nature of immunity and type of pathogen susceptible to)
- Types of IEI - see slide:
    - Combined immunodeficiencies
    - Antibody deficiencies
    - Phagocyte and innate defect
    - Complement defects
- Read slides on history taking - particularly part on inheritance pattern:
    - If suspecting AR pattern - ask if parents from same place (esp. if same last name)
    - If suspecting X-linked pattern - ask about maternal uncles, usually full penetrance
    - If suspecting AD pattern, which often have incomplete penetrance - ask father of some Sx related to disease
- See specific diseases (slides)
- **Severe combined immunodeficiency:**
    - **Definition** - X-linked disorder w/ combined loss of cellular and B cell defects characterised by a low absolute lymphocyte count
    - **Susceptible pathogens** - viral, bacterial or fungal
    - **Pathophysiology:**
        - <u>T cell deficiency</u> - results in defective cellular immunity against <u>viral and fungal infections</u>
        - <u>B cell deficiency</u> - due to impaired T cell "help" resulting in defective humoral immunity against <u>bacterial infections</u>
    - **Clinical manifestations:**
        - Severe viral, fungal or bacterial infection
        - Adverse effects on live-attenuated vaccines (e.g. MMR, varicella, BCG)
        - Vaccine failure
    - **Ix:**
        - <u>Newborn screening</u> - TREC
        - <u>CBC</u> - low absolute lymphocyte count (\< 2500 cells/ mL)
        - <u>Lymphocyte subsets</u> - T cell lymphopenia (CD3 T cells \< 300 cells/ mL)
    - **Mx** - HSCT
- **Hyper-IgE syndrome:**
    - **Definition** - group II disorders characterised by combined immunodefficiency and increased IgE levels
    - **Genetics** - AD condition a/w LOF mutation of STAT3 pathway resulting in failure of Th17 differentiation
    - **Pathophysiology** - central to Th17 differentiation resulting in <u>reduced IL-17 production</u>:
        - <u>T cell defect</u> - particularly a failure of Th17 differentiation and production of IL-17
        - <u>Neutrophil chemotactic deficits</u> - normal ANC and neutrophil function, resulting in reduced neutrophil chemotaxis, resulting in recurrent bacterial infections (and characteristically cold abscesses)
        - <u>B cell IgE class switching</u> - likely an associated abnormality due to underlying STAT signalling and not central to pathogenesis
    - **Clinical manifestations:**
        - Coarse facies w/ broad nasal base and nasal bridges with dysmorphia increasing w/ age
        - Cold skin abscesses
        - Repeated sinopulmonary bacterial infections despite afebrile and being systemically well
        - Eczema or other allergic disease
        - Retained primary teeth and poor dentition
    - **Ix:**
        - Elevated IgE and AEC
        - Molecular testing +ve for pathogenic variant
    - **Mx** - trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole and IVIG
- **X-linked agammaglobulinaemia:**
    - **Definition** - group III disorder a/w B cell defect
    - **Genetics** - X-linked disorder a/w LOF of BTK
    - **Ix:**
        - <u>New-born screening</u> - low KREC
        - <u>Ig pattern</u> - low IgGAM
        - <u>Lymphocyte subset</u> - low/ zero B cell count
    - **Mx** - IVIG/ SCIG replacement
- IG pattern on fetus and infant of first year of life:
    - Maternal IgG transferred via placenta by natural Fc-gamma receptor to the fetus (fetus natural IgG \> mother at end of pregnancy)
- Ix - see slides again:
    - CBC
        - See differentials
    - IgGAM
    - Lymphocyte subset
    - Genetics - for all clinically suspicious patients (IEI gene panel)

# GC142 A child with loose stool <span class="tag" tag-name="Lecture"><span class="smallcaps">Lecture</span></span>

- DDx of diarrhoea:
    - Infective - bacterial vs virus, secretory vs inflammatory
    - Food intolerance (not allergy, which usually present w/ colitis, bloody diarrhoea, mucus and failure to thrive)
    - Osmotic agent (toddler's diarrhoea after sweets or juices, ocassionally artificial sweeteners, but usually mild and just loose)
    - Drugs
    - Chronic diarrhoea - coeliac, IBD etc
    - Surgical disorders - e.g. intussusception, ischaemic bowel
    - Non-enteric infection - see slides for differentiating features:
        - RTI - e.g. gastric flu, adenovirus, pneumonia
        - UTI
        - Meningitis
        - ENT infections
- Infecive etiology:
    - Rotavirus is most common cause
    - Bacterial is usually campy, salmonella and GE
    - Most common protozoal GE is cryptosporidium (SE asia)
- DDx of fever, abdominal cramps, acute-onset blood stool:
    - Invasive GE - campylobacter, shigella, salmonella, C diff, Yersinia, Vibro, Enteroinvasive, EHEC
    - Initial presentation of IBD
- Hx:
    - Chief complaint - clarrification:
        - Is this diarrhoea (requires characterise the norm) - defined as alteration in normal bowel movement, characterised by change in consistency to loose or water, with increased stool frequency and volume
        - Character:
            - Watery vs bloody
            - Mucus
    - HPI - onset, quality, severity, timing:
        - Onset - acute or chronic
        - Quality - see above:
        - Severity - number of times, frequency, estimated amounts as judged by the parents (e.g. nappy changes and weight)
    - Associated Sx:
        - Vomiting
        - Fever
        - Poor appetite - quantify change in intake
        - Abdominal pain
        - Irritability
    - Risk of dehydration - further assess based on P/E: 
    ![](~/Documents/notes/img/risk_factors_for_dehydration_in_children.png)
        - Ask about premorbid weight to calculate weight loss
        - Ask about UO, dry lips, cool limbs (characterise when started to notice to assess duration of dehydration)
        - Risk factors for dehydration - frequent profuse watery diarrhoea, vomiting (unable to replenish fluids), poor fluid intake, concurrent fever
    - Dietary Hx:
        - Usual intake
        - Change in content, preparation (very uncommon as HK parents are quite meticulous)
        - Unhygienic food (dine out?)
        - Known contacts - intrafamilial spread or in nursery
    - TOCC Hx:
        - Setting - child-care centers, institutions, hospitals
    - See setting and considerations in slides
        - Think travel, contact, exposure
        - Think host factors (immunocomprised)
        - Think ABx usage
        - Think history of chronic diarrhoea or PMH related to GI diseases
    - What is done by parent:
        - Fluid challenge - how well does child tolerate
        - Medications - ?antidiarrhoeal (not recommended)
    - PMH:
        - Growth
        - Diet
    - Birth Hx
    - Immunization
    - Family Hx
    - Social Hx
- P/E - assessment of severity of dehydration: 
![](~/Documents/notes/img/assessment_of_dehydration_in_ge.png)
    - Red flags - see slides
- Clinical features of acute GE:
    - Diarrhoea - 5-7 d (most stop within 2 weeks)
    - Vomiting - usually 1-2d
    - Factors causing prolonged course:
        - Co-infections
        - Bloody/ mucoid stool - inflammatory causes lasting damage to colon which affects water absorption (only recover when bowel epithelium heals)
        - Malnutrition
        - Indiscrimate use of ABx - imbalance of gut microbiota
    - +/- ?fever?
- Hypernatremic dehydration:
    - Dehydration w/ Na \> 150 mmol/L due to immature kidney function
    - Problem - normal clinical indicators of dehydration (e.g. sunken eyes, fontanelles, skin turgor) are absent, and hence severity of dehydration often underestimated
    - Clinical suspicion when - disconcordant between different clinical indicators of dehydration (e.g. pulse is disconcordant to temp and skin turgor, extremely cold peripheries despite fontanelles normal)
        - Jittery movement
        - Neuro - increased muscle tone, hyper-reflexia, convulsions
        - Drowsiness or coma
    - Replenishment by pocari may not be suitable for children
- Considerations for setting of care:
    - Red flags for significant dehydration
    - Risk factors for significant ongoing loss - esp significant vomiting compromise oral rehydration
    - Grossly bloody stool
    - Host factors - immunocompromised
- Ix - depend if fluid replacement necessary:
    - U&E, glucose and VBG - if need IV therapy, reduced GC (r/o hypoglycaemia), suspecting electrolyte disturbance
    - +/- CBC - if inflammatory
    - Think about alkalosis vs acidosis and causes in GE:
        - Acidosis may be due to HCO3 loss in diarrhoea or even shock, hence need to check AG and BE
    - Microbiology Ix - typically not required for the following two reasons:
        - Most acute GE is viral in children
        - Even if bacterial - use of antimicrobials typically not necessary unless suspecting particular etiology
        - Only consider if colitis and presenting w/ bloody diarrhoea or admitted (read slides if they are really necessary)
- Mx:
    - Fluid Mx - see slides for details in Mx in children:
        - Primary prevention of dehydration - if suspecting not tolerable oral
        - Rehydration w/ ORS, IV fluid
        - Type of oral rehydration measures:
            - Breastfeeding
            - No fruit juices or carbonated drinks
            - ORS as supplemental fluids (first choice in first 3-4h for replacing fluid deficit clinically estimated)
        - Principles:
            - Replace fluid deficit over short period of time 4h
            - Maintenance fluid
            - Ongoing loss and replace accordingly 
            ![](~/Documents/notes/img/worked_example_for_fluid_replacement_in_child.png)
        - Types of ORS:
            - Historical interest:
                - In the past - chorea most common and causes NaCl loss, hence ORS then have high osmolarity
                - Currently - chorea is less common, hypo-osmolar ORS given to prevent osmotic fluid loss
        - IV fluid therapy:
            - Shock
            - Conscious states
            - Red flag signs despite ORS
            - Failed ORS due to persistent vomiting
            - Paralytic ileus
    - Nutritional Mx - re-introduce usual feeding early
    - Symptomatic:
        - Anti-emetics - not routine, pamela said zofran is most commonly used (see slides on evidence)
        - Anti-diarrhoeal - adsorbents vs anti-motility (no good evidence, and not logical to use anti-motility \[explain to parents\])
    - Probiotics? - some evidence as useful adjunct to rehydration therapy but not routine
    - ABx - see indications in slides, principals are:
        - Septic
        - Invasive
        - Immunocomprimised
        - C-diff suspected
        - Rare infections
- Discharge:
    - Rehydration complete
    - Minimal risk for recurrence of dehydration
    - Give info to carers (see slides)

# Irritable Bowel Syndrome - Literature Search on UpToDate

- **reference:**
    - [UpToDate: Treatment of irritable bowel syndrome in adults](https://www.uptodate.com.eproxy.lib.hku.hk/contents/clinical-manifestations-and-diagnosis-of-irritable-bowel-syndrome-in-adults?search=irritable%20bowel%20syndrome&source=search_result&selectedTitle=2~150&usage_type=default&display_rank=2)
- **Subtype of IBS** - dependent on patient's predominant stool form on days w/ abnormal BM based on Bristol stool form scale (BSFS)
- **Indications for referral** - presence of red-flag Sx suggesting organic pathology (even in established IBD):
    - PR bleeding
    - Unexplained iron deficiency anaemia
    - Unexplained weight loss
    - Nocturnal diarrhoea
    - Rapidly progressive Sx
    - New Sx and age \> 45y
    - FHx of CRC, IBD, or celiac sprue
- **Mx:**
    - **Principles of Mx:**
        - <u>Initial therapy</u> - lifestyle modifications, behavioural modifications and dietary modifications are indicated for all patients dependent on subtypes
        - <u>Medical Tx</u> - depending on most worrying symptomatology and IBS subtype
    - **Initial therapy** - initial modifications and establish a therapeutic alliance:
        - **Lifestyle modifications** - physical activity and sleep hygiene:
            - <u>Physical activity</u> - RCT demonstrates increasing physical activity **improves global IBS Sx** and **prevents worsening of Sx** (Johanneson et al 2011)
            - <u>Sleep hygiene</u> - sleep disturbances often a co-morbidity in patients with IBS and correlated well w/ abdominal discomfort
        - **Behavioural modifications** - brain-gut behavioural therapies as <u>adjunctive therapy</u> for refractory Sx, but early referral is appropriate if suspecting an underlying mood disorder or patients expressing interest in non-pharamcological therapy
        - **Dietary modification** - initial dietary evaluation starts w/ 2 week diet and stool Hx for clinician and dietitian review to identify specific foods and triggers:
            - **Traditional IBS diet advice** - considered 1st-line line prior to low FODMAP diet introduction:
                - <u>General considerations</u>:
                    - Meal planning - regular mealtimes, avoid missing meals, and prioritise smaller and more frequent meals
                    - Food to avoid:
                        - Personal IBS triggers
                        - High fat food (including fried food)
                        - Gas producing food (e.g. beans, onions, cabbage)
                        - Lactose-containg food
                        - Insoluble fiber
                    - Increase soluble fibre intake - e.g. oat, barley, legumes, **psyllium**

        
        ![](~/Documents/notes/img/traditional_ibs_dietary_advice.png)
            - **Low-FODMAP diet** - diet low in fermentable oligo-, di- and mono-saccharides and polyols which are poorly absorbed in SB and exert osmotic effect and bowel distension:
                - <u>Foods containing FODMAP</u> - extremely restricted diet: 
                ![](~/Documents/notes/img/foods_containing_fodmap.png)
                - <u>Implementation</u> - possible to cosider a less stringent diet (e.g. restriction of fructans and galacto-oligosaccharides as these are most typical triggers)
                    - Correlate chart to patient diet
                    - Trial of elimination of all high-FODMAP foods (extremely restrictive) to assess response (lack of response suggests that Sx not related to FODMAP)
                    - Re-introduction with close monitoring to determine individual tolerance to specific fermentable carbohydrates
    - **Medical Tx for IBS-C** - if refractory to dietary modifications (e.g. psyllium): 
    ![](~/Documents/notes/img/medical_tx_for_ibs_c.png)
        - **Polyethylene glycol** (PEG) - first line:
            - <u>MOA</u> - osmotic laxative
            - <u>Dosing</u> - 17g power dissolved in 8 ounce water once daily (titrated until symptomatic relief up to 34 g/d)
            - <u>Benefits</u> - available OTC, extremely tolerable and fewer S/E compared to other osmotic laxatives (e.g. lactulose)
            - <u>Clinical efficacy</u> - treat constipation, but not abdominal pain:
            - <u>S/E</u> - GI disturbance:
                - Bloating
                - Nausea
                - Cramping
                - Diarrhoea
        - **Linaclotide** - 2nd line:
            - <u>MOA</u> - guanylate cyclase agonists (increased gut fluid secretion for GI transit)
            - <u>Dosing</u> - 290 mg od (reduced to 72 or 145 mg od if diarrhoea or offher every-other-day dosing)
            - <u>Clinical efficacies</u> - improves abdominal pain and constipation
            - <u>S/E</u> - diarrhoea
    - **Medical Tx for IBS-D:** 
    ![](~/Documents/notes/img/medical_tx_of_ibs.png)
        - **Loperamide** - 1st line:
            - <u>MOA</u> - opioid receptor agonist to inhibit gut motility
            - <u>Dosing</u> - 2-8 mg daily (usually 2mg before every meal)
            - <u>Clinical efficacy</u> - effective in controlling diarrhoea but not global IBS symptoms
        - **Bile-acid sequestrants** - 2nd line:
            - <u>MOA</u> - sequesters unabsorbed bile acid to reduce its effects on secretion and motility
            - <u>Dosing</u> - 1 sachet per day
        - **Rifaximin** - indicated for moderate-to-severe IBS-D refractory to other therapies:
            - <u>MOA</u> - local-acting ABx
            - <u>Dosing</u> - trial of 2 weeks 600 mg/d
        - **Alosetron:**
            - <u>MOA</u> - 5-HT3 antagonists
            - <u>Dosing</u> - 0.5 mg od (titrate up to maximum of 1mg bid)
    - **Medical Tx for global IBS Sx:**
        - **Anti-spasmodic agents** - as needed for episodic Tx:
            - <u>Selection</u>:
                - CCB - Mebeverine
                - Anticholinergic - hyoscyamine (0.125-0.25 mg orally) as needed
                - Enteric-coated peppermint oil
        - **Tricyclic antidepressants** - for persistant abdominal Sx (e.g. amutriptyline)
    - **Brain-gut behavioural therapy:**
        - Hypnotherapy - promote relaxation and divert unnecessary focus on body discomfort
        - CBT - reducing maladaptive beliefs and stress-related behaviours
        - Mindfulness-based stress reduction - enhance person's ability to relax and cope w/ stress
