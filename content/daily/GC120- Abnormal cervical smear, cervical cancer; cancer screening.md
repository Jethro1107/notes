# GC120: Abnormal cervical smear, cervical cancer; cancer screening <span class="tag" tag-name="Lecture"><span class="smallcaps">Lecture</span></span>

- **Screening:**
    - **Definition** - to detect disease among apparently health asymptomatic population (a form of secondary prevention)
    - **Wilson and Jugner criteria for disease screening** (1968) - dependent on disease factor and treatment factor
- **Epideiology:**
    - <u>Incidence</u> - 7th in incidence in F (lifetime risk 1 in 117)
    - <u>Demographic</u>:
        - Age - median age of Dx at 56y
    - <u>Burden</u> - 8th in mortality in F
- **Why is cervical cancer suitable for screening** - DoH launched a territory wide cervical screening programme in 2004:
    - **Disease factor:**
        - <u>Natural Hx of the condition is well understood, with a recognisable latent or early symptomatic stage</u> - time for progression from pre-malignant lesions to invasive carcinoma is long
        - <u>There is accepted treatment for recognised disease</u> - early treatment for pre-cancerous lesions and early cervical cancers are effective and acceptable
    - **Test factors:**
        - <u>Acceptability of test</u> - minimally invasive sample collection and aceptable test
        - <u>Cost-effectiveness</u> - population-wide screening is deemed cost-effective
- **Cervical smear:**
    - <u>Procedure</u> - on speculum examination:
        - Use suitable speculum and visualise the cervix (cannot be performed if blood in cervix; if visible exophytic mass present, Bx is better option)
        - Insert sampling device (commonly Cervex brush or Ayre's spatula), push gently and rotate clockwise for 5 times to ensure **sampling of the squamo-columnar junction**
        - Rinse the brush into the vial by pushing it into the bottom 10 times forcing bristles apart, then vigorously swirl the brush

    
    ![](~/Documents/notes/img/pap_smear_procedure.png)
    - <u>Necessary information on request form</u> - clinical data helps cytopathologist:
        - Patient particulars (name, age)
        - Menstrual Hx (e.g. LMP, duration form menopause)
        - Obstetrics Hx (parity)
        - Contraceptive Hx
        - Relevant DHx and PMH
    - <u>Reasons for unsatisfactory cervical smear</u>:
        - Artifacts - e.g. air dried, too scanty cells, blood stained
        - Active inflammation or infection
        - Atrophic epithelium (hypoestrogenic changes; apply local estrogen and repeat)
        - Post-Rx - e.g. RT or ChT
- **HKCOG recommendations for cervical screening:**
    - <u>Timing</u>:
        - From 25y (or time of commencing sexual activity, whichever later) until 65y
        - Performed for \> 65y if no cervical smear Hx
        - Not performed during pregnancy
    - <u>Frequency of testing</u>:
        - Cervical cytology - screening at 3y intervals after 2 consecutive normal anual cytology tests
        - HPV testing - screening at 5y intervals (often co-test w/ cytology)
- **Current paradigm of cervical screening program in HK:**
    - \< 50% eligible are regularly screened (~ 21,3% if kicak female populations registered)
    - Those w/ benefit most from cervical screening are least likely to get it
- **Pathologies implicated from an abnormal cervical smear:**
    - Low-grade squamous intra-epithelial lesion or LSIL (CIN I)
    - High-grade squamous intra-epithelial lesion or HSIL (CIN II-III)
    - Invasive squamous cell carcinoma
    - Adenocarcinoma in situ of the cervix
    - Invasive adenocarcinoma of the cervix
    - Endometrial adenocarcinoma
- **Results of abnormal cervical smear:**
    - Atypical squamous cell of undetermined significance (ASC-US)
    - Low-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion (LSIL)
    - Atypical squamous cell - canot exclude high-grade (ASC-H)
    - High-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion (HSIL)
    - Atypical glandular cells (AGC)
- **Atypical Squamous Cell-Undetermined Significance** (ASC-US) - accounts for 5% of smear results:
    - <u>Common pathology presenting as SC-US</u>:
        - Normal epithelium
        - LSIL (most lesions)
        - HSIL (5-17%)
        - Invasive SCC (0.1-0.2%)
    - <u>Mx</u>:
        - Repeat cytology at 6 mo and 12 mo and colposcopy if persistent ASCUS or evolving lesion
        - High-risk HPV test as acceptable alternative and direct coloscopy if high-risk HPV detected
- **Low-grade squamous intra-epithelial lesion** (LSIL) - accounts for 1.5-2.5% of smear results:
    - <u>Common pathologies presenting as LSIL</u>:
        - LSIL
        - HSIL (15-30%)
        - Invasive SCC (0.1%)
    - <u>Mx</u>:
        - Direct coloscopy
        - High-risk HPV test as acceptable alternative; colposcopy if +ve, and releate co-test at 12mo if -ve
- **Atypical Squamous Cell-Cannot exclude High-grade** - between LSIL or HSIL, where 24-94% may eventually be HSIL on histology:
    - <u>Mx</u>:
        - Colposcopy and Bx
        - Manage accordingly if CIN detected
        - Repeat cytology q6mo or HPV co-testing q1y if no pathology identified
- **High-grade Squamous Intra-epithelial Lesion** (HSIL) - 70-75% are HSIL, and up to 1-2% are invasive carcinoma:
- **Atypical glandular cells** - work up w/ colposcopy +/- Bx, endocervical sampling, endometrial sampling
- **Colposcopy** - diagnostic procedure typically indicated for most abnormal cervical smear:
    - <u>Procedure</u>:
        - Colposcope magnifies cervix to 8-25x using binocular optic instrument
        - Addition of 3-5% acetic acid to identify suspicious lesions for Bx
        - Adjuvant Schiler's test by addition of Lugol's iodine
    - <u>Site of Bx</u> - at squamo-columnar junction:
        - On acetic acid, dense acetowhite lesions, w/ mosaic, punctations, or abnormal vascular patterns
        - Shiller's test -ve
- **Mx of LSIL** - most low-grade CIN spotaneously regress:
    - <u>Watchful waiting</u> - repeat cytology q6mo or HPV co-testing q1y for at least 2y if patient is reliable for FU
    - <u>Surgical therapy</u> - excisional therapy or ablative therapy can be offered at Dx for patients not reliable for FU, or if LSIL persistent for 2y
- **Mx of HSIL:**
    - **Immediate Mx:**
        - <u>Ablative therapy</u> (no histology obtained) - cryotherapy, cold coagulation, diatherpy, laser vaporisation
        - <u>Excisional therapy</u> (histology obtained) - loop excision, cone biopsy
    - **Subsequent follow-up** - repeat cytology/ HPV co-testing q6mo until 3 consecutive normal cytology or 2 consecutive normal HPV testing
- **Mx of early invasive cervical cancer** - radical hysterectomy w/ bilateral pelvic lymphadenectomy
- **HPV vaccination** - prevention of high-risk HPV infection theoretically can prevent cervical cancer:
    - <u>Different types of HPV vaccines</u>:
        - Bivalent - 16 and 18
        - Quadrivalent - 6, 11, 16, 18
        - Nonavalent - 6, 11, 16, 18, 31, 33, 45, 52, 58
    - <u>Scheduling</u> - 2-dose scheduling within 6-12mo apart is currently gold standard as recommended by WHO:
        - Historically, a 3-dose vaccination schedule is used
        - Currently, single-dose schedule is under investigations w/ suggested comparative efficacy, which may simplify vaccination programmes
    - <u>Current recomendations</u> - HPV vaccination program for P5-6 girls started in 2019:
        - 2-dose regimen for \< 15y
        - 3-dose regimen for \> 15y

## Acute appendicitis in adults: Clinical manifestations and differential diagnosis

- **Definition** - inflammation of the vestigial vermiform appendix, being one of the most common cause of the acute abdomen, and one of the most frequent indications for an emergency abdominal surgical procedure worldwide
- **Epidemiology:**
    - <u>Incidence</u> - 100/100000/y (rate of perforated appendicitis at 29/100000/y)
    - <u>Demographic</u>:
        - Age - most common in the 2nd-3rd decade of life (10-30y)
        - Sex - slight male preponderance (M:F = 1.4:1)
- **Pathophysiology of acute appendicitis:**
    - <u>Appendicial obstruction</u> - various causes of appendiceal oriface (AO), by faecaliths, lymphoid hyperplasia, local infective processes, or tumours, resulting in an **increased intraluminal pressure** (however studies have shown that only 1/3 of non-ruptured appendicitis have elevated intraluminal pressure)
    - <u>Elevation of intraluminal pressure</u> - results in progressive ischaemia, and subsequent necrosis of the appendicieal wall, predisposinng to rupture
    - <u>Bacterial overgrowth in the diseased appendix</u> - aerobic GNR (E. coli) predominate in early disease course, and subsequently mixed infections w/ anaerobes (e.g. Bacteroides, peptostreptococcus)
- **Signs of acute appendicitis:**
    - **General examination:**
        - <u>Temperature</u> - low-grade fever may be present initially; followed by high-grade fever if inflammation progresses
    - **Abdominal examination:**
        - <u>Palpation</u> - RLQ tenderness (may be absent in a retrocecal appendix due to absence of direct contact w/ anterior parietal peritoneum)
        - <u>Rosving's sign</u> (Sn 22-68%, Sp 58-96%) - pain in the RLQ on palpation of LLQ
        - <u>Psoas sign</u> (Sn 13-42%, Sp 79-97%) - useful for a retrocecal appendix, RLQ pain with passive right hip extension, causing patient to draw up right knee to shorten psoas muscle
        - <u>Obturator sign</u> (Sn 8%, Sp 94%) - useful for a pelvic appendix, RLQ pain a/w flexion of right hip and knee, and subsequent internal rotation of the hip
- **Ix:**
    - **Routine bloods** - CBC, LFT:
        - <u>CBC</u> - neutrophilic leukocytosis w/ left shift, where **higher WBC counts seen in necrotic or perforated appendix**
        - <u>LFT</u> - mild elevation of bilirubin have been noted as a marker for perforation
    - **Imaging:**
        - <u>AXR</u> - no role in establishing appendicitis
        - <u>USG</u> - dilatation of appendiceal oriface \> 6mm: 
        ![](~/Documents/notes/img/dilated_appendiceal_oriface.png)
        - <u>Contrast CT abdomen</u> - suggestive findings include:
            - Enlarged appendiceal diameter \> 6mm w/ occluded lumen
            - Appendiceal wall thickening
            - Periappendiceal fat stranding
            - Appendiceal wall enhancement
            - Appendicolith (seen in 25% of patients)

      
      ![](~/Documents/notes/img/contrast_ct_acute_appendicitis.png)
        - <u>MRI</u> - rarely used but can assist the evaluaton of actue abdominal and pelvic pain during pregnancy
- **DDx of appendicitis:**
    - **Other GI diseases:**
        - <u>Cecal diverticulitis</u> - especially in Eastern population where right-sided divertuculosis is much more common
        - <u>Meckel's diverticulitis</u> - may mimic acute appendicitis whent he inflammed Meckel's divertucula migrates to the RLQ
        - <u>Ileocolic Crohn's disase</u> - should be suspected if there is a Hx of prolonged diarrhoea, weight loss, or PR bleeding
    - **Urological conditions:**
        - <u>Kidney stones</u> - considered if the description of pain matches that of renal colic
        - <u>Testicular torsion</u> - urological emergency considered in all neonates and postpubertal boys
        - <u>Epididymitis</u> - scrotal pain referring to RLQ
    - **Gynaecological conditions:**
        - <u>Ovarian cyst complications</u> (e.g. rupture, torsion, haemorrhage) - considered if preceded by episodes of lower abdominal pain, PV bleeding etc.
        - <u>Tubo-ovarian abscess</u> - considered if PID is suspected from Hx
        - <u>Ectopic pregnancy</u> - tubal pregnancy should be considered in all women of reproductive age
        - <u>Miscarriage</u> - typically does not localise to the RLQ but considered in acute abdomen occuring in all women of reproductive age
