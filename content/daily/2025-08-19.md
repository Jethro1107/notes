# GC38: Comprehensive Geriatric Assessment <span class="tag" tag-name="Lecture"><span class="smallcaps">Lecture</span></span>

- **Definition** - CGA is a multidmensional, interdisciplinary diagnostic process intended to determine a frail eldery person's medical, psychosocial, and functional capacities and problems with the objective of developing an overall plan for treatment and long-term follow-up (Rubenstein)
- **Characteristics of the elderly people:**
    - <u>Multiple problems in different dimensions</u> - multiple organic, psychological, and social problems
    - <u>Reduced capacities</u>:
        - Functional and physiological capacities deminished
        - Frail and pre-fail common
        - Sarcopenia common
    - <u>Added clinical considerations</u>:
        - Atypical presentation of disease
        - Physical diseases might have psychiatric manifestations e.g. delirium or confusion
        - Adverse effects of drugs pronounced
- **Characteristic of CGA:**
    - Multidisciplinary approach
    - Utilization of one or more sets of measuring instruments
    - Use of interdisciplinary teams to poop expertise
    - Attempt to couple measurements with interventional programs, such as rehabilitation, counselling or placement
    - Flexibility is the rule to gear towards the need of a particular setting, purpose and society
- **Purpose of CGA:**
    - For diagnostic work-up to develop plan of therapy
    - For monitoring of progress
    - For screening or early detection of potential disability
    - For determine the level or setting of long-term care required by a patient
    - For education and research
- **Domains of CGA:**
    - Physical health
    - Mentaion function-cognitive and psychiatric symptoms
    - Functional-ADL and instrumental ADL
    - Social resources
    - Environmental resources
    - Economic resources
- **Members of multidisciplinary team:**
    - Geriatricians
    - Nurses
    - Allied Health - PT, OT, Dietitian, Speech therapiest, MSW, Podiatrist
- **Utilisation of geriatric assessment instruments** - standardise methods of assessing a geriatric patients:
    - More reliable
    - Therapeutic progress to be measured over time
    - Facilitate transmission of understandable and standardized clinical information between health providers
    - Make process of assessment easy to perform and teach
- **Division of labour in MDT during CGA:**
    - <u>Physiotherapist</u> - assess motor function, mobility and balance
    - <u>Occupational therapist</u> - cognitive assessment (AMT, MoCA), functional assessment (basic ADL, instrumental ADL, Advanced ADL), home environment and care needs
    - <u>Medical social worker</u> - geriatric depression scale, home circumstances, address care needs and discharge planning, including organisation of institutional care
    - <u>Dietitian</u> - nutrition
    - <u>Speech therapist</u> - communication and swallowing
- **Common geriatric assessment instruments** - used for 1) functional assessment, 2) cognitive assessment, 3) motor assessment, and 4) deprsesion assessment:
    - **Functional assessment** - determines capacity to cope with basic, instrumental and advanced activities of daily living (ADL):
        - <u>Barthel Idex</u> (Mahoney and Barthel 1965) - scoring system to assess patient's independence in performing ADL in different areas, with a simplified Barthel 20, and a more comprehensive Barthel 100
        - <u>Katz Index of ADL</u> (Katz and Akpom 1976) - simple assessment of indepence in performing ADL in 6 areas
        - <u>Five-item IADL Questionaire</u> (Fillembaum et al 1985) - 5 item assessment of instrumental ADL
        - <u>Advanced ADL</u> (Reuben and Solomon) - additional assessment of recreational, occupational, and community service function
        - <u>Functional Independence Measure</u> - complex scoring system (max: 126) in assessing level of independence in 6 functional categories
    - **Cognitive assessment** - assessment of cognitive function to identify mild cognitive impairments and dementia:
        - <u>Abbreviated mental test</u> (AMT) - first line screening tool in assessment of cognitive function
        - <u>Montreal Cognitive Assessment</u> (MoCA) - comprehensive, validated one-page 30 point test for assessing different cognitive domains
        - <u>Clock drawing test</u> - ancillary test by asking patient to draw a clock on a piece of paper
        - <u>Mini Mental State Examination</u> (MMSE) - 30-point questionaire with 11 questions to screen for cognitive impairment and dementia
    - **Motor assessment** - assessment of mobility, balance, and motor functions:
        - <u>Berg Balance Scale</u> (BBS) - specific items to determine balance
        - <u>Time up and go test</u> (TUGT) - assessment of balance, gait, and mobility
        - <u>Elderly Mobility scale</u> (EMS) - 20-point scoring tool for assessment of mobility in geriatric patients
    - **Depression scale** - screening for depression:
        - Geriatric depression scale
        - Hamilton Rating Scale for Depression
        - Selfcare (D)
        - Zung Self-rating Depression Scale
- **Evidence for comprehensive geriatric assessment:**
    - **Rubenstein et al 1984** - landmark RCT demonstrating efficacy of CGA:
        - RCT for randomly assigning frail elderly inpatients to geriatric evaluation unit and control group
        - Those in geriatric unit demonstrated:
            - Lower 1y mortality (23.8% vs 48.3%, P \< 0.005)
            - Less likely been discharge to a nursing home (12.7% vs 30.0%, P \< 0.05)
            - Less time spent in a nursing home (26.9% vs 46.7%, P \< 0.05)
            - Improved functional status and morale (P \< 0.05)
    - **Ellis et al 2011** - Systemic Review and Meta-analysis for CGA:

## Frailty

- **Definition** - a biological syndrome of <u>decreased reserve and resistance to stressors</u>, resulting from cumulative decline across multiple physiological systems, and causing <u>vulnerability to adverse outcomes</u>
- **Epidemiology:**
    - <u>Prevalence</u> - varies depending on definitions (7.9% in HK)
    - <u>Demographic</u> - more common in older women
- **Conceptual Models for frailty:**
    - <u>Phenotypic frailty</u> (Fried et al 2001) - 1) unintentional weight loss, 2) self-reported exhaustion, 3) objective demonstration of weakness (grip strength), 4) slow walking speed, 5) low physical activity
    - <u>Deficits Accumulation</u> (Rockwood 2007) - accumulation of physical deficits of multiple etiologies, resulting in increased vulnerability
- **Given same age, frail patients are a/w increased risk of adverse outcomes, resulting in increased healthcare cost:** 
![](~/Documents/notes/img/survival_curve_for-frail_vs_non_frail_patient.png)
    - Falls
    - Disability
    - Hospitalisation
    - Institutionalization
    - Death
    - And many more
- **Pathophysiology of fraility** - biological underpinnings are poorly understood:
    - **Endocrine dysfunction:**
        - Insulin resistance
        - Excess exposure to ctabolic functions
        - Vitamin D deficiency
        - Relative deficiencies in androgens
        - Decreased growth hormone and IGF-1
    - **Immune dysfunction and inflammation:**
        - Chronic CMV infection
        - Comprimised altered immune function
        - Proinflammatory state
        - Micronutrient deficiency and oxidative stress
    - **Other factors:**
        - Anaemia
        - Autonomic neuropathy
        - Sarcopenia
- **Indications for assessment of frailty:**
    - All persons \> 70u
    - Adults w/ multiple chronic illnesses or weight loss \> 5kg for over a year
- **Common screening tools for frailty** - complicated by \> 70 screening tools, with no consensus on how to screen:
    - Fraility phenotype (Fried et al 2001)
    - Frality index
    - Clinical Frality Scale (SFS)
    - "FRAIL" scale
- **Frality index** - based on deficits accumulation model of fraility:
    - <u>Basic principles</u> - vulnerability determined by number of deficits present (21-70 deficits recommended in assessment): 
    ![](~/Documents/notes/img/frailty_index.png)
    - <u>Extremely variable</u> - different FIs may incorporate different variables
- **Clinical Frailty Score** (Rockwood): 
![](~/Documents/notes/img/clinical_frailty_score.png)
- **"FRAIL" Score** - scoring system resembling model of phenotypic frailty: 
![](~/Documents/notes/img/frail_score.png)
- **Clinical application of frailty** - enhanced awareness increased susceptibility to adverse effects:
    - <u>A&E and triage setting</u>:
        - **Disposal of patients** - hospitalisation in acute or geriatric hospitals
        - **Consider community based care** - e.g. CGAT, CVMO, ECEOL etc.
    - <u>Mx of frail patient in acute and convalescence wards</u>:
        - **General measures** - fall prevention, pressure sore prevention, nutrition support, prevention of delirium
        - **Prescribing** - avoiding polypharmacy
        - **Discahrge** - early DC planning, appropriate placement, and rehabilitation
    - <u>Considers in surgical and invasive intervention</u> - consider poorer surgical outcomes:
        - **Enhanced pre-operative risk Mx** - careful medication review, nutritional augmentation, prehabilitation by PT/OT
        - **Enhanced post-operative Mx** - early mobilisation, attention to hydration, nutrition etc.
    - <u>Considerations of EOL care</u> - consider frailty and worsening frailty status is an independent risk factor for mortality

## Sarcopenia

- **Definition** - a syndrome characterised by progressive and generalised loss of skeletal muscle mass and strength w/ a risk of adverse outcomes such as physical disability, poor quality of lfe, and high mortality
- **Etiology of sarcopenia:**
    - <u>Primary sarcopenia</u> - aging
    - <u>Secondary sarcopenia</u>:
        - Inactivity (bed rest, sedentary lifestyle, deconditioning, zero-gravity)
        - Malnutrition
        - Advanced organ failure
- **Pathophysiology of sarcopenia:**
    - <u>Aging</u>:
        - Progressive loss of muscle mass at 1%/y after 30y, and accelerated at 1.5%/y after 70y
        - Progressive loss of muscle strength at 1-1.5%/y from 50-60y, and increased to 3%/y thereafter
    - <u>Additional pathological changes</u>:
        - Reduced Type II fibre w/ transition to type I fibres - reduced fast twitch fibres results in difficulty in initiating explosive actions and in swallowing
        - Intramuscular and intermusclar fat infiltration
- **Diagnostic criteria for sarcopenia** - Asian working group for sarcopenia defines sarcopenia as:
    - <u>Low-muscle mass</u> - DXA \< 7 kg/m2 in M and \< 5.4 kg/m2 in F; Bioimpedence \< 7 kg/m2 in M and \< 5.7 kg/m2 in F
    - <u>Low muscle strength</u> - handgrip strength \< 28kg for M and \< 18 kg/F
    - <u>Low physical performance</u> - defined by battery of screening tests:
        - 6MWT - \< 1m/s
        - Short Physical Performance Battery score \<= 9
        - Chair Stand test \>= 12s
- **Screening tools for sarcopenia** - simple, easily administered tests adopted to identify patients for further testing (e.g. DXA, Bioimpedence):
    - **SARC-F** - screening tool w/ high Sp and low Sn: 
    ![](~/Documents/notes/img/sarc_f_screen.png)
    - **Grip test** (Take 6 test) - test grip strength 3 times on each hand, and take highest value:
        - <u>Cut-off for M</u> - \>= 28kg
        - <u>Cut-off for F</u> - \>= 18kg
    - **Chair stand test** - stand and sit from chair for 5 times:
        - <u>Normal value</u> - \< 12s
        - <u>Sarcopenia probale</u> - \>= 12s
    - **Calf-circumference:**
        - <u>Cut-off for M</u> - \>= 34cm
        - <u>Cut-off for F</u> - \>= 33cm
- **Approach to screening for sarcopenia** - universal screening is not evidence based:
    - <u>Indications for case-finding</u>:
        - Falls, slowness or weakness
        - Decreased walking mobility and walking speed
        - Unintentional weight loss
        - Decreased ADL
        - Self-reported musce wasting
        - Admission to hospital/ RCHEs
    - <u>Simple algorithm for diagnosis in clinical practice</u>: 
    ![](~/Documents/notes/img/algorithm_for_diagnosis_of_sarcopenia.png)
- **Comorbidities associated w/ sarcopenia** - significant overlap w/ frailty:
    - <u>Sarcopenic obesity</u> - copresence of sarcopenia and obesity w/ excessive mass in the presence of reduced muscle mass
    - <u>Sarcopenic dysphagia</u> - impaired swallowing of the elderly because of aging (i.e. presbyphagia), due to progressive loss of type II fibres that initiate swallowing
    - <u>Osteosarcopenia</u> - co-existence of osteoporosis and sarcopenia, w/ higher chance of falls and fracture than either conditions alone

  
  ![](~/Documents/notes/img/overlap_of_sarcopenia_and_frailty.png)
- **Mx of sarcopenia:**
    - <u>Resistance exercise</u> - most evidence in improving muscle strength, muscle mass and balance
    - <u>Protein</u> - ESCEO recommends intake of 1-1.2g/kg BW of protein per day
    - <u>Vitamin D and calcium</u> - 800 IU/d of vitamin D, and 1000 mg/d of Ca to prevent osteosarcopenia
    - <u>Selective androgen receptor modulators</u> - SARMs are in clinical development w/ hope of agonistic activity against skeletal muscles resulting in gain in muscle mass and strength\*\* Management of Frailty and Sarcopenia
